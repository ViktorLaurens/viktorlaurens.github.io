This chapter is the **forward problem** of the report: given the equation of motion, its
parameters and an initial state, what motion follows? Every later chapter turns this around
and starts from measurements instead; the [storyline](/projects/pendulum-lab/#the-storyline-one-problem-four-questions)
shows how they fit together.

All figures come from `scripts/make_figures.py`, and all
animations from `animations/simple_pendulum_scenes.py`.
Both use the same model in
`src/pendulum_lab/simple_pendulum.py`.
All simulations use $g = 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$ and $L = 1\ \mathrm{m}$; each caption lists the
initial conditions $(\theta_0, \dot\theta_0)$ and the damping $\mu$.

## 1.1 Equation of motion

A point mass hangs from a massless rod of length $L$. Measure the angle $\theta$ from the
downward vertical, positive to the right. The mass moves along a circle, so its arc
position is $s = L\theta$ and its tangential acceleration is $\ddot s = L\ddot\theta$.

Gravity $g$ points straight down. Only its component along the direction of motion
accelerates the mass, and that component is $-g\sin\theta$. The minus sign means it always
pulls back toward the bottom. So the equation becomes:

$$
L\ddot\theta = -g\sin\theta
$$

Air resistance and friction at the pivot are modelled as a torque proportional to the
angular velocity, with damping coefficient $\mu$:

$$
\boxed{\mskip5mu \ddot\theta = -\frac{g}{L}\sin\theta - \mu\thinspace \dot\theta\mskip5mu }
$$

Two sanity checks:
- At $\theta = 0$ the restoring term vanishes, so there is no acceleration at the bottom.
- At $\theta = 90^\circ$ it equals $-\tfrac{g}{L}$, which is free fall along the arc.

The mass $m$ has dropped out. From motion data alone, only $\omega_0^2 = \tfrac{g}{L}$ and $\mu$ can
be identified, not $m$, and not $g$ and $L$ separately. Chapter 2 relies on this.

## 1.2 There is no closed-form solution

The $\sin\theta$ term makes the equation nonlinear. Without damping, an exact solution
exists, but it is written in terms of Jacobi elliptic functions. With damping, no
closed form is known at all. But we can solve it numerically and investigate it geometrically.

Numerically, the simplest approach is Euler's method: take small steps
$\theta \mathrel{+}= \dot\theta\thinspace \Delta t$ and $\dot\theta \mathrel{+}= \ddot\theta\thinspace \Delta t$.
This works, but it needs a tiny $\Delta t$ and slowly gains or loses energy. We use SciPy's
adaptive 8th-order Runge–Kutta method (`DOP853`) with tolerance $10^{-10}$ instead. A test
checks that the undamped energy stays constant to within $10^{-8}$ relative error.

## 1.3 The small-angle approximation, and where it breaks

For small angles, $\sin\theta \approx \theta$ and the equation becomes **linear**:

$$
\ddot\theta + \mu\thinspace \dot\theta + \omega_0^2\thinspace \theta = 0, \qquad \omega_0 = \sqrt{\tfrac{g}{L}}.
$$

Trying $\theta = e^{\lambda t}$ gives $\lambda^2 + \mu\lambda + \omega_0^2 = 0$, so
$\lambda = -\tfrac{\mu}{2} \pm i\thinspace \omega_d$ with the damped frequency
$\omega_d = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \tfrac{\mu^2}{4}}$. For the underdamped case
$\mu \lt 2\omega_0$, the solution with initial state $(\theta_0, \dot\theta_0)$ is a sine and
cosine under an exponential envelope:

$$
\theta(t) = e^{-\frac{\mu}{2} t}\left[\theta_0\cos\omega_d t + \frac{\dot\theta_0 + \tfrac{\mu}{2}\theta_0}{\omega_d}\thinspace \sin\omega_d t\right].
$$

This is the "harmonic motion" from introductory physics, with period
$T_0 = 2\pi\sqrt{\tfrac{L}{g}}$, independent of amplitude. Comparing it with the numerical
solution of the full equation shows how far it can be trusted:

<Figure src="/media/pendulum-lab/small_angle_comparison.png" width={100} alt="Nonlinear vs. small-angle solution for four release angles">

$\theta_0 = 10^\circ,\ 60^\circ,\ 120^\circ,\ 170^\circ;\ \ \dot\theta_0 = 0\ \text{rad/s};\ \ \mu = 0.15\ \text{s}^{-1}$

</Figure>

- At $\theta_0 = 10^\circ$ the curves are indistinguishable.
- At $\theta_0 = 60^\circ$ the real pendulum already lags behind.
- At $\theta_0 = 120^\circ$ and $170^\circ$ the shape changes too. The real pendulum lingers near the top,
  where $\sin\theta$ is much smaller than $\theta$, so its curve is flattened and stretched,
  and it is no longer a decaying sinusoid.

The lag is a **longer period**. For the undamped pendulum the exact period is

$$
T = 4\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}}\mskip5mu K\negthinspace \left(\sin^2\frac{\theta_0}{2}\right)
\mskip5mu \approx\mskip5mu  T_0\left(1 + \frac{\theta_0^2}{16} + \dots\right),
$$

where $K$ is the complete elliptic integral of the first kind. A test checks this
formula against the simulation at $\theta_0 = 150^\circ$.

<Figure src="/media/pendulum-lab/period_vs_amplitude.png" width={80} alt="Period as a function of amplitude">

Released from rest: $\theta_0 = 0^\circ \ldots 179^\circ,\ \dot\theta_0 = 0\ \text{rad/s}$; undamped, $\mu = 0$

</Figure>

The period is 7% longer at $60^\circ$, 37% longer at $120^\circ$, and grows without bound as
$\theta_0 \to 180^\circ$, where the pendulum balances upright.

## 1.4 State space

Plotting $\theta$ against $t$ hides the structure. The key idea is that the pendulum's
**state** is fully described by two numbers, the angle and the angular velocity:

$$
\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix}\theta \cr \dot\theta\end{bmatrix}, \qquad
\dot{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}) = \begin{bmatrix}\dot\theta \cr -\tfrac{g}{L}\sin\theta - \mu\thinspace \dot\theta\end{bmatrix}.
$$

One second-order equation has become a system of two first-order equations. Each point
of the $(\theta, \dot\theta)$ plane is one possible state, and the pendulum's history is a
curve through that plane.

<Figure video="/media/pendulum-lab/phase_space_trajectory.mp4" width={85} alt="Pendulum and its state tracing an inward spiral">

$\theta_0 = 60^\circ,\ \dot\theta_0 = 0\ \text{rad/s},\ \mu = 0.3\ \text{s}^{-1}$

</Figure>

Damping removes energy on every swing, so the state **spirals inward** to the origin,
where the pendulum hangs still. To make that precise, define the energy per unit
$mL^2$:

$$
E = \tfrac12\dot\theta^2 + \omega_0^2(1 - \cos\theta), \qquad
\dot E = -\mu\thinspace \dot\theta^2 \le 0 .
$$

## 1.5 The vector field and its fixed points

$\mathbf{f}$ assigns a velocity vector to every point of state space. Drawing those
vectors gives a **vector field**, and solving the equation means following it: at every
instant, the state moves with the arrow it is sitting on.

<Figure video="/media/pendulum-lab/vector_field_fixed_points.mp4" width={100} alt="Vector field on the unwrapped state space with fixed points and two trajectories">

$\mu = 0.25\ \text{s}^{-1}$; trajectories start at $(\theta_0, \dot\theta_0) = ({-2.4}\ \text{rad},\ 3.5\ \text{rad/s})$ and $(\pi + 0.03\ \text{rad},\ 0\ \text{rad/s})$; played at 1.5× speed · arrows are shortened to avoid clutter, color shows speed

</Figure>

**Fixed points** are where the field vanishes, $\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}^{\ast}) = \mathbf{0}$:

$$
\dot\theta^{\ast} = 0, \quad \sin\theta^{\ast} = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\mathbf{x}^{\ast}_k = (k\pi,\thinspace  0), \qquad k \in \mathbb{Z}.
$$

Because $\theta$ is an angle, $\theta$ and $\theta + 2\pi$ describe the same physical
position. The state space is really a **cylinder**, and the unwrapped plane repeats it
every $2\pi$. So there are only two physically different equilibria: hanging down
(even $k$) and balanced upright (odd $k$).

**Stability** follows from linearizing around each fixed point. The Jacobian is

$$
J(\mathbf{x}^{\ast}) = \frac{\partial \mathbf{f}}{\partial \mathbf{x}}\bigg|_{\mathbf{x}^{\ast}}
= \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \cr -\omega_0^2\cos\theta^{\ast} & -\mu \end{bmatrix}.
$$

| Fixed point | $\cos\theta^{\ast}$ | Eigenvalues | Type |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hanging, $\theta^{\ast} = 2k\pi$ | $+1$ | $-\tfrac{\mu}{2} \pm i\thinspace \omega_d$ | **stable spiral**: both have negative real part |
| Upright, $\theta^{\ast} = (2k+1)\pi$ | $-1$ | $-\tfrac{\mu}{2} \pm \sqrt{\tfrac{\mu^2}{4} + \omega_0^2}$ | **saddle**: one positive eigenvalue, so unstable |

The animation shows both cases. One state spirals into the bottom. The other starts a
hair away from upright and immediately falls off the saddle into the neighboring well.
The upright saddle comes back in later chapters: it is exactly the state that LQR, MPC
and reinforcement learning will have to stabilize on the Furuta pendulum.

### Changing the damping

Changing $\mu$ reshapes the whole field at once. With $\mu = 0$, every orbit around the
origin is closed (energy is conserved). As $\mu$ grows, the orbits open into spirals that
reach the bottom faster.

## 1.6 Phase flow

Instead of following one initial condition, we can let **every** point of state space
move at once. The result looks like a fluid, or wind on a weather map, and is called the
**phase flow**:

<Figure video="/media/pendulum-lab/phase_flow.mp4" width={100} alt="Streamlines flowing along the pendulum vector field">

$\mu = 0.25\ \text{s}^{-1}$; every point of the plane is an initial condition $(\theta_0, \dot\theta_0)$

</Figure>

The same structure, as a static portrait:

<Figure src="/media/pendulum-lab/phase_portrait.png" width={100} alt="Phase portrait of the damped pendulum">

$\mu = 0.25\ \text{s}^{-1}$; every streamline is the trajectory from one initial condition $(\theta_0, \dot\theta_0)$

</Figure>

The flow contracts around every hanging point (stable) and is split by every upright
point (saddle). Above and below the "eyes", the flow runs steadily right or left.
Those states have so much energy that the pendulum **rotates** instead of swinging.

## 1.7 Swinging it hard

Give the pendulum a strong push from the bottom: $\theta_0 = 0$, $\dot\theta_0 = 10$ rad/s.
Its energy $E = 50\ \mathrm{s^{-2}}$ is well above the energy needed to reach the top,
$2\omega_0^2 \approx 19.6\ \mathrm{s^{-2}}$. So it makes full rotations, and $\theta$ keeps
increasing. Damping removes some energy on every turn. Once $E$ drops below $2\omega_0^2$,
the state can no longer pass the next saddle. It is captured by a well and spirals in.

<Figure video="/media/pendulum-lab/high_energy_swing.mp4" width={100} alt="Pendulum rotating several times before settling, with its unwrapped state-space trajectory">

$\theta_0 = 0,\ \dot\theta_0 = 10\ \text{rad/s},\ \mu = 0.25\ \text{s}^{-1}$; played at 1.5× speed

</Figure>

The pendulum makes three full turns and comes to rest at $\theta = 6\pi$. Physically,
that is the same hanging position as $\theta = 0$, but in the unwrapped picture it is
three wells further along.

## 1.8 Takeaways

- The $\sin\theta$ in the equation is exactly why a real pendulum is **not** a sine wave:
  its period grows with amplitude, and large swings are visibly non-sinusoidal.
- The state $(\theta, \dot\theta)$ and the vector field $\mathbf{f}$ show *all*
  possible motions at once, without solving anything.
- The eigenvalues of the Jacobian at each fixed point determine its stability. The pendulum hanging straight down is a stable fixed point (a spiral that attracts all nearby states). The pendulum balanced upright is an unstable saddle point: the slightest disturbance sends the state away from it.

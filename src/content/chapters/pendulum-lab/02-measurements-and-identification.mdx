Chapter 1 assumed we know the equation of motion *and* its parameters. A real pendulum
only gives us measurements. This chapter asks: **from a noisy recording of the angle,
how well can we recover the parameters of the model?**

This is the first inverse problem of the report, and the one with the fewest unknowns (see
the [storyline](/projects/pendulum-lab/#the-storyline-one-problem-four-questions)). The structure of the
model comes from physics, or in section 2.3 from a library of physics-like terms; only its
constant parameters are unknown; and the whole recording may be used, after the experiment.
The model is also taken to be exact: the pendulum follows its equation, and only the
measurements are noisy. Chapter 3 keeps the question but learns the structure as well.
Chapter 5 drops the other two assumptions: it estimates the changing state while the
pendulum swings, with a model that is allowed to be imperfect.

To answer that honestly we simulate the measurements. Then the true parameters are known,
and every method can be scored against them.

The code is in `src/pendulum_lab/sensor.py`, the figures
come from `scripts/make_figures.py`, and the animation from
`animations/measurement_scenes.py`.

## 2.1 The sensor model

### What is measured

We assume an angle sensor on the pivot, for example a 12-bit magnetic encoder, read by a
microcontroller at a fixed sample rate $f_s$. It only measures the angle; the angular
velocity $\dot\theta$ is **not** measured. Sample $k$ is taken at $t_k = \tfrac{k}{f_s}$, and the
sensor reports

$$
y_k = Q\big(\theta(t_k) + b + v_k\big), \qquad k = 0, 1, \dots, N-1
$$

Three effects turn the true angle $\theta(t_k)$ into the reading $y_k$:

**Offset $b$.** The sensor's zero is never mounted exactly at the bottom. The offset is
constant, so it shifts every sample by the same amount. Unlike noise, it does not average
out; it has to be estimated. It is identifiable because the physics fixes the hanging
equilibrium at $\theta = 0$: wherever the measurements settle, that level is $b$.

**Noise $v_k$, with outliers.** Most samples have small Gaussian noise with standard
deviation $\sigma$ (electrical and magnetic noise). Occasionally, with probability
$\varepsilon$, a sample is an **outlier**: a glitch such as a corrupted message, with a
much larger spread $\sigma_\text{out}$. This is a *contamination model*, a mixture of two
Gaussians:

$$
v_k \sim (1-\varepsilon)\thinspace\mathcal N(0, \sigma^2) + \varepsilon\thinspace\mathcal N(0, \sigma_\text{out}^2)
$$

Equivalently, a hidden coin flip $z_k \sim \text{Bernoulli}(\varepsilon)$ decides per sample
which of the two Gaussians the noise comes from. In the simulation we keep $z_k$ as ground
truth, so we can later check whether a method found the outliers; the methods never see it.

**Quantization $Q$.** The encoder reports whole counts, so the reading is rounded to a
multiple of its resolution $\Delta$:

$$
Q(x) = \Delta \cdot \mathrm{round}\left(\frac{x}{\Delta}\right), \qquad \Delta = \frac{2\pi}{4096} \approx 0.088^\circ
$$

Because the noise is much larger than one count, the rounding error behaves like extra
uniform noise on $\left[{-\tfrac{\Delta}{2}},\ \tfrac{\Delta}{2}\right]$ with standard deviation
$\tfrac{\Delta}{\sqrt{12}} \approx 0.025^\circ$, negligible next to $\sigma$.

The default values:

| Quantity | Symbol | Value |
|---|---|---|
| Sample rate | $f_s$ | $100\ \text{Hz}$ |
| Gaussian noise | $\sigma$ | $0.5^\circ$ |
| Outlier probability | $\varepsilon$ | 1% |
| Outlier spread | $\sigma_\text{out}$ | $15^\circ$ |
| Mounting offset | $b$ | $1.5^\circ$ |
| Resolution (12 bit) | $\Delta$ | $0.088^\circ$ |

### The reference experiment

All identification methods in this chapter are tested on the same recording: the pendulum
of chapter 1 released at rest from a large angle, so that the $\sin\theta$ nonlinearity
matters, and recorded long enough for the damping to show.

| | Value |
|---|---|
| True parameters | $\omega_0^2 = \tfrac{g}{L} = 9.81\ \text{s}^{-2}$, $\mu = 0.2\ \text{s}^{-1}$ |
| Initial state | $\theta_0 = 120^\circ$, $\dot\theta_0 = 0\ \text{rad/s}$ |
| Recording | 20 s at $100\ \text{Hz}$: 2000 samples, of which 28 are outliers (seed 0) |

**The recording is treated as real data.** It was generated once and frozen in
`data/reference/`, and every method loads exactly those numbers, so
results can be compared honestly. The folder separates what an experimenter would have
from what only the simulation knows:

| File | Contents | Used for |

| `metadata.json` | sample rate and encoder resolution | identification |
| `truth/ground_truth.csv` | true $\theta$, $\dot\theta$ and outlier flags $z_k$ | scoring only |

### Why outliers matter so much

The variance of the noise is

$$
\mathrm{Var}(v_k) = (1-\varepsilon)\thinspace\sigma^2 + \varepsilon\thinspace\sigma_\text{out}^2
= 0.99 \cdot (0.5^\circ)^2 + 0.01 \cdot (15^\circ)^2 \approx 0.25 + 2.25 = 2.50\ \mathrm{deg}^2 ,
$$

a standard deviation of $1.58^\circ$. **1% of the samples cause 90% of the variance.**

The distribution makes the problem visible. On a logarithmic scale, the sensor's error is
a sharp peak with wide, heavy tails. A Gaussian with the same variance (dashed) gets both
wrong: it is far too wide in the middle and far too thin in the tails.

<Figure src="/media/pendulum-lab/sensor_noise_distribution.png" width={85} alt="Distribution of the measurement error: sharp Gaussian peak with heavy outlier tails, compared with a Gaussian of equal variance">

Error distribution of the sensor, from one million simulated samples of a still pendulum

</Figure>

This matters for identification. Least squares minimizes $\sum_k (y_k - \hat y_k)^2$, which
is the right thing to do *only* for Gaussian noise (it is then the maximum-likelihood
estimate). With squared errors, a single $30^\circ$ outlier weighs as much as
$\left(\tfrac{30}{0.5}\right)^2 = 3600$ ordinary samples. Section 2.3 will show how much that biases the
estimate, and how a robust loss fixes it.

### What the model leaves out

Real recordings can also have missing samples, timing jitter, an angle that wraps around
at $\pm 180^\circ$, and dynamics that differ from the model (for example dry friction at the
pivot). These are easy to add later; for now the data stays evenly sampled, which keeps
the methods in the next sections simple.

### The identification problem

Everything that is unknown about the experiment is collected in one vector:

$$
p = \big(\omega_0^2,\ \mu,\ \theta_0,\ \dot\theta_0,\ b\big)
$$

The task for the rest of this chapter: **given only $y_0, \dots, y_{N-1}$, estimate $p$**,
together with an honest statement of how uncertain that estimate is.

## 2.2 Regression on the equation of motion

The code is in `src/pendulum_lab/derivatives.py` and
`src/pendulum_lab/regression.py`; every number in this
section comes from `scripts/evaluate_regression.py`.

### The idea: the model is linear in its parameters

The equation of motion $\ddot\theta = -\omega_0^2 \sin\theta - \mu\dot\theta$ is nonlinear in
$\theta$, but **linear in the parameters**. Written as a row times a column, it reads

$$
\ddot\theta =
\underset{\text{computed from data}}{\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} {-\sin\theta} & {-\dot\theta} \end{bmatrix}}}
\underset{\text{unknown}}{\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} \omega_0^2 \cr \mu \end{bmatrix}}}
$$

Everything nonlinear, like the $\sin\theta$, lives in the row, and only involves the
signal, which we can evaluate at every sample. The parameters sit in the column and only
ever multiply those known functions. So each sample gives one *linear* equation in the
unknowns, and many samples give an overdetermined linear system: exactly what ordinary
least squares solves.

The sensor gives $y = \theta + b$ rather than $\theta$, and the offset $b$ sits inside the
sine. The angle-addition formula pulls it out exactly:

$$
\sin\theta = \sin(y - b) = \sin y \cos b - \cos y \sin b
$$

Because $b$ is constant, $\ddot y = \ddot\theta$ and $\dot y = \dot\theta$, so

$$
\ddot y = -c_1 \sin y + c_2 \cos y - \mu \dot y,
\qquad c_1 = \omega_0^2 \cos b, \quad c_2 = \omega_0^2 \sin b ,
$$

or, in the same row-times-column form, with one more known function in the row:

$$
\ddot y =
\begin{bmatrix} {-\sin y} & \cos y & {-\dot y} \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} c_1 \cr c_2 \cr \mu \end{bmatrix}
$$

The offset has disappeared into the coefficients, with no approximation. Writing this
equation once for every sample $k$ and stacking the rows gives the regression problem
$\mathbf d = \Phi\boldsymbol\beta$:

$$
\underset{\mathbf d}{\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} \ddot y_0 \cr \ddot y_1 \cr \vdots \cr \ddot y_{N-1} \end{bmatrix}}} =
\underset{\Phi}{\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}
{-\sin y_0} & \cos y_0 & {-\dot y_0} \cr
{-\sin y_1} & \cos y_1 & {-\dot y_1} \cr
\vdots & \vdots & \vdots \cr
{-\sin y_{N-1}} & \cos y_{N-1} & {-\dot y_{N-1}}
\end{bmatrix}}}
\underset{\boldsymbol\beta}{\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} c_1 \cr c_2 \cr \mu \end{bmatrix}}}
$$

With 2000 samples this is 2000 equations for 3 unknowns. The least-squares solution and
the parameters follow directly:

$$
\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = \left(\Phi^\top \Phi\right)^{-1} \Phi^\top \mathbf d,
\qquad \hat\omega_0^2 = \sqrt{c_1^2 + c_2^2}, \qquad \hat b = \mathrm{atan2}(c_2, c_1)
$$

One linear solve, no iterations, no initial guess, and the initial state
$(\theta_0, \dot\theta_0)$ is not even needed. **The catch:** the regression needs
$\dot y$ and $\ddot y$, and we only measure $y$. Everything below is about estimating
derivatives from noisy samples.

### Attempt 1: finite differences

The textbook approximations, with sample spacing $h = 0.01\ \text{s}$:

$$
\dot y_k \approx \frac{y_{k+1} - y_{k-1}}{2h},
\qquad
\ddot y_k \approx \frac{y_{k+1} - 2y_k + y_{k-1}}{h^2}
$$

Dividing by $h^2$ is the problem. If each sample carries independent noise with standard
deviation $\sigma$, the second difference carries noise with standard deviation

$$
\sigma_{\ddot y} = \frac{\sqrt{1 + 4 + 1}\thinspace\sigma}{h^2} = \frac{\sqrt 6\thinspace\sigma}{h^2} \approx 214\ \text{rad/s}^2
$$

for $\sigma = 0.5^\circ$, against a signal of about $10\ \text{rad/s}^2$. With the outliers
included it is $936\ \text{rad/s}^2$. The estimate is useless: $\omega_0^2$ comes out
675% too large. The noise also enters $\Phi$ through $\dot y$, and noisy regressors bias
least squares rather than merely making it noisy (the *errors-in-variables* problem).

### Attempt 2: Savitzky–Golay differentiation

Instead of differencing neighbours, fit a low-order polynomial to a short window of samples
by least squares, and take the derivatives of that polynomial at the window's center. We
use a cubic over $n = 25$ samples ($0.25\ \text{s}$), and slide the window along the signal:

<Figure video="/media/pendulum-lab/savitzky_golay_window.mp4" width={100} alt="A cubic fitted in a sliding window over the measurements; its slope and curvature at the center trace out the velocity and acceleration estimates">

Each position of the window gives one estimate of $\dot\theta$ and $\ddot\theta$

</Figure>

Because the fit is linear in the data, every derivative estimate is a fixed weighted sum
of the samples in the window, $\ddot y_k = \sum_j c_j\thinspace y_{k+j}$. The noise therefore
becomes $\sigma \lVert \mathbf c \rVert = 0.75\ \text{rad/s}^2$, about **285 times less** than
finite differences:

<Figure src="/media/pendulum-lab/regression_derivatives.png" width={100} alt="Angle, velocity and acceleration: finite differences are dominated by noise, Savitzky-Golay follows the truth">

Reference data, $2\ \text{s} \le t \le 5\ \text{s}$; offset $b$ removed in the top panel for comparison

</Figure>

**Choosing the window.** A longer window averages more noise away but also flattens the
curve it fits. We fix it *before* looking at any result: about a tenth of the swing period
(which is $2$ to $2.7\ \text{s}$ here). Tuning it by comparing with the true parameters would
be cheating in a benchmark.

### Outliers become waves

Savitzky–Golay is linear, so an outlier of size $e$ adds $e\thinspace c_j$ to every estimate
whose window contains it: it is smeared into a wave the width of the window.

<Figure src="/media/pendulum-lab/regression_outlier_effect.png" width={100} alt="Second derivative near three outliers: Savitzky-Golay on raw data shows large waves, rejecting outliers first removes them">

Reference data, $5.5\ \text{s} \le t \le 7\ \text{s}$; red lines mark the outliers

</Figure>

### Attempt 3: reject the outliers first

Outliers are rare and large, so they stand out from a smooth curve. Each pass of the
rejection:

1. smooths the current signal (Savitzky–Golay, same window) and computes the residuals
   $r_k = y_k - \hat y_k$;
2. estimates the noise level robustly with the median absolute deviation,
   $s = 1.4826 \cdot \mathrm{median}_k \left| r_k - \mathrm{median}(r) \right|$, which the
   outliers barely affect (the factor makes $s = \sigma$ for Gaussian noise);
3. flags every sample with $|r_k| \gt 4s$ and replaces it by interpolating its unflagged
   neighbours, so the next pass's smooth curve is no longer pulled toward it.

After three passes it flags 27 samples on the reference data, **all 27 real outliers, with
no false alarms**. The one it misses has an error of $1.74^\circ$, which is indistinguishable
from ordinary noise and does no harm.

### Results on the reference data

| Method | $\hat\omega_0^2$ | error | $\hat\mu$ | error | $\hat b$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Finite differences | 76.00 | +675% | 0.483 | +142% | $5.22^\circ$ |
| Savitzky–Golay | 9.780 | $-0.3$% | 0.195 | $-2.3$% | $1.53^\circ$ |
| Outlier rejection + Savitzky–Golay | 9.721 | $-0.9$% | 0.197 | $-1.3$% | $1.50^\circ$ |
| truth | 9.810 | | 0.200 | | $1.50^\circ$ |

On this one dataset, rejecting outliers looks like a mixed result: better $\mu$ and $b$,
slightly worse $\omega_0^2$. One dataset cannot tell which differences are real.

### Bias and variance: repeating the experiment

To separate systematic error from luck, we repeat the whole method on 200 *new* datasets
from the same experiment and sensor (different noise, same physics). The frozen dataset
remains the benchmark; this studies the method itself. At the chosen window:

| Method | error in $\hat\omega_0^2$ | error in $\hat\mu$ |
|---|---|---|
| Savitzky–Golay | $-0.60 \pm 0.21$% | $-1.73 \pm 0.88$% |
| Outlier rejection + Savitzky–Golay | $-0.93 \pm 0.05$% | $-1.19 \pm 0.25$% |

(mean $\pm$ standard deviation). Rejecting outliers makes the estimate **about four times
more repeatable**. What remains is a **systematic bias**: smoothing flattens the oscillation,
so the curvature, and with it $\omega_0^2$, comes out slightly too small. Sweeping the window
length shows the trade-off:

<Figure src="/media/pendulum-lab/regression_window_sweep.png" width={100} alt="Parameter error versus window length: bias grows with the window, outlier rejection removes most of the spread">

Mean (line) and standard deviation (band) over 100 new datasets; dashed: the chosen $0.25\ \text{s}$

</Figure>

The bias grows with the window. Short windows are only usable *after* rejecting outliers,
and then they are nearly unbiased. But the best window can only be read off this plot
because we know the truth; with real data there is no such plot.

### What we learned

- Equation-error regression is fast and needs no initial guess, which makes it an excellent
  **first estimate** for the slower methods that follow.
- Its weak point is differentiation: **the accuracy is set by how well we can estimate
  $\dot y$ and $\ddot y$, not by the regression itself.**
- Outliers must be removed *before* differentiating; afterwards about 1% of smoothing
  bias remains, and no window choice removes it without knowing the answer.

Both observations point to the next methods: SINDy keeps the regression but asks *which*
terms belong in the equation, and output-error least squares avoids differentiation
altogether by simulating the model and comparing it with $y$ directly.

## 2.3 SINDy: discovering the equation from data

So far we assumed the *form* of the equation and only estimated its numbers. What if we
did not know that the restoring term is a sine, or what kind of friction acts on the
pendulum? **SINDy** (Sparse Identification of Nonlinear Dynamics; Brunton, Proctor and
Kutz, 2016) keeps the regression of section 2.2 but lets the data choose the terms.

The code is in `src/pendulum_lab/sindy.py`; the numbers come
from `scripts/evaluate_sindy.py`.

### A library of candidate terms

Instead of one row $\big[{-\sin y},\ \cos y,\ {-\dot y}\big]$ we offer a whole library of
plausible terms, and give every true term some serious competition:

$$
\Theta(y, \dot y) = \begin{bmatrix}
1 & y & y^2 & y^3 & \sin y & \cos y & \dot y & \dot y^2 & \dot y^3 & \dot y|\dot y| & \mathrm{sign}\thinspace\dot y & y\dot y
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

- $\sin y$ competes with the polynomials $y$, $y^2$, $y^3$. A Taylor series says
  $\sin y \approx y - \tfrac{1}{6}y^3$, so this is a real contest.
- Viscous damping $\dot y$ competes with quadratic air drag $\dot y|\dot y|$ and dry
  (Coulomb) friction $\mathrm{sign}\thinspace\dot y$, the three standard friction models.
- The constant $1$ and $\cos y$ are where a sensor offset shows up (section 2.2).

The model is $\ddot y = \Theta(y, \dot y)\thinspace\boldsymbol\xi$ with twelve coefficients, most
of which should be zero. In this basis the truth is
$\xi_{\sin y} = -\omega_0^2 \cos b = -9.807$, $\xi_{\cos y} = \omega_0^2 \sin b = 0.257$ and
$\xi_{\dot y} = -\mu = -0.2$, with all other coefficients zero.

<Figure src="/media/pendulum-lab/sindy_library.png" width={100} alt="The twelve candidate functions over time on the reference data, with sin y and the angular velocity highlighted">

The candidate terms on the reference data; highlighted: the terms SINDy selects

</Figure>

### Sparse regression

Ordinary least squares would use all twelve terms. **Sequentially thresholded least
squares** (STLSQ) forces most of them to zero:

1. scale every column of $\Theta$ to unit RMS, so that a coefficient measures how much its
   term *contributes*, independent of units;
2. fit all coefficients by least squares;
3. set every coefficient smaller than a threshold $\lambda$ to zero;
4. refit the surviving terms, and repeat steps 3 and 4 until nothing changes.

As $\lambda$ rises, weak terms drop out one by one, and the remaining ones are refitted to
take over their work:

<Figure video="/media/pendulum-lab/sindy_threshold.mp4" width={100} alt="A threshold rising through the candidate terms; terms below it are pruned and the equation simplifies">

Reference data, derivatives as in section 2.2

</Figure>

### Choosing the threshold without knowing the answer

Every $\lambda$ gives a different equation, so which one should we believe? We fit on the
first 10 s, and test each candidate equation on the last 10 s by **simulating it** from
the smoothed state at $t = 10\ \text{s}$ and comparing the trajectory with the measurements.

Why simulate, rather than compare $\ddot y$ directly? Because the estimated $\ddot y$ is
noisy, and that noise is the same for every model: all equations fit it about equally
badly. A wrong term, however, changes how the motion *evolves*, and integrating over many
swings turns a small error in the equation into a visible drift in the trajectory.

<Figure src="/media/pendulum-lab/sindy_threshold_sweep.png" width={90} alt="Number of terms and validation error against the threshold, with a clear minimum at two terms">

Reference data; the dot marks the selected threshold

</Figure>

| Model size | Best validation error | Terms |
|---|---|---|
| 10 terms | $2.84^\circ$ | nearly every candidate |
| 3 terms | $3.50^\circ$ | $\sin y$, $\dot y^3$, $\dot y\lvert\dot y\rvert$ |
| **2 terms** | $\mathbf{2.13^\circ}$ | $\sin y$, $\dot y$ |
| 1 term | $12.10^\circ$ | $\sin y$ |

<Figure src="/media/pendulum-lab/sindy_validation.png" width={100} alt="Three discovered equations simulated over the held-out ten seconds: without damping the oscillation never decays">

The held-out half of the reference recording, with three discovered equations simulated from $t = 10\ \text{s}$

</Figure>

Without a damping term the simulated swing never decays. With ten terms the fit on the
training data is excellent, but the extra terms partly cancel each other and the
trajectory drifts. The two-term model wins. Refitting its two terms on all the data gives

$$
\ddot y = -9.706\sin y - 0.2004\thinspace\dot y ,
$$

so $\hat\omega_0^2 = 9.706$ ($-1.1$% error) and $\hat\mu = 0.2004$ ($+0.2$%). **SINDy picked
the sine over the polynomials and viscous damping over air drag and dry friction**, from
noisy, outlier-ridden data, without being told the equation.

It did miss one thing: the offset. Its $\cos y$ term contributes too little to survive the
threshold, so the discovered model swings about $y = 0$ rather than about $b = 1.5^\circ$.
That is visible in the validation plot, and it is why section 2.4 will estimate $b$
explicitly.

### Small swings: a good fit is not a correct model

Chapter 1 showed that for small angles $\sin\theta \approx \theta$. What does that mean for
discovery? We froze a second recording, `data/small_swing/`: the same
pendulum and sensor, released from only $20^\circ$. SINDy now returns

$$
\ddot y = 576.9 - 1872\thinspace y - 286.2\thinspace y^2 + 306.6\thinspace y^3 + 1862\sin y - 576.7\cos y - 0.209\thinspace\dot y^3 - 0.046\thinspace\mathrm{sign}\thinspace\dot y .
$$

Huge coefficients that nearly cancel, no viscous damping at all, and no threshold gives
fewer than six terms. Yet its validation error, $1.64^\circ$, is *better* than that of the
correct model on the large swing.

The reason is that on $|y| \le 20^\circ$ the candidate columns are almost linearly
dependent: $\sin y$ agrees with $y - \tfrac{1}{6}y^3$ to within 0.013%, and
$\cos y$ with $1 - \tfrac{1}{2}y^2$ to within 0.07%. Countless combinations of them describe
the data equally well, and noise decides which one comes out. The **condition number** of
the scaled library measures exactly this:

| Recording | Condition number | Discovered structure |
|---|---|---|
| Large swing ($120^\circ$) | 323 | $\sin y$, $\dot y$: correct |
| Small swing ($20^\circ$) | 449 554 | eight terms, physically meaningless |

<Figure src="/media/pendulum-lab/sindy_identifiability.png" width={100} alt="Coefficients discovered from the large and the small swing next to the true coefficients, on a symmetric log scale">

Coefficients found on each recording (symmetric log scale), with the true values

</Figure>

**Whether the structure of a model can be identified is decided when the experiment is
designed, not when the data is analysed.** The data must excite the behaviour that tells
the candidates apart, here a large swing. A low validation error alone proves nothing;
the condition number is a cheap check that should always come with it.

### What we learned

- SINDy turns system identification into *model discovery*: from data alone it found
  $\sin y$ and viscous damping among twelve candidates.
- The sparsity threshold should be chosen by simulating the candidate equations on
  held-out data, not by how well they fit the derivatives.
- Terms that contribute little, like the offset here, fall below the threshold and are
  lost.
- With data that does not excite the nonlinearity, discovery fails *silently*: the model
  fits well and means nothing. Check the conditioning of the library.

## 2.4 Output-error least squares: fitting the simulation

The code is in `src/pendulum_lab/output_error.py`; the
numbers come from `scripts/evaluate_output_error.py`.

### Fit the simulation, not the derivatives

Sections 2.2 and 2.3 differentiated the data to match the model, and every remaining error
came from that differentiation. The classical alternative works the other way around:
**integrate the model and compare it with the data.** For a candidate parameter vector

$$
p = \big(\omega_0^2,\ \mu,\ \theta_0,\ \dot\theta_0,\ b\big)
$$

we simulate the pendulum from $(\theta_0, \dot\theta_0)$, add the offset, and get the
predicted measurements $\hat y_k(p) = \theta(t_k; p) + b$. The estimate is the $p$ that makes
the prediction closest to what the sensor reported:

$$
\hat p = \arg\min_p \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} \rho\big(y_k - \hat y_k(p)\big).
$$

No derivatives of the data appear anywhere, so the smoothing bias of section 2.2
disappears. The price: every evaluation of the cost integrates the ODE, the problem is
nonlinear in $p$, and the optimizer needs a starting point. We start from the result of
section 2.2, which is already within $1$% of the answer and costs one linear solve.

### How the optimizer finds $\hat p$

Near the current guess, the prediction is approximately linear in a parameter change
$\boldsymbol\delta$:

$$
\hat{\mathbf y}(p + \boldsymbol\delta) \approx \hat{\mathbf y}(p) + J\boldsymbol\delta,
\qquad J_{kj} = \frac{\partial \hat y_k}{\partial p_j} .
$$

Each column of the Jacobian $J$ says how the whole simulated trajectory moves when one
parameter changes; we compute it by finite differences, one extra simulation per
parameter. Solving the linearized problem for $\boldsymbol\delta$ is ordinary least
squares again, as in section 2.2, and repeating it is the Gauss–Newton method. SciPy's
`least_squares` adds a trust region, so that no single step goes further than the
linearization can be trusted. Starting from a deliberately poor guess:

<Figure video="/media/pendulum-lab/output_error_fit.mp4" width={100} alt="The simulated trajectory is pulled onto the measurements iteration by iteration while the residual drops and the parameters converge">

Robust output-error fit of the reference data, started from $\omega_0^2 = 5$, $\mu = 0.05$, $\theta_0 = 1.5$, $\dot\theta_0 = 0$, $b = 0$

</Figure>

Twenty-one improving steps take the RMS residual from $58^\circ$ to $2.2^\circ$ (the outliers
keep it above the $0.5^\circ$ sensor noise). Along the way the optimizer wanders, through a
damping seven times too large and a starting angle past upright, before it finds the valley.
Started from the regression estimate instead, it needs only 8 evaluations.

### Least squares is maximum likelihood, for Gaussian noise

Why minimize the sum of *squares*? If the noise is Gaussian with standard deviation
$\sigma$, the probability of the observed data given $p$ is

$$
\mathcal L(p) = \prod_k \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\left({-\frac{\big(y_k - \hat y_k(p)\big)^2}{2\sigma^2}}\right),
\qquad
-\log\mathcal L(p) = \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_k \big(y_k - \hat y_k(p)\big)^2 + \text{const}.
$$

Maximizing the likelihood is minimizing the sum of squares: with $\rho(r) = r^2$, the fit
is the **maximum-likelihood estimate**. But our noise is *not* Gaussian (section 2.1): its
outliers would be wildly improbable under a Gaussian, so least squares works hard to
accommodate them. A **robust loss** grows quadratically for ordinary residuals and only
linearly for large ones. We use the soft-L1 loss with scale $c$:

$$
\rho(r) = 2c^2\left(\sqrt{1 + \left(\tfrac{r}{c}\right)^2} - 1\right) \approx
\begin{cases} r^2 & \lvert r\rvert \ll c \cr 2c\lvert r\rvert & \lvert r\rvert \gg c \end{cases}
$$

We set $c = 3\hat\sigma$, where the noise level $\hat\sigma$ is estimated before fitting,
without any model, from the second differences of the data:
$\hat\sigma = \tfrac{1.4826}{\sqrt 6}\thinspace\mathrm{MAD}\left(y_{k+1} - 2y_k + y_{k-1}\right) = 0.532^\circ$
(true: $0.5^\circ$). Residuals up to about $3\hat\sigma$ count as ordinary noise; an
outlier of $30^\circ$ now weighs like about 355 ordinary samples instead of 3600, ten
times less.

<Figure src="/media/pendulum-lab/output_error_fit.png" width={100} alt="The robust fit on the reference data, and residuals that stay within the noise band apart from the outliers">

Reference data with the robust fit; below, the residuals with the $\pm 2\hat\sigma$ band

</Figure>

On the reference data, with 95% confidence intervals (computed below):

| | Ordinary least squares | Robust (soft-L1) | Truth |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\omega_0^2$ | $9.8127 \pm 0.0073$ | $9.8096 \pm 0.0018$ | $9.8100$ |
| $\mu$ | $0.2002 \pm 0.0010$ | $0.20015 \pm 0.00025$ | $0.2000$ |
| $\theta_0$ [rad] | $2.0965 \pm 0.0068$ | $2.0943 \pm 0.0017$ | $2.0944$ |
| $\dot\theta_0$ [rad/s] | ${-0.005} \pm 0.027$ | $0.001 \pm 0.007$ | $0$ |
| $b$ [rad] | $0.0263 \pm 0.0017$ | $0.0261 \pm 0.0004$ | $0.0262$ |
| noise estimate $\hat\sigma$ | $2.21^\circ$ | $0.52^\circ$ | $0.5^\circ$ |

Both fits contain the truth in every interval. The errors are tiny compared with section
2.2: $\hat\omega_0^2$ is off by $+0.028$% and $-0.004$%, against $-0.9$% for equation
regression. The difference between the two losses is the size of the intervals: the
outliers make ordinary least squares believe the noise is $2.2^\circ$, and its intervals
are four times wider.

### Accuracy and precision over repeated experiments

One dataset cannot separate a good method from a lucky one, so we again repeat each
method on 200 new datasets of the same experiment:

<Figure src="/media/pendulum-lab/output_error_precision.png" width={100} alt="Error distributions over 200 repeated experiments: regression is biased, ordinary least squares is unbiased but spread out, robust least squares is unbiased and tight">

Each dot is one repeated experiment; vertical bars mark the mean

</Figure>

| Method | error in $\hat\omega_0^2$ | error in $\hat\mu$ |
|---|---|---|
| Equation regression (2.2) | $-0.93 \pm 0.05$% | $-1.19 \pm 0.25$% |
| Output error, ordinary least squares | $0.000 \pm 0.026$% | $0.000 \pm 0.188$% |
| Output error, robust | $0.000 \pm 0.009$% | $0.000 \pm 0.066$% |

(mean $\pm$ standard deviation). This figure is the clearest summary of the chapter so
far, and of two words that are easily confused:

- **Accuracy** is about bias: regression is systematically off; both output-error fits are
  centered on the truth.
- **Precision** is about spread: the outliers do not bias ordinary least squares here
  (they are symmetric), but they make it **three times less precise** than the robust fit.

### Honest error bars

Near the optimum the linearization also gives the uncertainty. If the noise has standard
deviation $\sigma$, the estimate is approximately Gaussian with covariance

$$
\mathrm{Cov}\big(\hat p\big) \approx \hat\sigma^2 \left(J^\top J\right)^{-1},
$$

and $\hat p_j \pm 1.96\sqrt{\mathrm{Cov}_{jj}}$ is a 95% confidence interval. For the robust
loss, `least_squares` returns a Jacobian in which the outliers are already down-weighted, and
$\hat\sigma$ is the robust spread (median absolute deviation) of the residuals.

Are these intervals honest, in the sense that they contain the truth 95% of the time?
Over the 200 repeated experiments:

| Method | $\omega_0^2$ | $\mu$ | $\theta_0$ | $\dot\theta_0$ | $b$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ordinary least squares | 96% | 94% | 96% | 94% | 94% |
| Robust | 98% | 95% | 96% | 96% | 96% |

Yes. The intervals from a *single* fit predict the spread of many. The two estimates are
also correlated: the region predicted for $(\hat\omega_0^2, \hat\mu)$ is a tilted ellipse,
and the repeated estimates fill it.

<Figure src="/media/pendulum-lab/output_error_uncertainty.png" width={75} alt="Scatter of 200 robust estimates of omega0 squared and mu inside the 95 percent ellipse predicted by one fit">

The 95% region from the Jacobian of one robust fit, and 200 repeated estimates

</Figure>

The correlation of $-0.39$ is physical: a slightly higher frequency can be partly traded
for slightly less damping when matching the measured decay.

An earlier version of this method set the robust scale to $c = \hat\sigma$. Then ordinary
samples were down-weighted too, and the intervals contained the truth 99 to 100% of the
time: too wide. Checking coverage on repeated experiments is how such mistakes are found.

### Local minima and the starting guess

The cost is not convex in $p$. Varying only $\omega_0^2$ (other parameters at the optimum)
shows one deep valley and, far from it, a nearly flat plateau with shallow dips:

<Figure src="/media/pendulum-lab/output_error_landscape.png" width={100} alt="Cost along omega0 squared: one deep valley and a flat plateau with small dips; arrows show where fifteen starting guesses converge">

Arrows: fifteen fits started from $\omega_0^2 \in \lbrace 2, 5, 20, 40, 55 \rbrace$ and three different $\theta_0$

</Figure>

Ordinary least squares reached the true $\omega_0^2$ from all fifteen starts. The robust fit
reached it from fourteen; started from $\omega_0^2 = 55$, $\theta_0 = 0.5$ it stopped at
$\omega_0^2 = 63.9$. The robust loss is the reason: far from the answer most residuals are
large, the loss grows only linearly there, and the plateau gives the optimizer little slope
to follow. Two habits make this harmless:

- **Start from a cheap estimate.** The equation regression of section 2.2 lands in the
  valley every time.
- **Check the residuals.** The stuck fit reports a noise level of $31.8^\circ$, sixty times
  the sensor noise: a fit that does not describe the data is easy to recognize.

### What we learned

- Fitting the simulated model to the data avoids differentiation, and with it the bias:
  output error is about **100 times more accurate** than equation regression here.
- Least squares is maximum likelihood for Gaussian noise. Outliers break that assumption;
  a robust loss restores the precision, three times better on this sensor.
- The Jacobian gives **honest error bars** from a single dataset, verified by coverage on
  repeated experiments.
- The problem is non-convex. Equation regression is the natural starting point, and a
  residual far above the sensor noise flags a failed fit.

## 2.5 Bayesian inference: the full posterior with MCMC

The code is in `src/pendulum_lab/bayes.py`; the numbers come
from `scripts/evaluate_bayes.py`.

### From one best estimate to a distribution

Section 2.4 produced a best estimate and error bars. Both are summaries of one object, the
**posterior distribution**: how plausible every parameter vector is, given the data. Bayes'
theorem gives it as

$$
p(q \mid \mathbf y) \propto p(\mathbf y \mid q)\thinspace p(q),
$$

the **likelihood** (how probable the measurements are if $q$ were true) times the **prior**
(what we believed before measuring). Output-error least squares found the peak of this
distribution and described its shape with a Gaussian built from the Jacobian. Markov chain
Monte Carlo (MCMC) draws samples from the posterior itself, with no assumption about its
shape. Comparing the two answers the question left open by section 2.4: **when are the
cheap Jacobian error bars good enough?**

### The likelihood: heavy-tailed noise, learned from the data

The likelihood needs a model of the noise, and section 2.1 showed that the noise has heavy
tails. Instead of copying the exact sensor model (which a real experimenter would not
know), we use the standard robust choice, a **Student-t distribution** of the residuals
$r_k = y_k - \hat y_k(q)$:

$$
p(r_k) = \frac{\Gamma\left(\tfrac{\nu + 1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\tfrac{\nu}{2}\right)\sqrt{\nu\pi}\thinspace\sigma}
\left(1 + \frac{r_k^2}{\nu\sigma^2}\right)^{-\frac{\nu + 1}{2}} .
$$

The tail weight $\nu$ controls how often large residuals are expected: for $\nu \to \infty$
this is a Gaussian, for small $\nu$ outliers are routine. Both the scale $\sigma$ and $\nu$
are sampled along with the physics, so the full parameter vector is
$q = \big(\omega_0^2,\ \mu,\ \theta_0,\ \dot\theta_0,\ b,\ \sigma,\ \nu\big)$.

The priors are broad and only encode physics: uniform on $0 \lt \omega_0^2 \lt 50$,
$0 \le \mu \lt 5$, $\lvert\theta_0\rvert \lt \pi$, $\lvert\dot\theta_0\rvert \lt 10\ \text{rad/s}$ and
$\lvert b\rvert \lt 0.2\ \text{rad}$, and log-uniform for $\sigma$ and $\nu$. Damping cannot be
negative, and the prior says so.

### Sampling the posterior

MCMC builds a random walk through parameter space whose long-run visiting frequency is the
posterior. In its simplest form (Metropolis), a proposed move from $q$ to $q'$ is accepted
with probability $\min\left(1, \tfrac{p(q' \mid \mathbf y)}{p(q \mid \mathbf y)}\right)$.
Only a *ratio* of posteriors is needed, so the normalizing constant of Bayes' theorem never
has to be computed.

We use `emcee`, an *ensemble* sampler: 32 walkers move together, each proposing steps along
the line towards another walker, so the ensemble adapts to correlated, elongated posteriors
by itself. Each posterior takes 3000 steps, of which the first 1000 are discarded as
burn-in (the walkers forgetting where they started). Every step evaluates the likelihood
for all 32 walkers, so one posterior costs 96 000 simulations of the pendulum, about
15 minutes. Two diagnostics show the chains are usable: about 45% of the proposals are
accepted, and the autocorrelation time of 49 to 127 steps leaves 500 to 1000 effectively
independent samples per posterior.

The first second of the reference recording: the walkers fill in the posterior of $(\omega_0^2, \mu)$

</Figure>

We sample the posterior for the reference recording, for the small-swing recording that
defeated SINDy in section 2.3, and for only the **first second** of the reference
recording, half a swing:

Top: posterior samples of $(\omega_0^2, \mu)$ with the 95% Gaussian ellipse of section 2.4. Bottom: posterior of $\mu$ and the Gaussian approximation

</Figure>

| Data | $\omega_0^2$, 95%: posterior | Jacobian | $\mu$, 95%: posterior | Jacobian | correlation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Reference | $\pm 0.0017$ | $\pm 0.0018$ | $\pm 0.00023$ | $\pm 0.00025$ | $-0.40$ |

**Plenty of data: the Gaussian is excellent.** On the reference recording the posterior is
symmetric, and the Jacobian intervals are only 5 to 10% wider than the true ones. The error
bars of section 2.4 are trustworthy, from one fit instead of 96 000 simulations.

**Small swing: the parameters are fine.** SINDy could not discover the *structure* from this
recording, but with the structure given, the *parameters* are almost as well determined as
from the large swing ($\omega_0^2$ to $\pm 0.05$%). Discovering an equation and estimating its
coefficients are different problems with different data requirements.

**Half a swing: the Gaussian breaks down.** One second of data cannot tell a faster pendulum
with more damping from a slower one with less: $\omega_0^2$ and $\mu$ become almost
interchangeable (correlation $+0.95$), and the posterior is a long, narrow band. It is also
**cut off at $\mu = 0$** by the prior and skewed towards larger values. The Gaussian
approximation knows none of this: its interval is 45% wider for $\omega_0^2$, symmetric, and
puts 14% of its probability on *negative* damping, which is physically impossible. The
truth, $\mu = 0.2$, lies well inside the posterior.

### The noise model, learned from the data

The posterior also contains the noise parameters. On the reference recording the tail
weight is $\nu = 2.7$ (95% interval $2.4$ to $3.0$): far from Gaussian. Without being told
about outliers, the likelihood concluded from the residuals that large errors are common,
and so it automatically gave them little influence, much like the robust loss of
section 2.4.

### What we learned

- The posterior is the complete answer to "what do the data say about the parameters?";
  MCMC samples it using only evaluations of likelihood times prior.
- With plenty of data the posterior is close to Gaussian, and the Jacobian error bars of
  output-error least squares are an excellent and far cheaper substitute.
- With little data the posterior is correlated, skewed and bounded by physics, and only
  sampling shows it.
- A heavy-tailed likelihood with a learned tail weight handles outliers by itself.
- Whether a *model structure* can be identified and whether its *parameters* can be are
  separate questions.

That completes chapter 2: from raw sensor data to parameters, discovered structure and
honest uncertainty, all computed *after* the experiment from the whole recording.

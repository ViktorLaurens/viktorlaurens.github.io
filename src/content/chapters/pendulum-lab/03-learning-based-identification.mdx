Chapter 2 asked what a recording of the angle says about the pendulum, and answered with a
model whose structure came from physics: an equation with three unknown numbers. This chapter
asks the same question with **more flexible models**, up to neural networks that know nothing
about pendulums. It is still identification, offline, from the whole recording (see the
[storyline](/projects/pendulum-lab/#the-storyline-one-problem-four-questions)); only the model class changes.

The code is in `src/pendulum_lab/learning.py`; the numbers come
from `scripts/evaluate_learning.py`. The networks are
written in PyTorch and trained on the CPU.

## 3.1 From parameters to functions

### A ladder of model classes

Every model in this report describes the same thing, how the state of the pendulum evolves,
but they differ in how much of that description is fixed in advance and how much is learned
from the data:

| model | what is learned | unknowns | section |
|---|---|---|---|
| the pendulum equation | $\omega_0^2$, $\mu$, $b$ | 3 | 2.2, 2.4, 2.5 |
| a library of candidate terms | which terms, and their coefficients | 12, most of them zero | 2.3 |
| the pendulum equation plus a network | a correction to the physics | 3 plus a small network | 3.5 |
| a network for the dynamics | the acceleration as a function of the state | 8600 weights | 3.4 |
| a network for the trajectory | the angle as a function of time | 50 000 weights | 3.2, 3.3 |

Going down the ladder, the model assumes less and can represent more, but it also has to
learn more from the same 1000 samples. The bottom row has fifty times more unknowns than
there are measurements. What keeps such a model from fitting the noise exactly is not the
data but the way it is trained, and that is worth understanding before trusting it.

### Training a network

A neural network is a function built from simple pieces. Here every hidden layer computes
$\mathbf h_{\ell+1} = \tanh\left(W_\ell \mathbf h_\ell + \mathbf c_\ell\right)$, and a final linear layer
produces the output. The weights $W_\ell$ and $\mathbf c_\ell$ are the unknowns. *Training*
is the same least-squares fit as in section 2.4, the robust loss included, but with a different
optimizer:

- **No closed form, many minima.** The loss is not convex in the weights. Training starts from
  random weights and follows the gradient downhill, so the result depends on the starting
  point: every network here is trained with a fixed random seed, and the spread over seeds is
  reported.
- **Gradients by automatic differentiation.** PyTorch records every operation of the forward
  computation and applies the chain rule backwards through it. That gives the exact gradient
  of the loss with respect to all weights, at the cost of about one extra forward pass.
- **Adam.** The optimizer takes small steps along a running average of the gradients, each
  weight scaled by its own running gradient size. The step size is lowered gradually to zero
  over the training run (a cosine schedule).

### The test: forecast what was not seen

Fitting the data is not enough; a flexible model fits almost anything. The test in this
chapter is therefore **prediction**: every method sees only the **first 10 s** of a recording
(1000 samples) and is then asked about the **last 10 s**, which it has never seen. Inside the
training window a model *interpolates*, after it it must *extrapolate*.

The physics model of chapter 2 sets the bar. Output error (2.4), fitted on the training window
only, finds $\omega_0^2 = 9.8099 \pm 0.0016$ and $\mu = 0.2002 \pm 0.0002$ on the reference
recording, and its simulation forecasts the unseen 10 s with an RMS angle error of
$0.040^\circ$ and a velocity error of $0.0021\ \text{rad/s}$:

<Figure src="/media/pendulum-lab/learning_split.png" width={100} alt="The reference recording split in a shaded training window and a test window; the simulation of the output-error fit lies on the true angle in both, with an error below a tenth of a degree throughout">

Reference recording. The output-error fit of section 2.4 sees only the shaded first 10 s

</Figure>

Three numbers, the right equation, and the forecast is as good as the fit. Every learned model
in this chapter is held against this.

## 3.2 A network for the trajectory

The most direct use of a network is to fit the measured curve itself: a function
$\hat\theta(t)$ from time to angle, with no physics at all. The network has four hidden layers
of 128 neurons (50 000 weights), its input is time scaled to $[{-1}, 1]$ over the training
window, and it is trained for 6000 Adam steps on the robust loss of section 2.4, which takes
about 20 s on a laptop CPU.

Two things come for free. The derivative $\tfrac{d\hat\theta}{dt}$ is available exactly, by the
same automatic differentiation that computes the gradients: the network is its own velocity
estimate. And the robust loss lets the outliers barely pull on the fit. One thing is
impossible: the network fits the measurement $y = \theta + b$, and without a model of the
dynamics nothing distinguishes the offset $b$ from the angle. The figures subtract the true
offset only for display.

### What the network learns first

<Figure src="/media/pendulum-lab/network_training.png" width={100} alt="Four snapshots of the network's curve during training over the measurements of the first ten seconds: after 100 steps a nearly flat line, after 400 steps the first two and a half swings, after 1500 steps all swings roughly, after 6000 steps all swings closely; below the training loss on logarithmic axes, flat for 100 steps and then falling by two orders of magnitude">

Reference recording, training window. Bottom: the training loss, with the four snapshots marked

</Figure>

Training proceeds in stages. For the first hundred steps the network finds only the average
level. Then it picks up the oscillation, starting with the large early swings, which dominate
the loss, and working its way to the smaller later ones. The fine details come last. This
preference for simple, smooth functions is typical of networks trained by gradient descent,
and it is what keeps 50 000 weights from fitting 1000 noisy samples exactly: training stops
long before the network gets to the noise.

**Inside the training window** the network is a good smoother. Its velocity is 1.5 times more
accurate than the Savitzky–Golay derivative of chapter 2, which is also a curve fit without a
model, but it is 20 times less accurate than the physics model. Near the edges of the window,
where it has data on one side only, its velocity error doubles ($0.087\ \text{rad/s}$ in the
first and last half second).

**Outside the training window** it knows nothing. Each $\tanh$ neuron levels off far from the
data, so the network's curve flattens to a constant and its velocity goes to zero: an RMS angle
error of $56^\circ$, against $0.04^\circ$ for the physics model. Nothing in the network says that
a pendulum keeps swinging; it was only ever asked to reproduce ten seconds of numbers.

The result does not hinge on the details:

| network, reference recording | velocity error, training window | angle error, test window |
|---|---|---|
| 2 layers of 32 neurons | $0.065\ \text{rad/s}$ | $82^\circ$ |
| 3 layers of 64 neurons | $0.097\ \text{rad/s}$ | $161^\circ$ |
| 4 layers of 128 neurons | $0.040\ \text{rad/s}$ | $56^\circ$ |
| the same, 20 000 steps instead of 6000 | $0.036\ \text{rad/s}$ | $54^\circ$ |
| the same, two other random seeds | $0.038$ and $0.028\ \text{rad/s}$ | $52^\circ$ and $61^\circ$ |

Larger networks fit the training window better, and no network extrapolates. The spread over
random seeds, a factor 1.4 in the velocity error, is as large as most of the differences between
the settings: one training run of a network is one sample of a random outcome. On the small
swing the picture is the same: $0.015\ \text{rad/s}$ inside (Savitzky–Golay:
$0.059\ \text{rad/s}$), and an angle error of $15^\circ$ outside, where the pendulum itself
swings by less than $7^\circ$.

### What we learned

- A network can represent the measured curve, and automatic differentiation gives its
  derivative for free: inside the training window it is a better smoother than
  Savitzky–Golay.
- What keeps a network with more weights than data from fitting the noise is the training
  itself: it learns smooth, large-scale shapes first.
- A model of the *trajectory* cannot forecast: it has learned what happened, not why. The
  physics model, with three numbers, forecasts ten seconds ahead a thousand times more
  accurately.

## 3.3 The physics-informed neural network

The network of section 3.2 knew nothing about pendulums. A **physics-informed neural network**
(PINN) is the same network, told the equation of motion. It still represents the angle as a
function of time, $\hat\theta(t)$, but it is now also asked to satisfy

$$
\ddot{\hat\theta} + \omega_0^2 \sin\hat\theta + \mu\dot{\hat\theta} = 0,
$$

with both time derivatives taken by automatic differentiation, at a set of **collocation
points**: times where the equation is checked, whether or not there are measurements. The
unknown parameters $\omega_0^2$, $\mu$ and the offset $b$ are trained together with the weights.
The loss has two parts, the robust data loss of section 3.2 and the mean square of the
equation's residual $r$:

$$
\mathcal L = \frac{1}{N}\sum_k \rho\left(\hat\theta(t_k) + b - y_k\right) +
\lambda\thinspace\frac{1}{M}\sum_j \left(\frac{r(t_j)}{10}\right)^2,
\qquad
r = \ddot{\hat\theta} + \omega_0^2 \sin\hat\theta + \mu\dot{\hat\theta} .
$$

Dividing the residual by $10\ \text{rad/s}^2$, about $\omega_0^2$ times one radian, puts both
parts on a comparable scale; the weight $\lambda$ sets the balance. The collocation points lie
every $0.02\ \text{s}$ from $0$ to $10\ \text{s}$. With the offset now a separate parameter, the network
represents the angle itself: physics is what separates $b$ from $\theta$.

This is an inverse problem solved in an unusual way. Output error (2.4) also fits the
parameters to the data through the equation, but it *solves* the equation with an
integrator. The PINN never integrates: it searches for a curve and parameters that are
consistent with the data and with the equation at the same time.

### Making it train

Trained naively, as in section 3.2 but with both losses from the start, the PINN fails. With a
strong physics weight ($\lambda = 100$) it finds a **trivial solution**: a flat line at
$\hat\theta = 0$ (within $0.01^\circ$), the hanging equilibrium, satisfies the equation almost exactly (residual
loss $6 \cdot 10^{-10}$) whatever the parameters are, and the data loss is simply ignored. The
angle error is $51^\circ$, and $\omega_0^2$ ends at a meaningless $6.69$.

The standard remedy is used here: **first fit the data alone** for 3000 steps, as in
section 3.2, then switch on the physics, its weight raised gradually from 0 to $\lambda$ over
the next 2500 steps, and train both for 5000 steps in total. By the time the equation enters,
the curve already looks like a pendulum, and the parameters only have to explain it. Training
takes about 70 s.

When it converges, **the PINN finds the same parameters as output error**, within its error
bars, and its curve fits the training window as well as the physics model does. Its velocity
inside the window, $\tfrac{d\hat\theta}{dt}$, is now accurate to $0.0021\ \text{rad/s}$, against
$0.040\ \text{rad/s}$ for the network without physics: the equation is a far better regularizer
than the implicit smoothness of section 3.2. The offset, which section 3.2 could not separate
from the angle at all, is found to $0.03^\circ$.

But "when it converges" carries weight. The physics weight has to be right within a factor of
ten: too weak and the parameters are biased because the equation barely matters, too strong
and the optimizer favors the equation over the data and the fit degrades. And even at the best
weight, one of three random seeds stalls before converging ($\omega_0^2 = 9.800$, six output-error
standard deviations off). It can be recognized without knowing the truth: its physics loss is
thirty times higher than that of the converged runs. In practice one trains several seeds and
keeps the run with the lowest loss. On the small swing the picture is the same: two seeds give
$\omega_0^2 = 9.808$ and $9.817$ (output error: $9.8174 \pm 0.0043$), the third stalls at $9.725$.

All of this takes about 70 s per run. Output error solves the same problem in under a second.

### Forecasting, first attempt: extending the curve

<Figure src="/media/pendulum-lab/pinn_fit.png" width={100} alt="Top: inside the training window both PINN networks follow the true angle; after it, the network with physics up to 10 s flattens like the plain network, the one with physics up to 20 s keeps oscillating for a while with a shrinking amplitude and then settles at zero, while the simulation with the PINN's parameters stays on the truth. Bottom: the equation residual of the first network jumps from a hundredth to several rad/s squared after 10 s; that of the second stays below one but is much larger than inside the training window">

Reference recording, $\lambda = 1$, seed 1. The equation residual is in $\text{rad/s}^2$

</Figure>

The PINN's network is still a function of time. Outside the collocation points nothing
constrains it, and after $10\ \text{s}$ it flattens just like the network of section 3.2
($33^\circ$ RMS). The obvious fix is to enforce the equation beyond the data as well, with
collocation points up to $20\ \text{s}$. It helps only a little: the network oscillates for one
or two more swings, then decays to $\hat\theta = 0$, the trivial solution again ($13^\circ$ RMS),
and the effort to satisfy the equation where there are no data pulls the parameters off
($\mu = 0.233$). This is a known weakness of PINNs: the equation constrains each point only
through its neighbors, and information propagates poorly over many periods of an oscillation.

The identified model itself does forecast well: the PINN's parameters and its own estimate of
the initial state, **simulated with an integrator**, are within $0.08^\circ$ of the truth over
the test window, against $0.04^\circ$ for output error.

### Forecasting, second attempt: solving the equation with a PINN

Extending the fitted curve asks the network to do two jobs at once: reproduce the data and
continue past it. A PINN can also be used the way it was originally introduced (Raissi,
Perdikaris and Karniadakis, 2019): to **solve** a differential equation, with no data at all.
After the identification, everything needed is known: the parameters, and the state at the
end of the training window, read from the PINN's own curve and its derivative at
$t_0 = 10\ \text{s}$ ($-37.46^\circ$ and $-0.585\ \text{rad/s}$, against $-37.54^\circ$ and
$-0.590\ \text{rad/s}$ in truth). A fresh network $\hat\theta(t)$ is then trained on the
equation residual and the initial conditions only:

$$
\mathcal L = \frac{1}{M}\sum_j \left(\frac{r(t_j)}{10}\right)^2 +
100\left[\left(\hat\theta(t_0) - \theta_0\right)^2 +
\left(\frac{\dot{\hat\theta}(t_0) - \dot\theta_0}{5}\right)^2\right],
$$

with the same residual $r$ as before, now with $\omega_0^2$ and $\mu$ fixed, at collocation
points every $0.01\ \text{s}$. Each network is trained with Adam and then polished with L-BFGS,
a quasi-Newton optimizer that is the usual final stage for PINNs.

One network for the whole test window fails in the same way as before: after a swing and a
half it decays to the trivial solution, $14^\circ$ RMS. The standard remedy is
**time-marching**: cut the interval into short windows and solve them one after another, each
window starting from the end state of the previous one. Within a window the equation only has
to carry the solution over part of a swing.

**So a PINN can predict the trajectory**, once it is used as an equation solver rather than as
a curve fit. With windows of a second or less it reproduces the integrator's solution to a few
hundredths of a degree, and what remains of the error against the truth, about $0.1^\circ$, is
shared with the integrator: it comes from the identified parameters and initial state, not
from the solver.

**For this problem it is purely illustrative.** The integrator computes the same forecast
fifteen thousand times faster, needs no training, no windows and no tuning, and is at least as
accurate. That is the honest verdict for any ordinary, low-dimensional differential equation:
classical solvers, whether time integrators, finite differences or finite elements, are faster
and more accurate. PINNs earn their place where those methods struggle:

- **Partial differential equations on complicated geometries**, where generating a good mesh is
  the hard part, for example flow, heat or elasticity problems; the original PINN paper solved
  the Burgers, Navier–Stokes and Schrödinger equations.
- **High-dimensional equations**, where any grid grows exponentially with the dimension: the
  Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation of optimal control, or Black–Scholes-type equations for many
  assets in finance. A network evaluated at random collocation points needs no grid.
- **Inverse problems for whole fields from sparse data**, where the unknown is not three numbers
  but a function of space and time: velocity and pressure in a flow inferred from images of a
  dye, blood flow from noisy MRI, or a map of material stiffness from a few sensors. There the
  equation fills in what the measurements cannot see.

### What we learned

- A PINN enforces the equation of motion on a network $\hat\theta(t)$ through an extra loss,
  and learns the parameters along with the weights.
- It needs care to train: without a data-only stage it collapses to the trivial solution
  $\hat\theta = 0$; the physics weight must be balanced; some seeds stall.
- When it converges it matches output error, the offset included, and its curve and velocity
  inside the training window are as good as the physics model's.
- Its fitted curve does not forecast. Used as an equation solver instead, from the identified
  parameters and state, and with time-marching over short windows, a PINN does predict the
  trajectory, as accurately as an integrator and fifteen thousand times more slowly. Its
  strengths lie with partial differential equations and fields, not with a pendulum.

## 3.4 The Neural ODE: learning the dynamics

Sections 3.2 and 3.3 both learned the *trajectory*, a function of time, and neither fitted
curve could forecast; the PINN only did once it was put to work solving the identified
equation anew. The lesson of chapter 1 is that a pendulum is not described by its trajectory but by
its **vector field**: the rule that turns any state into its rate of change. A **Neural ODE**
learns that rule. The kinematics stay exact, and only the acceleration is a network
$g$ of the state:

$$
\dot\varphi = \dot\varphi,
\qquad
\ddot\varphi = g(\varphi, \dot\varphi),
$$

where $g$ has three hidden layers of 64 neurons (8600 weights) and knows nothing about
sines, gravity or friction. To predict, the model is integrated like any other ODE, here with
the classical fourth-order Runge–Kutta (RK4) step at the sample time.

One detail matters. A black box has no idea that the pendulum hangs at $\theta = 0$: it can
just as well learn an equilibrium anywhere. The offset $b$ can therefore not be separated from
the angle, as in section 3.2, and the model lives in the sensor's own frame,
$\varphi = \theta + b$. The comparisons below shift the truth accordingly.

### Training in two stages

The two classical routes of chapter 2 both work with a network in place of the pendulum
equation, and they are used one after the other:

1. **Equation error, as in section 2.2.** Fit $g$ to the accelerations of the Savitzky–Golay
   smoothing, $g(\varphi_k, \dot\varphi_k) \approx \ddot\varphi_k$, sample by sample. This is
   plain regression with no integration and takes a few seconds.
2. **Simulation error, as in section 2.4.** Train through the integrator: simulate the model,
   compare with the measurements, and differentiate the robust loss through every RK4 step.
   Simulating the whole training window at once would let any early error grow over ten
   swings, so the window is cut into segments of $0.25\ \text{s}$, each with its own trainable
   initial state (**multiple shooting**), and a penalty keeps the end of each segment close to
   the start of the next. All 40 segments are simulated in parallel.

Both stages together take about 90 s.

**This is the first learned model that forecasts.** Simulated from its initial state for 20 s,
ten seconds beyond its data, it stays within a few degrees of the true angle, where the
trajectory network of section 3.2 was off by $56^\circ$. The test window is no problem for
it: the swings there are smaller than the ones it was trained on, so every state it reaches
lies inside the region it has seen. Learning the rule instead of the record is what makes the
difference.

The physics model is still forty to a hundred times more accurate. An acceleration error of 1% looks
small, but it changes the period slightly, and a phase error accumulates over ten swings.

The second stage is honest bad news. Training through the integrator makes the learned
acceleration slightly more accurate along the trajectory, yet the 20 s simulation gets
*worse* for all three seeds. Each segment only spans $0.25\ \text{s}$, a tenth of a swing, so
the loss rewards local agreement and says little about the period. Longer segments did not
help either in trials: one-second segments left the forecast at $2.5$ to $2.6^\circ$. With
this much data and this little noise, the cheap first stage was the better model.

### Reading physics out of the network

The network is a black box, but it can be asked what it learned. Fitting
$g(\varphi, \dot\varphi) \approx -a\sin(\varphi - \beta) - c\thinspace\dot\varphi$ to it over the
visited states gives an effective $\omega_0^2 = a$, $\mu = c$ and offset $b = \beta$:

| distilled from the Neural ODE, reference | $\omega_0^2$ | $\mu$ | $b$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| after equation error (all three seeds) | $9.725$ | $0.199$ | $1.50^\circ$ |
| after multiple shooting | $9.792$ to $9.802$ | $0.209$ to $0.210$ | $1.35^\circ$ to $1.45^\circ$ |
| regression on the equation (2.2), same window | $9.726$ | $0.199$ | |
| output error (2.4), same window | $9.810$ | $0.200$ | $1.47^\circ$ |

After the first stage, the network has learned *exactly* what the linear regression of
section 2.2 learned from the same smoothed derivatives, to three digits and including its
bias: smoothing flattens the peaks of the acceleration, and $\omega_0^2$ comes out 0.9% low. The
flexible model does not escape the flaws of its training data. Training through the
integrator moves $\omega_0^2$ towards the truth but pulls $\mu$ away from it.

### Outside the visited region

<Figure video="/media/pendulum-lab/neural_ode_releases.mp4" width={100} alt="Four pairs of pendulums released together: at 60 and 120 degrees the Neural ODE swings with the true pendulum; released at 170 degrees it falls faster and runs ahead; pushed from the bottom with 8 rad/s the true pendulum goes over the top while the Neural ODE turns back">

The Neural ODE trained on the reference recording (released at $120^\circ$), released from four new states, real time

</Figure>

<Figure src="/media/pendulum-lab/node_releases.png" width={100} alt="Angle over five seconds for four releases. Top row, model trained on the reference: agreement at 60 and 120 degrees, a growing phase error at 170 degrees, and no rotation when pushed with 8 rad/s. Bottom row, model trained on the small swing: good at 60 degrees, a visible phase error at 120 degrees, wrong at 170 degrees and for the push">

Each column is a new initial state; the rows are Neural ODEs trained on different recordings

</Figure>

| angle error over 5 s | released at $60^\circ$ | at $120^\circ$ | at $170^\circ$ | pushed at $0^\circ$ with $8\ \text{rad/s}$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| trained on the reference (up to $120^\circ$) | $0.6$ to $0.7^\circ$ | $1.8$ to $2.4^\circ$ | $65$ to $68^\circ$ | about $600^\circ$ |
| trained on the small swing (up to $20^\circ$) | $2.8^\circ$ | $12.8^\circ$ | $106^\circ$ | about $600^\circ$ |

A Neural ODE generalizes to **new initial conditions inside the region its data covered**, and
fails outside it. Released at $170^\circ$, beyond the $120^\circ$ it saw, the reference model
falls too fast and runs ahead. Pushed hard enough to go over the top, the true pendulum
rotates, but the learned one has never seen the inverted position and turns back. The model
trained on the small swing already misses at $120^\circ$, where it would have to know how the
restoring torque bends away from linear. The pendulum equation, fitted to the same small
swing, knows that $\sin\theta$ continues all the way around.

### What we learned

- Learning the vector field instead of the trajectory makes a learned model that forecasts:
  a few degrees after 20 s, against $56^\circ$ for the trajectory network.
- The network is only as good as its data: it reproduces the field where the training data
  went, inherits the bias of the derivatives it was trained on, and is wrong elsewhere.
- Training through the integrator with short segments did not improve the long-term forecast
  here; it is the standard method, not automatically the better one.
- A black box cannot tell the offset from the angle and needs 8600 weights for what the
  physics says with three numbers, forty to a hundred times more accurately.

## 3.5 Physics plus a network

The Neural ODE threw away everything known about pendulums. A **hybrid model** keeps the physics
and adds a network $g$ only for what the physics misses:

$$
\ddot\theta = -\omega_0^2 f(\theta) - \mu\dot\theta + g(\theta, \dot\theta) .
$$

Because the physics fixes the hanging equilibrium at $\theta = 0$, the offset $b$ is again a
separate parameter. The idea is attractive: the physics carries the structure and extrapolates,
the network mops up the rest.

To test it, the physics must be missing something, and the frozen recordings were generated by
the exact pendulum equation. So the physics is deliberately made incomplete: the **small-angle
model** of section 1.3, $f(\theta) = \theta$, which on the $120^\circ$ reference misses the
nonlinear part $-\omega_0^2(\sin\theta - \theta)$ of the gravity torque. As a control, the
same network is also added to the complete pendulum equation, $f(\theta) = \sin\theta$, where
there is nothing to correct.

### Look at the residuals first

Before adding a network, it is worth asking whether the physics leaves anything to learn. The
residuals of output error (2.4) answer that:

<Figure src="/media/pendulum-lab/hybrid_residuals.png" width={100} alt="Top: the residuals of the small-angle model oscillate with an amplitude of 15 to 45 degrees, clearly structured. Bottom: the residuals of the pendulum equation are white noise of about half a degree, with a few outliers marked as triangles at the edges">

Reference recording, training window. Triangles mark residuals beyond the plotted range

</Figure>

The small-angle model leaves a large, slowly varying pattern, the signature of missing
dynamics. Its fit also distorts what it can: $\omega_0^2 = 7.93$ instead of $9.81$, and an
initial state of $83^\circ$ and $3.7\ \text{rad/s}$ for a pendulum released at rest from
$120^\circ$. The pendulum equation leaves only the sensor noise and its outliers: there is
nothing for a network to find.

### Two ways to train

**Joint:** physics parameters and network together, in the same two stages as the Neural ODE
(equation error, then multiple shooting), with a penalty on the size of $g$ so that the physics
explains as much as it can. **Sequential:** the physics alone first, by output error; then,
with its parameters frozen, the network learns what is left. Both take about 25 s.

**When the physics is incomplete, the network helps.** It learns the missing part of the
gravity torque near the turning points of the swing, and the forecast error drops from
$23^\circ$ to between 5 and $13^\circ$. That is useful, but no better than the Neural ODE
without any physics, and the correction levels off beyond the $120^\circ$ it saw, where the
true missing term keeps growing. Released at $170^\circ$, the hybrid is as wrong as the
small-angle model itself: the network cannot add what the data never showed it.

**When trained jointly, the parameters lose their meaning.** The network and the physics can
trade one for the other: a smaller $\omega_0^2$ with a larger correction fits the data just as
well. With the small-angle model, $\omega_0^2$ ends at $7.27$; with the complete pendulum
equation, it is pulled from $9.81$ down to $8.73$, even though the physics was exactly right.
Without the penalty on the network's size, it is worse still: in a trial run the offset $b$ drifted to $20^\circ$.

**When the physics is complete, adding a network does harm.** The sequential hybrid keeps the
exact parameters, yet its forecast degrades from $0.04^\circ$ to between 10 and $16^\circ$.
There is nothing left to learn but noise, and the network learns it: the correction wiggles by
up to $4\ \text{rad/s}^2$ near $\theta = 0$, at zero velocity, a state the training data never
visited because the pendulum always passes the bottom at speed. Simulated forward, those
wiggles are enough to throw the swing off.

### What we learned

- A hybrid model helps exactly when the physics misses something, and the residuals of the
  physics alone show whether it does. Adding a network to a model whose residuals are already
  noise only adds error.
- Trained together, physics and network are not identifiable: the network absorbs part of the
  physics and the parameters drift, even when the physics is exact.
- The network's part generalizes no further than the data, so the hybrid extrapolates no better
  than its physics.

## Chapter summary

This chapter asked the question of chapter 2, *what do the measurements say about the
pendulum?*, with increasingly flexible model classes, and tested every answer on the same task:
fit the first 10 s of the reference recording, forecast the last 10.

| method (reference recording) | what is learned | $\omega_0^2$ | forecast, test window | released at $170^\circ$ | training |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| output error (2.4) | 3 parameters | $9.810 \pm 0.002$ | $0.04^\circ$ | $0.04^\circ$ | under 1 s |
| network for the trajectory (3.2) | $\theta(t)$, 50 000 weights | | $56^\circ$ | | 20 s |
| PINN (3.3), converged seeds | $\theta(t)$ and 3 parameters | $9.810$ | curve: $33^\circ$; equation solved by PINNs: $0.10^\circ$ | | 70 s, plus 133 s to solve |
| Neural ODE (3.4) | the vector field, 8600 weights | $9.73$ to $9.80$, distilled | $1.5$ to $4.3^\circ$ | $65$ to $68^\circ$ | 90 s |
| small-angle model plus a network (3.5) | physics and a correction | $7.3$ to $7.9$ | $5$ to $13^\circ$ | $120$ to $132^\circ$ | 25 s |

Every learned model was trained on data from a pendulum that follows the pendulum equation
exactly, and none of them beat that equation fitted by output error. Three lessons stand out:

- **Learn the dynamics, not the trajectory.** A curve fitted in time, physics-informed or not,
  has learned what happened, not why, and cannot be extended. The models that forecast are the
  ones that hold a rule for how the state evolves: the Neural ODE's vector field, or the
  identified equation, whether it is then solved by an integrator or by PINNs.
- **A flexible model is only as good as the region its data covered.** Inside it, the Neural
  ODE is within a few degrees; outside it, every learned part fails. Physics, by contrast,
  carries over to states that were never observed.
- **More flexibility is not free.** Each step down the ladder needed more care to train (data
  stages, physics weights, seeds, penalties) and gave up something: interpretable parameters, the
  separation of offset and angle, or accuracy.

None of this makes learning useless. The pendulum is the easiest possible case for physics: its
equation is known and exact. The learned models earn their place when the physics is
incomplete, which the small-angle experiment imitated and the Furuta pendulum of chapter 6 may
make real. The next chapter puts all identification methods side by side on the same frozen
data.

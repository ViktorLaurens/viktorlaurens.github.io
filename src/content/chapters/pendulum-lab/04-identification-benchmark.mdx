Chapters 2 and 3 identified the pendulum in eight different ways, each tested along the way
on its own terms. This chapter puts them **side by side under one protocol**, on the same
frozen recordings, and then asks two questions the earlier chapters did not: how does each
method hold up when the data get worse, and what happens when the pendulum does not follow
the pendulum equation (see the
[storyline](/projects/pendulum-lab/#the-storyline-one-problem-four-questions))?

The code is in `src/pendulum_lab/benchmark.py`; the
numbers come from `scripts/evaluate_benchmark.py`.

## 4.1 The scoreboard

### One protocol

Every method sees only the **first 10 s** of a recording and is scored on three things:

- **Forecast:** the RMS angle error over the last 10 s, which the method never saw, simulated
  from the method's own estimate of the state at the start of the recording.
- **Release:** the RMS angle error over 5 s after a release from $170^\circ$ at rest, a state
  outside both recordings. It asks whether the method learned the pendulum or only the
  recording.
- **Training time** on one laptop CPU. Times vary by tens of percent between runs; read them as
  orders of magnitude.

All angles are compared in the **sensor's frame**, $\varphi = \theta + b$. A black box cannot
separate the offset $b$ from the angle (3.4), so this is the only frame in which every method
can be scored. For the release, the black boxes are given the true offset as a gift; the
physics methods start from $170^\circ$ and add their own estimate of $b$. The settings are those
of the earlier chapters: three seeds for the PINN and the Neural ODE, each keeping the seed with
the lowest final training loss (no truth needed), and 1500 MCMC steps. That chain is shorter
than emcee's advice of 50 autocorrelation times, which is enough for a posterior median and an
interval, not for its tails.

Two frozen recordings from `data/`, both with $\omega_0^2 = 9.81$,
$\mu = 0.2$ and $b = 1.5^\circ$: the **reference**, released from $120^\circ$, and the
**small swing**, released from $20^\circ$, which barely leaves the linear regime.

Three groups appear:

- **The physics fitted by simulation** (output error, MCMC, PINN) all land at the noise floor,
  a twentieth of a degree, and carry over to the $170^\circ$ release. They differ only in cost:
  output error takes a fraction of a second, MCMC and the PINN take minutes for the same
  answer. MCMC adds honest error bars; the PINN adds nothing here that output error lacks.
- **The physics fitted on the equation** (regression, SINDy) is fast but limited by the
  differentiated noise (2.2). SINDy is the fragile one: with 10 s of data it keeps 10 of its 12
  terms on the reference, and on the small swing, where $\sin y$ and $y$ are nearly the same
  function, it trades one against the other and reports $\omega_0^2 = {-22\ 330}$, a model that
  still fits the small swing and explodes when released from $170^\circ$.
- **The learned models** forecast within a few degrees at best and fail outside the data. The
  Neural ODE is the best of them inside the visited region and among the worst outside it. The
  hybrid inherits exact physics and only adds error to it, as in 3.5.

The small swing makes every method's release harder, since $170^\circ$ is now far from anything
seen, but it separates them more sharply: the physics methods lose almost nothing, the learned
ones lose an order of magnitude.

## 4.2 Stress tests

The scoreboard used one recording each. Here one property of the data is changed at a time,
on 20 fresh recordings of the reference pendulum per setting (generated from fixed seeds, not
frozen), while everything else stays at the default sensor: **how long** the training window
is, **how noisy** the sensor is, and **how often** it produces outliers. The test window is
always the 10 s after the training window. The three fast methods run on all 20 recordings; the
Neural ODE, as the representative of the learned models, on 3 with one seed each.

The default setting, 10 s of training data with $0.5^\circ$ noise and 1 % outliers, is the
same point in all three panels.

<Figure src="/media/pendulum-lab/benchmark_stress.png" width={100} alt="Three panels of forecast error against training length, noise level and outlier fraction, on a log scale. Output error stays lowest everywhere, from 0.008 to 0.3 degrees; regression and SINDy stay near 4 to 6 degrees; the Neural ODE improves strongly with more data and degrades strongly with more noise">

Median forecast error over the recordings, with the band from the 25th to the 75th
percentile. Neural ODE: 3 recordings per setting

</Figure>

| forecast error, median (worst) | 2.5 s of data | $2^\circ$ noise | $5^\circ$ noise | 20 % outliers |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| regression (2.2) | $3.0^\circ$ ($11^\circ$) | $3.9^\circ$ ($360^\circ$) | $14^\circ$ ($722^\circ$) | $3.9^\circ$ ($360^\circ$) |
| SINDy (2.3) | $22^\circ$ ($98^\circ$) | $15^\circ$ (diverges) | $35^\circ$ (diverges) | $5.8^\circ$ (diverges) |
| output error (2.4) | $0.27^\circ$ ($0.81^\circ$) | $0.12^\circ$ ($0.35^\circ$) | $0.29^\circ$ ($0.85^\circ$) | $0.16^\circ$ ($19^\circ$) |
| Neural ODE (3.4) | $23^\circ$ ($29^\circ$) | $16^\circ$ ($17^\circ$) | $55^\circ$ ($1800^\circ$) | $3.8^\circ$ ($8.8^\circ$) |

- **Output error degrades gracefully.** Its error grows in proportion to the noise, from
  $0.008^\circ$ at $0.1^\circ$ noise to $0.29^\circ$ at $5^\circ$, and shrinks as the data
  grow. Its robust loss shrugs off 5 % outliers; at 20 %, one recording out of 20 went wrong.
- **Regression is limited by its bias, not by the data.** Its $\omega_0^2$ is about 0.9 %
  off whether it gets 2.5 s or 10 s, little noise or much: the bias of the Savitzky–Golay
  derivatives (2.2) does not average out. At high noise or many outliers, a few forecasts
  go over the top (an error of $360^\circ$ is one full turn).
- **SINDy and the Neural ODE need clean, plentiful data.** Both learn the structure as well
  as the numbers, and both fall apart when the data get short or noisy. The Neural ODE gains
  the most from data, from $23^\circ$ to $1.2^\circ$ between 2.5 s and 10 s. Its settings
  were tuned on the default setting, so part of its loss at high noise may be tuning, but
  that is part of its cost.

The more structure a method assumes, the less data it needs, and the less it suffers from
noise. That holds only while the structure is right, which 4.3 tests.

## 4.3 When the model is wrong

Every recording so far came from a pendulum that follows the pendulum equation exactly, which
is the most favourable case for the physics methods and the least favourable for the learned
ones. A new frozen recording, `data/air_drag/`, breaks that: the same
pendulum and sensor, released from $120^\circ$, but with **quadratic air drag**,

$$\ddot\theta = -\omega_0^2 \sin\theta - \mu \dot\theta - c \dot\theta \lvert\dot\theta\rvert, \qquad \mu = 0.1, \quad c = 0.05.$$

Drag takes energy out of the fast swings only, so the decay is no longer exponential: the
amplitude falls quickly at first and slowly later. No method in chapter 2 has the drag term,
except SINDy, whose library includes $y'\lvert y'\rvert$. The recording was generated once,
like the others, and is treated as real data.

Each method on the exact pendulum (grey) and on the pendulum with air drag (red)

</Figure>

|---|---|---|---|---|
| regression (2.2) | $9.723$ | $0.253$ | $5.5^\circ$ | $15^\circ$ |
| SINDy (2.3), $c = {-0.048}$ | $7.77$ | $0.386$ | $4.4^\circ$ | diverges |
| output error (2.4) | $9.865 \pm 0.005$ | $0.240 \pm 0.001$ | $4.1^\circ$ | $15^\circ$ |
| MCMC (2.5), 95 % interval | $9.850$ to $9.865$ | $0.237$ to $0.240$ | $3.9^\circ$ | $16^\circ$ |
| network for the trajectory (3.2) | | | $38^\circ$ | |
| PINN (3.3), simulated | $9.829$ | $0.253$ | $4.4^\circ$ | $13^\circ$ |
| Neural ODE (3.4) | | | $1.3^\circ$ | $51^\circ$ |
| pendulum equation plus a network (3.5) | $9.865$ | $0.240$ | $10^\circ$ | $2.9^\circ$ |
| true | $9.81$ | $0.1$, plus drag | | |

- **The physics methods fail gracefully but confidently.** They fold the drag into a larger
  damping, $\mu \approx 0.24$ instead of $0.1$, and fit an average of the fast and the slow
  decay: $4^\circ$ on the test window, $15^\circ$ on the release, where the swing is faster than
  anything seen and the drag matters most. Worse, their error bars do not show it: the MCMC
  interval for $\omega_0^2$, $9.850$ to $9.865$, excludes the true $9.81$, and the one for $\mu$
  is off by a factor of more than two. An uncertainty is computed *given the model*; with the
  wrong model it measures how well the wrong question was answered. The innovation checks of
  chapter 5 are one way to notice.
- **SINDy had the right term and could not use it.** The drag term is in its library, but with
  10 s of noisy data it selects 10 terms and gives the drag the wrong sign.
- **The Neural ODE is now the best forecaster**, $1.3^\circ$, because it never assumed the
  equation. Its release from $170^\circ$ still fails: the data limit it as before.
- **The hybrid is the best at the release**, $2.9^\circ$, but on the test window it is worse
  than the physics alone, because its network learned noise along with the drag. It uses the
  settings tuned in 3.5 for an exact pendulum; a smaller network or a stronger penalty would be
  the next thing to try.

Model mismatch is where learning earns its place. It is also where honesty is hardest: the
methods with the tightest error bars were wrong by the most standard deviations.

## Chapter summary

The three sections asked one question each: who is best when everything is right (4.1),
who survives worse data (4.2), and who survives a wrong model (4.3).

| situation | use | why |
|---|---|---|
| the equation is known | output error (2.4) | the noise floor in under a second, with standard errors |
| the data are short, noisy or full of outliers | output error (2.4) | its error grows with the noise, never with a breakdown; robust loss for outliers |
| the equation is known, and the uncertainty matters | MCMC (2.5) | the same answer, plus a posterior, at minutes of cost |
| only a quick first estimate is needed | regression (2.2) | milliseconds, a good start for output error |
| the equation is uncertain | look at the residuals of the physics first (3.5) | they show whether, and where, something is missing |
| something is missing, the data cover the states of interest and are clean | Neural ODE (3.4) | the best forecast under mismatch, but only inside the data, and it needs plenty of clean data |
| predicting far from the data | physics, corrected by hand if needed | no learned part extrapolated in any test |

The learned models were never the first choice on the exact pendulum, and none of them
extrapolated. Their case is model mismatch, which the Furuta pendulum, with its two coupled
bodies and real friction, may bring. Before that, chapter 5 turns from *what is the pendulum?* to
*where is it now?*

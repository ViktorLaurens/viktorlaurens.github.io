Chapters 2 to 4 identified the pendulum *after* the experiment, using the whole recording at
once. Even the Savitzky–Golay derivative of section 2.2 used samples from the future: its
window is centered on the sample it estimates. A controller cannot work that way. At every
sample of a $100\ \text{Hz}$ control loop it needs the full state $(\theta, \dot\theta)$
**now**, from past measurements only, while the sensor measures only $\theta$. Estimating
the state in real time is the subject of this chapter, and the bridge to the control
chapters that follow.

There is a second difference. Output error (2.4) trusted the model completely: given the
parameters and the initial state, the simulation fixes every later state, so estimating the
initial state was estimating them all. Here the model is allowed to be imperfect, through a
random *process noise*. The model can then no longer carry the state forward on its own, and
the state has to be re-estimated from the measurements at every sample. The two views meet
again in sections 5.5 and 5.6, where the filter turns out to be an identification method as
well (see the [storyline](/projects/pendulum-lab/#the-storyline-one-problem-four-questions)).

The code is in `src/pendulum_lab/kalman.py`; the numbers come
from `scripts/evaluate_kalman.py`.

## 5.1 The problem: real-time estimates from angle measurements

### Model-free attempts

The obvious causal estimate of the velocity is the backward difference
$\dot\theta_k \approx \tfrac{y_k - y_{k-1}}{h}$. Its noise is
$\tfrac{\sqrt 2\thinspace\sigma}{h} = 1.31\ \text{rad/s}$ for the Gaussian part of the sensor
noise alone, larger than the velocity itself on the small swing. Smoothing the differences
over time, for example with an exponential average
$\hat v_k = \hat v_{k-1} + \alpha\left(\tfrac{y_k - y_{k-1}}{h} - \hat v_{k-1}\right)$, trades
noise for **lag**: a causal smoother can only average the past, so its estimate trails the
truth.

<Figure src="/media/pendulum-lab/causal_estimates.png" width={100} alt="Backward differences scatter far around the true velocity; exponential smoothing follows it with visible lag and spikes at outliers">

Small-swing recording; exponential smoothing with $\alpha = 0.1$

</Figure>

| Causal estimate of $\dot\theta$ (small swing, $t \ge 2\ \text{s}$, outliers included) | RMS error |
|---|---|
| Backward difference | $5.26\ \text{rad/s}$ |
| Exponential smoothing, $\alpha = 0.1$ | $0.39\ \text{rad/s}$ |
| Exponential smoothing, $\alpha = 0.03$ | $0.27\ \text{rad/s}$ |

Without a model, every causal estimator is stuck between noise and lag. The way out is to
use what we learned in chapter 2: **how the pendulum moves.**

### Recursive Bayesian estimation

Section 2.5 computed a posterior from the whole recording at once. Here we want the
posterior of the *current* state given the measurements *so far*, updated as each sample
arrives. Two steps alternate:

1. **Predict.** Before measurement $k$ arrives, the model carries the belief about the
   state forward by one sample. The belief becomes less certain, because the model is not
   perfect.
2. **Update.** Measurement $y_k$ arrives and corrects the belief, weighted by how much we
   trust the sensor compared with the prediction.

The **Kalman filter** carries out these two steps exactly when the model is linear and all
uncertainty is Gaussian: then the belief stays Gaussian forever, and a mean and a
covariance describe it completely.

## 5.2 The Kalman filter

### The model, in discrete time

For small angles the pendulum is linear (chapter 1): with state
$\mathbf x = (\theta, \dot\theta)$,

$$
\dot{\mathbf x} = A\mathbf x, \qquad
A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \cr -\omega_0^2 & -\mu \end{bmatrix}.
$$

Between two samples, $h = 0.01\ \text{s}$ apart, this moves the state exactly by the matrix
exponential, $\mathbf x_{k+1} = F\mathbf x_k$ with $F = e^{Ah}$. To this we add **process
noise** $\mathbf w_k$ with covariance $Q$, which stands for everything the model leaves out,
and the measurement equation from section 2.1:

$$
\mathbf x_{k+1} = F\mathbf x_k + \mathbf w_k,
\qquad
y_k = H\mathbf x_k + b + v_k,
\qquad H = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix},
\quad v_k \sim \mathcal N(0, R).
$$

All model parameters come from identification. We identify once (section 2.4) and then
filter online: $\omega_0^2$, $\mu$ and $b$ are the robust output-error estimates from the
same recording, and $R = \hat\sigma^2$ with $\hat\sigma = 0.53^\circ$ from the noise estimate
of section 2.4.

**Process noise from physics.** We model the error as a random, white-noise acceleration
with spectral density $q$. Over one sample it disturbs both angle and velocity, in a
correlated way:

$$
Q = q \begin{bmatrix} \tfrac{1}{3}h^3 & \tfrac{1}{2}h^2 \cr \tfrac{1}{2}h^2 & h \end{bmatrix}.
$$

Its size follows from what the model ignores. On the small swing ($20^\circ$) the
linearization error $\omega_0^2(\theta - \sin\theta)$ is at most about $0.07\ \text{rad/s}^2$,
so an acceleration of about $0.1\ \text{rad/s}^2$ per sample gives $q = 10^{-4}$. Section 5.5
examines what happens when $q$ is chosen wrongly.

### The filter

With the belief about $\mathbf x_k$ written as mean $\hat{\mathbf x}$ and covariance $P$,
each sample performs:

$$
\text{predict:}\qquad
\hat{\mathbf x}^- = F\hat{\mathbf x}, \qquad P^- = FPF^\top + Q,
$$

$$
\text{update:}\qquad
S = HP^-H^\top + R, \qquad
K = P^-H^\top S^{-1}, \qquad
\hat{\mathbf x} = \hat{\mathbf x}^- + K\big(y_k - b - H\hat{\mathbf x}^-\big), \qquad
P = (I - KH)P^- .
$$

The **innovation** $y_k - b - H\hat{\mathbf x}^-$ is the surprise: how far the measurement is
from what the model predicted. The **Kalman gain** $K$ decides how much of that surprise to
believe. It is large when the prediction is uncertain ($P^-$ large) and small when the
sensor is noisy ($R$ large).

The update only *measures* the angle, yet it improves the velocity estimate too. The reason
is the correlation that the prediction step creates. A belief that is certain about the
angle but not about the velocity is a tall, thin region in the phase plane. One sample
later, states with higher velocity have moved further: the region **shears** into a tilted
one, in which angle and velocity are correlated. The next angle measurement then cuts
through that tilted region and narrows the velocity as well.

</Figure>

### Results on the small swing

The filter starts from the first measurement with an uninformed velocity
($\sigma_{\dot\theta} = 2\ \text{rad/s}$). Its uncertainty and gain settle within about
$0.8\ \text{s}$ to constant values. Those are the solution of the **discrete algebraic Riccati
equation**, $P^- = F\left(P^- - P^-H^\top S^{-1}HP^-\right)F^\top + Q$, which our tests check:

<Figure src="/media/pendulum-lab/kalman_convergence.png" width={100} alt="The estimate's standard deviations and the Kalman gains decrease during the first second and settle at the Riccati steady state">

Small-swing recording; dashed: the steady state from the Riccati equation

</Figure>

After that, the Kalman filter is just a fixed linear filter with gain
$K = (0.0285,\ 0.0413)$. The difference from the smoothing of section 5.1 is that its
prediction step runs the pendulum model, so it does not lag.

<Figure src="/media/pendulum-lab/kalman_small_swing.png" width={100} alt="The Kalman velocity estimate lies on the true velocity; its error stays within the filter's band except after outliers">

Top: velocity on the small swing. Bottom: the filter's error and its own $\pm 2\sigma$ band; red lines mark outliers

</Figure>

| Estimate of $\dot\theta$ (small swing, $t \ge 2\ \text{s}$) | RMS error |
|---|---|
| Exponential smoothing, best $\alpha$ | $0.27\ \text{rad/s}$ |
| Kalman filter | $0.0136\ \text{rad/s}$ |
| Kalman filter's own claim, $\sigma_{\dot\theta}$ | $0.0060\ \text{rad/s}$ |
| Kalman filter, away from outliers | $0.0054\ \text{rad/s}$ |

The Kalman filter is **20 times more accurate** than the best smoothing, and it computes
each estimate in about $13\ \mu\text{s}$ in plain Python, far within the
$10\ \text{ms}$ of a $100\ \text{Hz}$ loop.

Its error is, however, more than twice what the filter claims. The bottom panel shows why:
every outlier is taken at face value, kicks the estimate out of its band, and the error
decays only over about half a second. Away from outliers the error ($0.0054$) matches the
filter's own uncertainty ($0.0060$). The filter assumes Gaussian noise; section 5.4 teaches
it to recognize measurements that are too surprising to be true.

### What we learned

- Without a model, a real-time estimate must trade noise for lag. A Kalman filter uses the
  dynamics to escape that trade-off.
- Each sample alternates predict and update; the gain weighs the model against the sensor.
- An angle sensor reveals the velocity through the correlation that the dynamics create
  between angle and velocity.
- For a linear model the filter settles to a fixed gain, given by the Riccati equation.
- One assumption is already failing: the noise is not Gaussian.

## 5.3 The extended Kalman filter

On the small swing the linear model was good enough. On the reference recording, released
from $120^\circ$, it is not: chapter 1 showed that at this amplitude the real period is 37%
longer than the small-angle model predicts.

### Linearize where you are

The **extended Kalman filter** (EKF) keeps the nonlinear model wherever it can. The predicted
estimate follows the true equation of motion, integrated over one sample with a fourth-order
Runge–Kutta step:

$$
\hat{\mathbf x}^- = \hat{\mathbf x} + \int_{t_k}^{t_k + h} \mathbf f(\mathbf x)\thinspace dt,
\qquad
\mathbf f(\mathbf x) = \begin{bmatrix} \dot\theta \cr -\omega_0^2\sin\theta - \mu\dot\theta \end{bmatrix}.
$$

Only the covariance needs a linear model, and the EKF linearizes *around the current
estimate*. That linearization is the Jacobian from the fixed-point analysis of chapter 1,
now evaluated wherever the pendulum happens to be rather than at an equilibrium:

$$
A(\hat{\mathbf x}) = \frac{\partial\mathbf f}{\partial\mathbf x}\bigg|_{\hat{\mathbf x}}
= \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \cr -\omega_0^2\cos\hat\theta & -\mu \end{bmatrix},
\qquad
F_k = e^{A(\hat{\mathbf x})h},
\qquad
P^- = F_k P F_k^\top + Q .
$$

The measurement $y_k = \theta_k + b + v_k$ is already linear, so the update step is exactly
that of section 5.2. Near the bottom, $\cos\hat\theta \approx 1$ and the EKF *is* the linear
filter; at $120^\circ$, $\cos\hat\theta = -0.5$ and the local dynamics are completely different.

### Results on the large swing

Both filters use the identified parameters and the same process noise as before; only the
model differs.

</Figure>

Reference recording. Bottom: actual error (solid) and the filter's own standard deviation (dashed)

</Figure>

| $\dot\theta$ on the reference ($t \ge 2\ \text{s}$) | RMS error | largest error | filter's claim $\sigma_{\dot\theta}$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Linear Kalman filter | $0.417\ \text{rad/s}$ | $1.71\ \text{rad/s}$ | $0.0060\ \text{rad/s}$ |
| Extended Kalman filter | $0.0119\ \text{rad/s}$ | $0.062\ \text{rad/s}$ | $0.0060\ \text{rad/s}$ |
| Savitzky–Golay of section 2.2 (*non-causal*) | $0.062\ \text{rad/s}$ | | |

The linear filter's estimated swing is too large and runs ahead of the real one, and its angle
error reaches $5.8^\circ$ RMS. Worse, it *claims* the same small uncertainty as the EKF: its
actual error is 70 times larger than what it reports. **A wrong model does not make a
Kalman filter uncertain, it makes it wrong and confident.** Its error shrinks as the swing
decays, because its model becomes valid again: the failure is the model's, not the filter's.

The EKF tracks the large swing as well as the linear filter tracked the small one; away from
outliers its error ($0.0059\ \text{rad/s}$) again matches its claimed uncertainty. It is even
five times more accurate than the Savitzky–Golay derivative of chapter 2, which used future
samples: **a model of the dynamics is worth more than knowing the future measurements.**
On the small swing both filters perform alike, as they should. The EKF costs about
$35\ \mu\text{s}$ per sample, three times the linear filter, mostly for the matrix
exponential that is now recomputed at every step.

### What we learned

- The EKF follows the nonlinear model for the estimate and linearizes it, around the current
  estimate, only for the covariance.
- With the wrong model a Kalman filter fails silently: its error bars stay small while its
  estimate is far off. Consistency checks (section 5.5) are how such failures are found.
- A causal filter with a good model beats a non-causal estimate without one.

## 5.4 Outliers and innovation gating

The sensor of chapter 2 occasionally returns a reading that is wildly wrong: 28 times in the
20 s of the reference recording, by $1.7^\circ$ to $36^\circ$. A Kalman filter has no notion of
an outlier. It weighs every measurement by the same gain, so each spike pulls the estimate
off and the error of the EKF in section 5.3 is dominated by these moments: $0.0059\ \text{rad/s}$
away from outliers, $0.0119\ \text{rad/s}$ overall.

### The filter already knows what to expect

Before each update the filter predicts the measurement, $\hat y_k = \hat\theta^-_k + b$, and
its uncertainty, $S_k = H P^-_k H^\top + R$. The **innovation** $\nu_k = y_k - \hat y_k$ is the
surprise. If the filter's model is right, $\nu_k$ is Gaussian with variance $S_k$, so the
normalized innovation squared

$$
d_k^2 = \frac{\nu_k^2}{S_k}
$$

follows a $\chi^2$ distribution with one degree of freedom. That gives a test for free:
**if $d_k^2 \gt \gamma$, skip the update** and keep the prediction. With $\gamma = 9$ a
measurement is rejected when it lies more than $3\sqrt{S_k}$ from the prediction, which for
a correct filter happens to only 0.27% of good measurements. On the reference
$\sqrt{S_k} \approx 0.54^\circ$, so the gate is about $\pm 1.6^\circ$ wide. This costs one
comparison per sample and is how almost every practical Kalman filter handles outliers.

<Figure video="/media/pendulum-lab/gating_innovations.mp4" width={100} alt="Innovations of the gated filter appearing one by one inside a narrow gate band; three outliers fall outside and are rejected; below, the velocity error of the ungated filter jumps at each outlier while the gated one stays flat">

Reference recording, 8.3 s to 9.4 s, ten times slowed down. Triangles mark innovations off the scale

</Figure>

| $\gamma = 9$ ($3\sqrt{S}$) | 28 of 28 | 0 | $0.0030\ \text{rad/s}$ | $0.0100\ \text{rad/s}$ |

| $\gamma = 100$ ($10\sqrt{S}$) | 22 of 28 | 0 | $0.0030\ \text{rad/s}$ | $0.0096\ \text{rad/s}$ |

With $\gamma = 9$ every outlier is caught without a single false alarm, and the error drops
four times, to $0.0030\ \text{rad/s}$. That is *below* the $0.0059\ \text{rad/s}$ measured away
from outliers in section 5.3: an outlier does not only cause a spike, the filter needs the
better part of a second to forget it.

The result is remarkably insensitive to $\gamma$. A tight gate wastes a few good samples, a
loose gate lets the small outliers through ($\gamma = 100$ misses the six below $5^\circ$),
but the small ones barely matter. Two numbers hint at a problem for the next section, though.
The gated filter's actual error is now within its own $2\sigma$ at every sample after the start-up, and the gate
fires *less* often on good data than the $\chi^2$ distribution predicts (0 instead of about 5
at $\gamma = 9$, 56 instead of about 90 at $\gamma = 4$). Both say the same thing: the filter's
uncertainty is now too large. The process noise $q$ was chosen in section 5.2 to cover the
linearization error of the *linear* model; the EKF and the gate no longer need that much.

### The danger: locking out

The gate trusts the filter's own prediction to judge the measurements. If that prediction is
wrong *and* confident, the gate rejects exactly the measurements that would correct it. To
show this, start the EKF $10^\circ$ off while claiming an uncertainty of $0.1^\circ$:

<Figure src="/media/pendulum-lab/gating_lockout.png" width={100} alt="Angle error over time for a confident wrong start: the ungated filter recovers within a second, the gated filter stays off by up to 23 degrees for 15 seconds">

Reference recording, EKF started $10^\circ$ off with a claimed $\sigma_\theta = 0.1^\circ$

</Figure>

Without a gate the filter is within $1^\circ$ after $1.2\ \text{s}$. With $\gamma = 9$ it rejects
1400 of the 2000 measurements: every disagreement looks like an outlier, and it only accepts a
measurement when its wrong trajectory happens to cross the true one. It is still $12^\circ$ off
at $t = 5\ \text{s}$ and only recovers for good after $15.5\ \text{s}$, once the swing has decayed
enough for the two trajectories to meet. Practical safeguards are an honest initial
covariance, a start-up period without gating, and a reset when many consecutive samples are
rejected; a long run of rejections is never "a lot of outliers", it is a lost filter.

### What we learned

- The innovation and its predicted variance give every Kalman filter a built-in outlier test:
  reject a measurement when $\nu_k^2 \gt \gamma S_k$.
- On the reference a $3\sqrt{S}$ gate rejects all 28 outliers and nothing else, and reduces the
  velocity error four times. The exact threshold matters little.
- The gate is only as good as the filter's own uncertainty: a confident, wrong filter locks
  itself out. And how often the gate fires on clean data is already a consistency test.

## 5.5 Consistency and tuning the noise

A Kalman filter reports two things: an estimate, and how uncertain it is. Section 5.3 showed
a filter that was wrong and confident; section 5.4 ended with one that is now *too modest*.
Both numbers matter: the gate of section 5.4, a controller, or a fault detector downstream
all act on the reported uncertainty. A filter whose uncertainty matches its actual errors is
called **consistent**. The filter has only two knobs that set its uncertainty: the
measurement noise $R$ and the process noise $q$. Until now both were set by reasoning, not
by checking.

### Two tests

**With the truth** (only in simulation, or here because the frozen data were recorded with
it), the normalized estimation error squared (NEES)

$$
\epsilon_k = \mathbf e_k^\top P_k^{-1} \mathbf e_k,
\qquad
\mathbf e_k = \mathbf x_k - \hat{\mathbf x}_k,
$$

follows a $\chi^2$ distribution with 2 degrees of freedom, so its mean should be 2. Below 2
the filter is underconfident, above 2 overconfident.

**Without the truth**, only the innovations of section 5.4 are available. Their normalized
squares $d_k^2 = \tfrac{\nu_k^2}{S_k}$ (the NIS, normalized innovation squared) should average 1, and if $\nu_k$ really is Gaussian with
variance $S_k$, the product of these Gaussians is the likelihood of the measurements:

$$
\log p(y_1, \dots, y_N) = -\frac{1}{2} \sum_k \left( \log 2\pi S_k + \frac{\nu_k^2}{S_k} \right).
$$

This is the same output-error idea as section 2.4, now evaluated by the filter itself.

### The innovations calibrate $R$

The gated EKF of section 5.4 has a mean NIS of $0.86$: its innovations are smaller than it
expects. The innovation variance has two parts, $S_k = H P^-_k H^\top + R$, and here the first
is only about 3% of $S_k$: one step ahead, the filter predicts the angle far better than the
sensor measures it. So the NIS checks almost only $R$, and the innovations themselves give
a better estimate of it, $R = \overline{\nu_k^2} - \overline{H P^-_k H^\top}$:

| noise level of the sensor, $\hat\sigma = \sqrt{R}$ | |
|---|---|
| from second differences (section 2.1, used so far) | $0.532^\circ$ |
| from the innovations | $0.493^\circ$ |
| true value | $0.500^\circ$ |

The second-difference estimate is model-free, but it is a robust estimate from a median, which
makes it noisy, and outliers spill into three second differences each. The filter, which
knows the dynamics, does better. With this $R$ the mean NIS is $1.000$.

### $q$ is invisible to the NIS

For $q$ the same test fails. Across five decades of $q$ the mean NIS stays between $0.99$ and
$1.01$, because $q$ only changes the small $H P^- H^\top$ part of $S_k$. The truth is not
indifferent at all:

<Figure src="/media/pendulum-lab/consistency_sweep.png" width={100} alt="Four panels against the process noise q on a log scale: the mean NIS is flat at 1; the log-likelihood is flat for small q and drops beyond a few times 10 to the minus 6; the mean NEES falls from 40 to about 1 and crosses 2 near 2 times 10 to the minus 6; the actual velocity error grows with q while the filter's own sigma grows faster">

Reference recording, gated EKF ($\gamma = 9$), $R$ from the innovations. Yellow line: the chosen $q$; teal line: $q$ of sections 5.2 to 5.4

</Figure>

The NEES (bottom left) crosses 2 at about $q = 2 \cdot 10^{-6}$; at the $q = 10^{-4}$ of
sections 5.2 to 5.4 it is about 1, which is what an angle error matching its uncertainty and a
velocity error far *below* its uncertainty add up to. The bottom right panel shows the price:
the filter's own $\sigma_{\dot\theta}$ is twice its actual error, and the actual error itself
is 60% larger than after tuning, because a large $q$ makes the filter follow the noisy
measurements instead of its good model.

The likelihood (top right) is the one measurement-only test that sees $q$. It does not
pick a single value: it is flat for small $q$, because the data cannot tell a tiny $q$ from
zero, and it drops clearly once $q$ is too large. A rule that follows from this is to **take
the largest $q$ that the data cannot distinguish from the best**, the point where the
log-likelihood has dropped by $1.92$ (a 95% likelihood-ratio interval). Larger is the safe
side: section 5.4 showed what an overconfident filter does with a gate. On the reference this
gives $q = 2.6 \cdot 10^{-6}$, chosen without the truth, and it lands right next to the
NEES-consistent value.

identified parameters being very slightly off.

| tuned $q = 2.6 \cdot 10^{-6}$ | 3.12 | $0.103\ \text{rad/s}$ | 1004 of 2000 |
| $q = 10^{-4}$ | 1.21 | $0.021\ \text{rad/s}$ | 38 of 2000 |

The tuned filter trusts its model so much that it rejects half of the measurements and its
error grows fifty times. The filter with the larger $q$ degrades far more gracefully. Two
things save the situation. First, the innovations see the problem even without the truth: a
mean NIS of 3 is unmistakable, so **monitoring the NIS online** is how a deployed filter
knows it has lost its model. Second, the honest fix for a model error is not a larger $q$, it
is a better model, which is the subject of the next section.

### What we learned

- A filter is consistent when its reported uncertainty matches its actual errors: mean NEES 2
  (needs the truth), mean NIS 1 (needs only the measurements).
- Here the NIS calibrates $R$, but it cannot see $q$: one step ahead, the prediction is much
  more precise than the sensor. The innovation likelihood can, and its flat region gives a
  rule: the largest $q$ the data cannot distinguish from the best.
- Tuned this way, the gated EKF is 37% more accurate and honest about it, but it relies on its
  model: with a 1% parameter error it loses track. The running NIS shows that immediately.

## 5.6 Estimating the parameters online

Every filter so far borrowed its model from chapter 2: identify offline on the whole
recording, then filter. Section 5.5 showed how much depends on that model: 1% off in
$\omega_0^2$ and the tuned filter lost track. The Kalman filter can also learn the model
itself, from the same measurements, while it runs.

### Parameters as states

The trick is to treat the unknown parameters as states that do not move. The augmented
state is

$$
\mathbf z = \begin{bmatrix} \theta & \dot\theta & \omega_0^2 & \mu & b \end{bmatrix}^\top,
\qquad
\dot{\mathbf z} = \begin{bmatrix} \dot\theta \cr -\omega_0^2\sin\theta - \mu\dot\theta \cr 0 \cr 0 \cr 0 \end{bmatrix},
\qquad
y_k = \theta_k + b + v_k .
$$

The parameters enter the dynamics nonlinearly ($\omega_0^2$ multiplies $\sin\theta$), so this
is an EKF even for small angles. Its Jacobian gains two columns that say how a parameter
error turns into an acceleration error:

$$
A(\hat{\mathbf z}) = \begin{bmatrix}
0 & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 \cr
-\hat\omega_0^2\cos\hat\theta & -\hat\mu & -\sin\hat\theta & -\hat{\dot\theta} & 0 \cr
0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \cr
0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \cr
0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
H = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

Nothing else changes: predict, update, gate. The parameters are never measured; they are
corrected through their covariance with the angle. That covariance builds up during the
prediction: a larger $\omega_0^2$ makes the pendulum swing back sooner, a larger $\mu$ makes
it lose amplitude, and a larger $b$ shifts every measurement. Every innovation therefore
says a little about each of them.

To make the test honest, the filter starts **without chapter 2**: $\omega_0^2 = 8 \pm 3$
(a pendulum length guessed at about $1.2\ \text{m}$), $\mu = 0 \pm 0.5$, $b = 0 \pm 5^\circ$, with
$R$ from second differences and $q$ from section 5.5.

Within about a second and a half, half a period of this large swing, the filter knows
$\omega_0^2$ to 1% and $\mu$ to 10%; after 20 s it agrees with the offline fit of chapter 2
within the error bars of both. **The recursive filter, one sample at a time, reproduces the
batch estimate that saw the whole recording at once.** Its error bars are honest too: the
true values lie within $\pm 2\sigma$ 97% to 100% of the time after the start. Meanwhile it
estimates the state: after the first $5\ \text{s}$ its velocity error is $0.0017\ \text{rad/s}$,
no worse than the tuned filter of section 5.5 that was handed the offline model
($0.0021\ \text{rad/s}$ over the same interval). All of this costs $0.06\ \text{ms}$ per sample.

Two differences with the offline fit are worth noting. The online error bars are somewhat
wider ($\omega_0^2$: $0.0013$ against $0.0009$) because the filter allows process noise: it
can explain part of any mismatch as a random push rather than as a parameter error, so it
learns a little less from the same data. And on the small swing, where the parameters are
harder to separate, the online estimate is less precise: $\omega_0^2 = 9.8159 \pm 0.0044$
against $9.8126 \pm 0.0025$ offline, with the true value outside $2\sigma$ 13% of the time,
and a velocity error after $5\ \text{s}$ of $0.0030$ against $0.0018\ \text{rad/s}$.

### The start-up is fragile

With constant parameters the filter never forgets: their covariance only shrinks, so what
they learn early stays. That makes the first samples, when everything is uncertain, the
dangerous ones. In a first version the gate of section 5.4 was switched on after
$0.2\ \text{s}$, a common choice to let a filter settle. The recording has an outlier at
$0.11\ \text{s}$:

<Figure src="/media/pendulum-lab/online_gate_late.png" width={100} alt="Estimate of omega0 squared over time on a logarithmic time axis: with the gate on from the first sample it converges to 9.81; with the gate on from 0.2 seconds, an outlier at 0.11 seconds throws it to above 20 where it stays">

Reference recording, augmented EKF, the same start with two gate settings

</Figure>

The ungated filter takes the $22^\circ$ outlier at face value. With the parameters still
uncertain, it explains the jump partly as a much stiffer pendulum, $\omega_0^2 \approx 20$.
From then on its predictions are wrong, and once the gate is switched on it rejects 1904
of the remaining 1980 measurements as outliers: the lock-out of section 5.4, now permanent.
It ends at $\omega_0^2 = 23.1$, $\mu = 2.1$, $b = -3.3^\circ$. With the gate on from the very first
sample, the same outlier is rejected: the angle uncertainty collapses within a few samples,
and the gate becomes narrow long before the parameters are known.

### Parameters that change

A parameter whose covariance shrinks to zero cannot follow a change: if the damping doubles
halfway through a run, the filter keeps the old value. The remedy is the same as for the
state, a small process noise on the parameters, a random walk with spectral density
$q_\mu$. In a simulated test where $\mu$ doubles from 0.1 to 0.2 halfway, the filter with
$q_\mu = 10^{-4}$ ends at $0.209$, the one with constant parameters stays at $0.101$. The cost
is noisier estimates when nothing changes; $q_\mu$ sets how fast the filter forgets, the
same trade-off as $q$ in section 5.5. The frozen recordings have constant parameters, so the
filter here uses none.

### What we learned

- Unknown parameters can be estimated online by making them states; the EKF then learns the
  model and the state together, one sample at a time.
- From a rough guess, the filter learns $\omega_0^2$ and $\mu$ within one or two swings and
  ends where the offline fit of chapter 2 ended, with honest error bars, at $0.06\ \text{ms}$
  per sample.
- What a filter with constant parameters learns early, it keeps. Outliers must be rejected
  from the first sample, and parameters that may change need a process noise of their own.

## 5.7 The unscented Kalman filter

The EKF approximates the dynamics: it replaces them by a straight line through the current
estimate, and pushes the covariance through that line. The **unscented Kalman filter**
(UKF) approximates the belief instead. It keeps the true nonlinear model, and pushes a
handful of carefully chosen points through it.

### Sigma points

For a state with $n$ components, take $2n + 1$ **sigma points**: the mean, and two points on
either side of it along each axis of the covariance,

$$
\mathcal X_0 = \hat{\mathbf x},
\qquad
\mathcal X_{\pm i} = \hat{\mathbf x} \pm \sqrt{n}\thinspace \mathbf s_i,
\qquad
\mathbf s_i = \text{column } i \text{ of a square root of } P .
$$

They have exactly the mean and covariance of the belief. Move each one with the full
nonlinear model, one RK4 step, and measure the cloud they form: its weighted mean and
covariance are the prediction. This is the **unscented transform**. It needs no Jacobian,
and it is exact for the mean up to second order where the linearization is only first-order:
it sees that a belief spread over a curved region has a mean that the curve's tangent misses.
The measurement of the pendulum is linear, so the update step is unchanged. In the code
only the prediction differs, and it costs five model steps instead of one.

### Where the linearization goes wrong

The difference shows when a wide belief is pushed far through a strongly nonlinear region,
without measurements to correct it. Start near $120^\circ$ with $\sigma_\theta = 15^\circ$ and
$\sigma_{\dot\theta} = 0.5\ \text{rad/s}$, and compare both filters with 20 000 samples of the
same belief, each moved with the exact model:

</Figure>

The same belief at three moments. Dots: the sigma points of the UKF

</Figure>

| mean of the belief after | samples (truth) | EKF | UKF |
|---|---|---|---|
| $0.5\ \text{s}$ | $60.4^\circ$ | $57.5^\circ$ (0.53 $\sigma$ off) | $60.4^\circ$ (0.00 $\sigma$ off) |
| $1.0\ \text{s}$ | $-66.2^\circ$ | $-73.5^\circ$ (0.41 $\sigma$ off) | $-67.0^\circ$ (0.09 $\sigma$ off) |

The UKF puts the mean where the samples are; the EKF is off by half a standard deviation.
Neither gets the *shape* right: the cloud bends into a banana that no ellipse can describe,
and after one second both ellipses have only about 40% of the area of the samples'
covariance ellipse. From $20^\circ$, where
the dynamics are nearly linear over the belief, both filters are indistinguishable from the
samples.

### In the filter

With measurements every $10\ \text{ms}$ the belief never gets wide: after the start-up the
angle is known to a fraction of a degree, and over such a small region every smooth model is
linear. On the reference recording, with the tuned noise of section 5.5:

| reference, $\dot\theta$ RMS error ($t \ge 2\ \text{s}$) | EKF | UKF |
|---|---|---|
| measured at $100\ \text{Hz}$ | $0.0019\ \text{rad/s}$ | $0.0019\ \text{rad/s}$ |
| measured at $10\ \text{Hz}$ | $0.0066\ \text{rad/s}$ | $0.0067\ \text{rad/s}$ |
| measured at $4\ \text{Hz}$ | $0.0059\ \text{rad/s}$ | $0.0055\ \text{rad/s}$ |
| cost per measurement at $100\ \text{Hz}$ | $37\ \mu\text{s}$ | $61\ \mu\text{s}$ |

At $100\ \text{Hz}$ the two filters agree to four digits. Only when the measurements become
sparse does the belief grow between them, and the UKF starts to help a little.

The hardest case is the start-up at $2\ \text{Hz}$: half a second of prediction from a belief
that still barely knows the velocity. Started with the right velocity, $\dot\theta_0 = 0$ (the
pendulum was released from rest), the EKF does well ($0.0061\ \text{rad/s}$) and the UKF fails
($0.21\ \text{rad/s}$). That comparison is unfair to the UKF: the EKF follows the single trajectory
through its prior mean, which here happens to be the true one, while the UKF averages over
all the trajectories its prior allows, and with $\sigma_{\dot\theta} = 2\ \text{rad/s}$ at
$120^\circ$ some of them go over the top. Started with a wrong velocity, as would happen in
practice, the picture reverses:

| 12 starts with a wrong velocity ($\pm 0.5$, $\pm 1$, $\pm 1.5\ \text{rad/s}$; $\sigma_{\dot\theta} = 1$ or $2\ \text{rad/s}$) | EKF failed | UKF failed |
|---|---|---|
| measured at $10\ \text{Hz}$ | 0 | 0 |
| measured at $4\ \text{Hz}$ | 0 | 0 |
| measured at $2\ \text{Hz}$ | 8 | 4 |

A failure here means the gate locks the filter out, as in section 5.4. The UKF fails half as
often, but it still fails: when the belief straddles the separatrix, part of it swings back and
part goes over the top, and the true belief has two separate lumps. No Gaussian filter,
extended or unscented, can represent that.

The same holds for the parameters of section 5.6: with an unscented prediction the augmented
filter ends at $\omega_0^2 = 9.8102 \pm 0.0013$ (EKF: $9.8093 \pm 0.0013$, true $9.81$), with the
true parameters within $2\sigma$ all the time instead of 97% to 100%, at about twice the cost.
A small gain in honesty, no gain in accuracy.

### What we learned

- The UKF approximates the belief by sigma points instead of approximating the model by a
  line; it needs no Jacobian, which matters for models where one is hard to write down.
- It predicts the mean of a wide belief correctly where the EKF is biased, but neither can
  represent a belief that is not shaped like an ellipse.
- For this pendulum, measured at $100\ \text{Hz}$, the two filters are indistinguishable. The
  UKF only helps when the belief becomes wide, here between sparse measurements.

## 5.8 Looking back: the Rauch–Tung–Striebel smoother

Every estimate in this chapter so far used the past only, because a controller has nothing
else. After the experiment, the whole recording is available, and an estimate of the state at
time $t_k$ may also use what was measured after it. That is **smoothing**: the offline
counterpart of filtering, and the missing corner of the
[storyline](/projects/pendulum-lab/#the-storyline-one-problem-four-questions). The Savitzky–Golay derivative of
section 2.2 was a smoother without a model.

### A backward pass

The Rauch–Tung–Striebel (RTS) smoother needs no new model and no new measurements. It runs
the Kalman filter forward as before and stores its estimates and predictions. A second pass
then runs *backwards* in time, from the last sample to the first. At the last sample the
filter has already seen everything, so the smoothed estimate equals the filtered one. Going
back, each filtered estimate is corrected by how much the smoothed estimate one step later
differs from what the filter had predicted for it:

$$
G_k = P_k F_k^\top \left(P^-_{k+1}\right)^{-1},
$$

$$
\hat{\mathbf x}^s_k = \hat{\mathbf x}^f_k + G_k \left(\hat{\mathbf x}^s_{k+1} - \hat{\mathbf x}^-_{k+1}\right),
$$

$$
P^s_k = P_k + G_k \left(P^s_{k+1} - P^-_{k+1}\right) G_k^\top .
$$

The superscripts mark the filtered estimate ($f$, with covariance $P$), the filter's
prediction for the next sample ($-$) and the smoothed estimate ($s$). $F_k$ is the transition the filter used from
sample $k$ to $k + 1$; for the EKF, the linearization around the filtered estimate. The gain $G_k$ says how much of a surprise
about the next state should be blamed on the current one. The cost is one more pass over the
data, with no new model evaluations.

The biggest gain is at the start. The filter has to learn the velocity from the first
measurements, and its error starts above $1\ \text{rad/s}$. The smoother knows the velocity at
$t = 0$ from the measurements that followed, and is as accurate there as anywhere else. Once the
filter has settled, the future adds little: after $2\ \text{s}$ the velocity error only drops
from $0.0019$ to $0.0018\ \text{rad/s}$, because the dynamics already carry most of the past
information forward. The angle, measured directly, improves more: by 40%.

The smoother is 35 times more accurate than the Savitzky–Golay derivative of chapter 2, which
also used past and future samples, but no model. And it lands right on top of the simulation
of the output-error fit. That is no coincidence: with less and less process noise, the
smoother trusts the model more and more, and in the limit it *is* the output-error trajectory.

| process noise $q$ | $10^{-10}$ | $10^{-8}$ | $10^{-6}$ | $2.6 \cdot 10^{-6}$ (5.5) | $10^{-4}$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| smoother's distance to the output-error trajectory, $\dot\theta$ RMS | $0.00009$ | $0.00009$ | $0.00017$ | $0.00028$ | $0.0019$ |

This is the link between chapters 2 and 5 in numbers: **output error is the smoother of a
model without process noise.** Allow process noise, and the smoother may deviate from the
model's trajectory where the measurements insist.

One honest caveat: the smoother is slightly overconfident. Its mean NEES is $2.7$, and its
error stays within $\pm 2\sigma$ 92% of the time instead of 95%. The bottom panel shows why:
what is left of the error is the slow oscillation of section 5.5, caused by the identified
parameters being very slightly off. That is a model error, not noise, and no amount of
smoothing removes it. On the small swing the picture is the same: $0.029$ falls to
$0.0016\ \text{rad/s}$, against $0.0012\ \text{rad/s}$ for the output-error simulation.

### What we learned

- The RTS smoother is the offline state estimate: one forward Kalman pass, one backward
  correction, every estimate using all measurements.
- It removes the filter's start-up error and improves the directly measured angle by 40%.
  Where the filter has settled, the future adds little.
- Without process noise it becomes the output-error simulation of chapter 2: identification
  and state estimation are the same problem.

failures were as instructive as the successes: a wrong model makes a filter confident
rather than uncertain, a gate can lock a filter out, a small process noise leaves no margin
for model error, and an early outlier can ruin learned parameters for good.

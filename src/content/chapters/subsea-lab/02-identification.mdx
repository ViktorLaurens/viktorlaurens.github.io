Chapter 1 ended with a ranking: of the thirty-odd hydrodynamic coefficients, the horizontal
plane's quadratic drag and added mass set where the tip goes, yaw drag most of all, and the
roll and pitch coefficients hide behind the autopilot. pendulum-lab's chapter 9 ended with a
warning: random thrust steps barely excited the pitch drag, and a "gentle" run lost the added
mass altogether. This chapter puts the two together next to the structure, where a vehicle
cannot fly wild steps, and asks: how much identification quality is lost when the vehicle may
not leave a box, and can designing the experiment buy it back?

The code is in `src/subsea_lab/identification.py`; the
numbers come from
`scripts/evaluate_identification.py`.

## 2.1 What an identification run knows

The run reads the sensors of the mission at $100\ \text{Hz}$: the body velocities from the
DVL and the gyros, the attitude, the joint angles and rates, each with Gaussian noise
(1 cm/s, $0.002\ \text{rad/s}$, $0.3^\circ$, $0.1^\circ$, $0.02\ \text{rad/s}$). It knows
what it asked for: the wrench the compensating mixer of chapter 5 believes it delivers and the
joint torques. It also knows the thrusters' third-order lag, which the paper identified, so
the wrench the thrusters actually deliver follows from the command history; the lag's states
are part of the record, not of the unknowns. One thing it is given that a real vehicle would
have to estimate: the water's velocity, as a DVL's water track would give it. Chapter 3
estimates the current; here it is known, and the identification errors below are the floor
that knowing it leaves.

The unknowns are the twenty coefficients of chapter 1's sensitivity study: six added masses,
six linear and six quadratic drag coefficients of the vehicle, and the drag and added-mass
coefficients of the arm's links. They span three decades, so each is scaled by the order of
magnitude a designer knows from the vehicle's class (ten kilograms of added mass, a hundred
newtons of quadratic drag at one metre per second, one for the arm), and every error below is
quoted over that scale. Two estimators fit them, both inherited from chapter 9:

- **Regression.** The inverse dynamics of section 1.2 are affine in the coefficients,

  $$
  M(\boldsymbol\theta) \thinspace \dot{\boldsymbol\zeta} + \mathbf c(\boldsymbol\theta) - \mathbf f(\boldsymbol\theta) = \mathbf r_0 + \Phi \thinspace \boldsymbol\theta,
  $$

  so with the accelerations differenced from the measured velocities (a Savitzky–Golay
  filter over 0.11 s) the coefficients come from one linear least squares over the run. The
  compiled model of section 1.6 gives $\Phi$ exactly, as the Jacobian of the residual.
- **Output error.** The designer's model replays the recorded inputs from the measured state
  in windows of 1.5 s, and Gauss–Newton finds the coefficients that make its velocities match
  the measured ones,

  $$
  \hat{\boldsymbol\theta} = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol\theta} \sum_k \left( \mathbf y_k(\boldsymbol\theta) - \mathbf y_k \right)^\top \Sigma^{-1} \left( \mathbf y_k(\boldsymbol\theta) - \mathbf y_k \right),
  $$

  with $\mathbf y_k$ the ten velocities at every tenth sample and $\Sigma$ the sensor noise.
  Short windows matter: the textbook model, with too little drag and no righting in pitch,
  blows up inside a longer one before the fit can correct it. Each fit from the textbook model
  takes between half a minute and two minutes on the compiled twin.

Before any run is flown, its value is known. The sensitivities of the replayed outputs to the
coefficients, $S_k = \frac{\partial \mathbf y_k}{\partial \boldsymbol\theta}$, give the Fisher
information $J = \sum_k S_k^\top \Sigma^{-1} S_k$, and the **Cramér–Rao bound** says no
unbiased estimator can do better than $\sqrt{(J^{-1})_{ii}}$ on coefficient $i$. That number,
per coefficient and per run, is what the chapter compares, and $\log\det J$ is what the
**experiment design** maximises: the thrust levels of ten 1.5-second intervals in surge, sway,
heave and yaw, under a roll-and-pitch autopilot and with the arm swung to the side and back
as in chapter 1, chosen by Powell's method with a penalty for every metre the run leaves its
box. The box is the safety constraint of a vehicle next to a structure: in open water, two
metres each way; next to the structure, half a metre to each side, 30 cm toward the wall and
30 cm in depth, and $20^\circ$ of tilt either way. Next to the structure the programme can be
flown in two ways: as open-loop thrust steps, or as perturbations added to the wrench of
chapter 5's station-keeping LQR, which holds the box while the steps excite; both are
designed, each in 250 evaluations of about twenty minutes.

| excitation | box | farthest from the start | heading swept | $\log\det J$ |

What it shows:

- **Open water rewards design, a little.** The random steps of chapter 1 roam over three
  metres and would need no box at all; the programme designed inside two metres is more
  informative (227 against 224 nats) while never leaving them. It does so by spending its
  thrust early and letting the vehicle coast back through the current in the second half.
- **Next to the structure, open loop is not an option.** No programme of thrust steps holds
  a half-metre box for fifteen seconds in a 0.2 m/s current with gusts; the best the design
  finds drifts a metre downstream and 45 cm out of the box. The experiment that is allowed is
  the closed-loop one: the station-keeping controller holds the vehicle within 30 cm and the
  designed perturbations ride on top.
- **The box costs eleven to fourteen nats**, out of 227, and the two confined designs are
  worth the same (213) whether or not one of them cheats on the box. Spread over twenty
  coefficients that is a factor of about two in each bound, not an order of magnitude; the
  next section says where it lands.

## 2.3 What the fits actually get

Each excitation is flown once and measured three times with different sensor noise; both
estimators fit each record, the output-error fit starting from the textbook model of
chapter 1.

Right, the error the fits made, root mean square over three noise realisations: output error as filled markers, a few hundredths and five to twenty times the bound; regression as hollow markers, around a half for the added masses and above one for several drags">

| $Y_{\dot v}$ sway added mass | 0.001 | 0.003 | 0.004 | 0.029 | 0.54 |
| $X_{u\lvert u \rvert}$ surge drag | 0.005 | 0.009 | 0.023 | 0.049 | 0.17 |
| $Y_{v\lvert v \rvert}$ sway drag | 0.008 | 0.017 | 0.053 | 0.038 | 0.13 |
| $N_{r\lvert r \rvert}$ yaw drag | 0.006 | 0.012 | 0.007 | 0.040 | 0.13 |

| $C_{d,\mathrm{arm}}$ arm drag | 0.002 | 0.003 | 0.006 | 0.010 | 0.005 |
| $C_{a,\mathrm{arm}}$ arm added mass | 0.004 | 0.006 | 0.037 | 0.026 | 0.43 |

What it shows:

- **The box roughly doubles the bound on what matters.** Surge and sway added mass go from
  a thousandth of their scale to three; the quadratic drags of the horizontal plane from
  half a percent to one or two. Every coefficient chapter 1 ranked as important is still
  bounded within 2% of its scale from inside a half-metre box.
- **Output error gets within a few percent; regression does not.** Fitted by output error,
  the open-water design pins the added masses to 0.4% and the drags to 1 to 5%; the
  station-kept run to 3% and 4 to 5%. Regression, on the same records, is off by half on
  every added mass and by 13 to 17% on the drags, with or without noise: the accelerations it
  differences miss the first tenth of a second of every thrust step, and that is exactly
  where the added mass shows. Chapter 9 saw the same with a much cheaper model.
- **Nobody reaches the bound.** The output-error errors sit five to twenty bounds above the
  Cramér–Rao floor. The bound assumes an unbiased estimator that knows where each window
  starts; the fits start their windows from smoothed, noisy measurements and stop after
  thirty evaluations. The gap is a cost of the method, not of the experiment, and it is the
  same for every excitation.
- **Pitch is the one place the structure helps.** The station-kept run bounds the quadratic
  pitch drag at 0.025 where the open-water runs allow 0.08 to 0.3, and fits it to 15% where
  the others get 30 to 50%: the LQR holds pitch more loosely than the autopilot's stiff PD,
  so the pitch actually moves. Chapter 1's warning stands in general (an axis that is held
  cannot be identified) and this is the exception that proves it.

## 2.4 Predicting the mission

The test that matters is not the run the coefficients were fitted on but the mission they will
serve. Chapter 5's mission is flown on the true plant in a different current, and each
identified model replays it in 3 s windows from the true state: the arm reaching, the grasp,
the lift, the return with the object (which no model knows and all carry as the same payload
term). The arm's joints are taken from the record, because they are servoed and measured and
because replaying their recorded torques open loop makes an arm holding an object a pendulum
balanced on a constant torque, which runs away within a window for any model but the true one;
the models are judged on where they put the vehicle, and the tip with it.

Right, the velocity error after up to three seconds of prediction over the mission's sixty-two seconds, one output-error fit per excitation against the textbook model and the truth, with the approach, reach, lift and return shaded: the fitted models stay between a hundredth and half a centimetre per second, rising a little while the object is carried; the textbook model is at ten">

velocity error along it (right)

|---|---|---|---|

| output error, station kept | 0.16 to 0.40 cm/s | 0.2 to 0.6 cm | 0.6 to 1.6 cm |
| true coefficients | 0 | 0 | 0 |

What it shows:

- **Any fit beats the textbook by two orders of magnitude.** The nominal model of chapter 1
  predicts the vehicle's velocity 23 cm/s wrong after three seconds and the tip 36 cm; every
  identified model is within half a centimetre per second and six millimetres.
- **The confined experiment costs a factor of two to four on the mission**, not more. Fitted
  from the station-kept run, the model predicts the mission's velocities within 0.2 to
  0.4 cm/s and the tip within 2 to 6 mm, against 0.1 cm/s and 1.3 mm from the random steps
  flown in open water. For comparison, the acoustic fix the mission's estimator will live on
  is 5 cm.
- **Regression is good enough for the mission, by this measure.** Half-wrong added masses
  cost 1.5 cm/s and 2 cm of tip over three seconds at the mission's speeds, where drag and
  gravity dominate and the accelerations are small. On the station-kept run its errors are
  smaller still, because the gentler run differences better. Chapter 9's conclusion returns:
  identification matters most for the fast transients, and the mission has few.
- **The object is where the error grows.** For every fit the velocity error rises while the
  object is carried back (38 to 58 s), by a factor of two to three: the drag and the added
  mass of the payload are modelled, but the arm's own drag at the carrying pose, with the
  flow at 0.35 m/s, exposes the arm coefficients' errors more than the rest of the mission.
  That is chapter 8's problem.

## 2.5 What no allowed experiment can know

Some coefficients stay uncertain whatever the run, and the chapter is obliged to say which:

- **The pitch and roll drags.** With roll and pitch held, their quadratic drags are bounded
  at 2 to 30% and fitted to 7 to 50% of their scale. The mission holds them too, so it does
  not care; a controller that lets the vehicle pitch, such as a reach that uses the body, will
  have to tolerate a factor-of-two error in how fast the pitch stops.
- **The linear drags.** Where the paper gives zero (sway, roll, yaw) the fits return 0 to
  25% of scale; where it gives a value (surge, heave) the heave term is 10 to 45% off: at
  the speeds of these runs the quadratic term dominates and the two trade off. Chapter 1's
  sensitivity study said the linear terms were worth a tenth of the quadratic ones, and the
  mission prediction confirms that the error is cheap.
- **The pitch coefficients from an open-loop run**, and the yaw coefficients from a run that
  does not turn: the station-kept run turns through $21^\circ$ and still bounds the yaw drag
  at 1.2% because the LQR's corrections excite yaw continuously; a run that held its heading
  as stiffly as the PD held pitch would not.

What later chapters must therefore tolerate: coefficients of the horizontal plane within a
few percent, the arm's within a few percent, the vertical plane's within ten, roll and pitch
drag within a factor of two, and nothing at all about the object until chapter 8.

## Chapter summary

| | topic | what it showed |
|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | the run | twenty scaled coefficients, two estimators, a bound per run before it is flown, and a design that maximises it inside a box |
| 2.2 | four excitations | design beats random steps in open water; open-loop steps cannot hold a half-metre box in the current; perturbing a station-keeping controller can, and the box costs a factor of two in the bounds |
| 2.3 | the fits | output error within a few percent, five to twenty bounds above the floor; regression biased by half on the added masses; pitch identified only where it is allowed to move |
| 2.4 | the mission | every fit predicts the mission a hundred times better than the textbook; the confined run costs a factor of two to four; the carried object is where the error grows |
| 2.5 | what cannot be known | roll and pitch drag within a factor of two, linear drag poorly, the object not at all |

Three threads continue:

- **Safety is the experiment.** The only identification run allowed next to the structure
  is a closed-loop one: the controller holds the box, the excitation rides on it, and the
  controller's own corrections turn out to excite what the autopilot used to hide. Chapter 6
  will set the box from a barrier rather than by hand.
- **The bound is known before the run.** Each programme's Fisher information was computed on
  the designer's model before it was flown, and the fits landed a fixed factor above it. The
  online version (excite only when the posterior's uncertainty hurts the task) is left open,
  as the plan said it might be; it needs chapter 5's NMPC in the loop.
- **The current was given.** Every number here assumes the water's velocity known. Chapter 3
  estimates it, and the identification errors that estimate adds are the next floor.

What we learned:

- A vehicle next to a structure cannot fly an open-loop identification programme in a
  current; the experiment that is allowed perturbs a station-keeping controller, and it
  costs about a factor of two in every coefficient's bound and two to four in how well the
  mission is predicted.
- Experiment design buys back less than the box takes: a few nats in open water, nothing
  against the current without feedback.
- Output error reaches a few percent on the coefficients the mission cares about; regression
  is biased by half on the added masses by the thrust steps it cannot resolve, and still
  predicts the mission within 2 cm.
- The axes an autopilot holds cannot be identified, except where a looser controller lets
  them move, which the station-kept run did for pitch.

The mission has flown since the first pass of this chapter: a station LQR with integral
action moved the vehicle, the arm's own servos moved the arm, and a schedule said when. That
controller carried chapters 2 to 8 and it was never the question. The question is that the
system has ten degrees of freedom for a five-dimensional task, and someone has to decide
whether the vehicle or the arm does the work. Three controllers decide it differently: a
stock autopilot that does not decide at all, a kinematic task-priority scheme that decides by
a price, and a whole-body optimizer that decides by a cost. And the grasp, which every
chapter so far took as a change in the plant's equations, becomes a contact: MuJoCo closes
the jaws on an object with mass, buoyancy, drag and added mass that no model of the plant
knows, and the mission carries whatever the fingers hold.

The code is in `src/subsea_lab/wholebody.py` and
`src/subsea_lab/contact.py`; the numbers come from
`scripts/evaluate_wholebody.py`.

## 5.1 Three ways to move ten joints

Every controller is given the same task: the tip's position along the mission, the arm's
plan on the station reference (stowed during the approach, to the handle in three seconds
of reach, up by 20 cm after the grasp, to the carrying pose on the way back), with the
thrusters' limits, the joints' limits and rates, and the keep-out zone around the structure
to respect. They differ in what they make of the ten degrees of freedom.

**The autopilot.** A PID on each of the vehicle's six axes, no model, the error integrated
against the current; the arm's servos follow the plan's joint angles. The vehicle holds
station and the arm reaches; nobody weighs one against the other, and the vehicle learns of
the object only through its integral.

**Set-based task priority.** A kinematic scheme in the family Pettersen's group works on
(Moe, Antonelli, Pettersen and Teel, 2016): the tasks are ordered, the lower ones live in
the null space of the higher ones, and the set-based tasks, the joint limits and the
keep-out zone, are inequalities that become equalities with zero rate only when the solution
would push them out of their set. The tip's position is the task; the vehicle's station is
the last priority, where it fits. The pseudo-inverses are weighted: a unit of vehicle
velocity costs `vehicle_weight` units of joint rate, and that one number is the decision of
who moves. The scheme resolves, twenty times a second, a generalized velocity for the
reference configuration (not the measured one, or the vehicle's error would move the
reference and the integral action would be lost), and chapter 5's LQR and the servos track
what it integrates. Two things it needed that the textbook version does not say: the
pseudo-inverses of the equality tasks are damped, because an elbow nearly straight makes the
shoulder's and the elbow's columns almost parallel and an undamped null-space step asked for
$24\ \text{rad/s}$; and the set-based tasks are not damped, because a damped zero rate leaks into the
set at millimetres per second.

**The whole-body NMPC.** The optimizer of chapter 7 extended to the arm: the vehicle's
thirteen states and the four joint angles, with the wrench and the joint rates as the
inputs, eight intervals of 0.1 s, the tip's distance from its plan in the cost with the
attitude, the velocities, the station (weakly) and the inputs, the thrusters' limits and the
joint rates as bounds, the joint limits as state bounds, and the keep-out zone and the depth
band as constraints at every node. The servos track the joint angles of the plan's next
node. ipopt from the shifted previous plan, a failed solve holding the last inputs; its
solve time is measured, as the plan asked, against the 100 ms budget.

**What it shows.** Before the grasp the four are hard to tell apart on the tip, and the
numbers rank them: the NMPC holds the plan to 0.1 cm rms, the LQR and the task-priority
scheme to 0.4, the autopilot to 1.5, and the time from the reach's start to the tip within
1 cm of the handle is 2.5 to 2.6 s for three of them and 5.3 s for the autopilot, whose
integral has to find the arm's weight. The grasp sorts them. The LQR and the task-priority
scheme, both with integral action on the vehicle, take the 7 N in their stride: 3.7 and 3.9 cm
of tip error after the lift, $4.6^\circ$ of pitch, back within 2 cm in 4.4 and 4.7 s. The autopilot
pitches $11^\circ$, misses the lifted target by 10 cm and never gets back within 2 cm; its return
is a 6 cm rms wander. The NMPC, with no object in its model and no integral action, does
what chapters 7 and 9 said it would: 7.4 cm after the lift, never within 2 cm again, and on
the return, where it must move the vehicle 3 m with a weight it does not know, it pitches
the vehicle to $22^\circ$. It is also the only one that lets the vehicle wander from the
station while the tip is on plan: 16 cm off during the station phase, because the station
is in its cost at a low weight and the tip at a high one, and the only one that uses the
keep-out margin: 13 cm at closest, against 24 to 26 for the others, which never ask. On
energy the task-priority scheme is cheapest in thrust (12045 against 12935 for the LQR and
13037 for the autopilot, in the $\sum \lvert F \rvert^{1.5}$ measure of a T200's power)
and spends it on the joints instead (8.7 J against 5.7); the NMPC is cheapest of all in thrust
(11396) and spends most on the joints (14.4 J), and chatters least (2.7 sign changes of a
thruster command per second against 5.1 for the LQR, whose compensating mixer jumps the
dead band on every small request).

**The solve time.** 2.1 s per solve on average, 5.0 s at worst, over 621 solves with none
failed, on a laptop running two such flights at once. The budget is 100 ms. The plan said
this measurement decides the language: it does. Twenty times over, with the exact Hessian
of a 17-state model through two RK4 substeps and eight intervals, is not a tuning problem;
it is a generated-code problem, and real-time iteration with a compiled solver is the
answer the chapter leaves for the gallery. What the number buys here is honesty about the
whole-body NMPC's place in this report: a design tool and a comparison, not the controller
that flies the mission.

## 5.2 Who should move

The scheme's one knob is the price of vehicle motion against joint motion. On the mission as
flown the knob does nothing, because the handle is inside the arm's reach from the station
and the station task, last in priority, keeps the vehicle still whatever the price: the arm
does the whole reach for any weight from 1 to 100. To make the question real the station
task is put 20 cm further from the wall than the plan the tip follows, so that the handle is
beyond the arm's reach from where the vehicle is asked to stand, and someone has to move.

Middle, the thrusters' energy near 15.5 at every price and the joints' energy at 8.0, 8.0 and 8.5 joules. Right, the tip's error while holding at about 1 centimetre and the least keep-out margin at 32 centimetres, both flat across the prices">

**What it shows.** Nothing moves with the price. At every weight the vehicle walks 0.28 to
0.29 m forward and the joints travel 2.24 rad, the thrusters spend the same, the tip is held
to half a centimetre, the margin is 32 cm. The reason is the geometry: with the handle 0.68 m
from the arm's mount and the arm 0.35 m long at full stretch, the arm stretches to its
limits (the set-based tasks activate) and the vehicle must supply the rest; the price can
only trade within the redundancy that is left, and with four joints against a task that
uses the arm's reach, there is none. Who moves is decided by the reach, and the weighting
decides at most how fast. The scheme pays for the standoff in other ways: the stretched arm
lifts the object with 6.3 cm of error against 4 cm from the station, and the tip's lift
takes the elbow through its singular stretch, where the damping slows it.

## 5.3 The grasp as a contact

The jaws are two fingers on slide joints, driven closed by position actuators, on a body
that follows the plant's tip frame through a stiff weld (a mocap body would do, except that
it has no velocity, and contacts with fingers that teleport never slide). The object is a
vertical post to grip, 2.4 cm across, on a body with the mission's mass and volume, its
buoyancy applied as a force, its drag and added mass from MuJoCo's own ellipsoid fluid
model, held to the structure by a weld that is released when both fingers touch it. Every
plant step the jaws are moved to the plant's tip, MuJoCo steps ten times, and the force the
object puts on the fingers comes back to the plant as the force at its jaws. The plant's
equations have no payload term any more; what the vehicle carries is what the contact holds.

**What it shows.** The fingers open 5 cm and the post is 2.4 cm across, so a lateral offset
of 2 cm still closes on it and 2.5 cm does not; ahead of the handle the fingers' 6 cm of
depth forgive 2 cm, and at 3 cm they knock the object off its hold before closing and it
sinks away; along the post's 8 cm of height any offset up to 6 cm holds. Within the window
the grasp is not free: closing 2 cm off centre drags the object into the fingers and the
peak force on the tip rises from 16 N, the object's weight in water plus the closing
impulse, to 84 N, and a 4 cm vertical offset to 100 N, a jolt the tip-force state of chapter
3's filter would see as a fault. Chapter 4 put the tip at the handle to 1 cm with the camera
and 4.4 cm without; the contact says 1 cm is a grasp and 4.4 cm, sideways, is not.

**The mission on the contact.** With the grasp a contact, the LQR carries the object to the
end: held at every sample, 8.7 N on the tip (the object as built, post included, is 8.7 N in
water), a peak of 17.6 N at the closing, and a ringing of $\pm 1.5\ \text{N}$ between 34
and 40 s while the arm's servo integral catches the weight through the compliant wrist, then
a flat line. The cost of the real object against the payload change: 6.2 cm of tip error
after the lift instead of 3.7, 5.1 s to recover instead of 4.4, $5.0^\circ$ of pitch instead of
4.6, and 9.4 dead-band sign changes a second instead of 5.1, the thrusters answering the
ringing. The NMPC holds the object too, with no failed solve, and pitches $27^\circ$ on the
return instead of 22 with the payload change, never recovering to 2 cm: the contact does not
change the verdict, it sharpens it. And one thing the contact taught that no payload change
could: a rigid coupling between the jaws and the plant, with forces exchanged every ten
milliseconds, rang and shook the object out of the fingers two seconds after the grasp,
under any plan; a wrist with 50 ms of compliance holds it through the whole mission. The
model of the grasp is also a model of the arm's compliance, and a stiff one drops things.

What we learned:

- With ten degrees of freedom and a plan, the controller that decides nothing (the LQR
  with servos) is as good as the one that decides by priority, and both beat the one that
  decides by optimization, because what the grasp needs is integral action and the
  optimizer has none. Chapter 9's question has the same answer in three dimensions with a
  real contact: integral action rides out what the certainty-equivalent NMPC cannot.
- Who should move is a question the geometry answers before the controller does. A four-
  joint arm against a task that uses its reach leaves no redundancy for a price to trade;
  the set-based scheme's value here is not the choice but the guarantee: the joint limits
  and the keep-out zone are never crossed, by construction.
- The whole-body NMPC's solve time, 2.1 s against 100 ms, decides the language: the
  optimizer stays a design and comparison tool in Python, and a real-time controller is a
  generated-code problem the gallery must take up.
- A grasp has a window, and chapter 4's tip floor sits inside it with the camera and outside
  it without. Closing off centre within the window is not free: the object is dragged into
  the fingers with ten times its weight on the tip. The first thing the contact teaches is
  that the grasp is an impact.
- A model of the grasp is also a model of the wrist. The contact held nothing until the
  coupling between the jaws and the plant was given fifty milliseconds of compliance; with
  it, the object rides through the mission on friction alone, and the mission flown on the
  real object costs two centimetres and a second more than the one flown on a payload
  change. The payload change of chapters 2 to 8 was a fair stand-in for the carry, and no
  stand-in at all for the closing.

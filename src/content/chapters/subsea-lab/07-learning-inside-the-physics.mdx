pendulum-lab's chapter 9 ended on a warning. Its NMPC was the most capable controller on the
planar vehicle and the only one that failed after the grasp, and when a residual network was
put inside it to learn what the lumped model missed, the controller drove the vehicle 85 to
115 cm off: nothing constrained the network where the optimizer looked, which was off the
training trajectory. This chapter asks the third of the report's three questions, what may be
learned inside the physics. The answer it builds is a learned model that cannot create energy,
because its structure forbids it, and the question it puts to that model is whether the
structure costs accuracy, whether it buys anything an identified model does not already have,
and whether it survives inside an optimizer where the free network did not.

The code is in `src/subsea_lab/learned.py` and
`src/subsea_lab/nmpc.py`; the numbers come from
`scripts/evaluate_learning.py`.

## 7.1 What is left to learn

Chapter 2 identified the vehicle to within a few percent on every coefficient the tip is
sensitive to. What it could not identify is what is not in the model: the object. After the
grasp the designer's model (chapter 2's station-kept output-error fit) is wrong by the
object's weight in water, its mass and added mass at the jaws, and its drag, and it is wrong
in a way that depends on the arm's configuration, because a weight at the jaws is a pitch
moment on the vehicle whose arm is the lever. The **residual** is defined on the generalized
coordinates of chapter 1, the vehicle's twist and the four joint rates:

$$
\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{M}(\mathbf{q})\dot{\boldsymbol\zeta} + \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q}, \boldsymbol\zeta) - \mathbf{f}(\mathbf{q}, \boldsymbol\zeta, \mathbf{u}),
$$

the force the designer's model would have needed to reproduce the plant's acceleration, with
the thrust from the delivered wrench (the thruster lag of chapter 2 reconstructed from the
command history). It is computed on the true plant carrying the mission's object, 1 kg and
0.3 L (6.87 N in water), from the grasp at 32 s to the end at 62 s, one sample every tenth of
a second: 301 samples. Before the grasp the residual is the lumping error of chapter 2, a
hundred times smaller, and the models below are not asked about it; the learned part is the
object's model, switched on by the grasp the mission knows.

The residual is not small. Its heave component is 6.85 N rms, its pitch moment 2.5 N m, its
shoulder torque 1.26 N m; the forward force, 0.3 N, and the roll moment, 0.34 N, are what the
object's drag and the arm's lever do when the vehicle moves. The identified model carries none
of it, which is the controller's problem in section 7.4.

## 7.2 Four models of it

Four things are fitted to the same 301 samples, each with the features the plant exposes
(the joint angles, the ten generalized velocities, and the direction of gravity in the body
frame, seventeen numbers), and each exported to CasADi so that it can sit inside an optimizer.

**Three numbers.** The physics of an object at the jaws is a weight $w$ along gravity, a mass
$m$ that resists the tip's acceleration, and a quadratic drag $d$ against the tip's velocity,
each mapped to the generalized coordinates by the tip's Jacobian $\mathbf{J}$:

$$
\mathbf r_{\text{phys}} = w\ \mathbf{J}^\top \mathbf e_\downarrow - m\ \mathbf{J}^\top \mathbf a_{\text{tip}} - d\ \mathbf{J}^\top \lVert \mathbf v_{\text{tip}} \rVert \mathbf v_{\text{tip}}.
$$

This is linear in the three numbers and is fitted by least squares in a tenth of a second.
It is chapter 2's way of thinking carried to the object, and it is the baseline every learned
model must beat. On the training flight it leaves 3.6% of the residual unexplained; it finds
the weight at 6.86 N (true 6.87) and the mass at 1.15 kg (the object plus the water it drags).

**A free network.** A two-layer network of 64 units on the standardised features, ten
outputs, the residual of chapter 9 reproduced: 3000 epochs of Adam, 3 s. Nothing constrains it
anywhere.

**A passive model.** The three numbers, held at their least-squares values, plus the two
things the physics says an unknown body in water can add and nothing else: an **added mass**
on the vehicle, constant, symmetric and positive semidefinite by construction
($\mathbf M_a = \mathbf L_m \mathbf L_m^\top$, with Fossen's skew-symmetric Coriolis
derived from it, so that it stores kinetic energy and returns it), a diagonal added inertia
on the joints, and a **dissipation** that is positive semidefinite at every state because it
is built as a square, $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{L}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{L}(\mathbf{x})^\top$,
with $\mathbf{L}$ the lower-triangular output of a small network of 32 units (55 numbers).
The force is

$$
\mathbf r_{\text{passive}} = \mathbf r_{\text{phys}} - \mathbf M_a \dot{\boldsymbol\zeta} - \mathbf C_a(\boldsymbol\zeta)\boldsymbol\zeta - \mathbf{D}(\mathbf{x})\boldsymbol\zeta ,
$$

and the network, the factor $\mathbf L_m$ and the joint inertias are trained by Adam on what
the three numbers leave, 7 s. The model is **passive by construction**: with the storage
function $H = \tfrac12 \boldsymbol\zeta^\top(\mathbf{M} + \mathbf M_a)\boldsymbol\zeta + w z_{\text{tip}}$,
the weight is the gradient of a potential, the added mass and its Coriolis term do no net
work, and the drag and the dissipation can only take energy out, so along any motion
$\dot H \le \boldsymbol\zeta^\top \boldsymbol\tau$: the learned part cannot put energy in.
This is the property chapter 9's network lacked, and the one section 7.3 tests.

**A sparse Gaussian process.** A squared-exponential kernel on the standardised features,
200 inducing points, one output per coordinate, with the variance it reports away from its
data. Its prior mean is zero, so far from the data it predicts nothing at all, and says so.

**What it shows.** On the training flight the free network fits best (0.8% of the residual
left), the passive model next (1.2%), the three numbers 3.6%, the GP 16%. In another current,
with the same object, all three physics-bearing models are at 3 to 4% and the three numbers
alone, at 2.8%, are the best of them: whatever the networks learned beyond the three numbers
was the flight, not the object. The GP is at 23%.

Then the object changes. A 2 kg object never seen (13.7 N in water) leaves every model
predicting the 1 kg one, 51% wrong for all of them, and a 0.3 kg object leaves them predicting
a residual two and a half times the real one. No feature says what is in the jaws, so nothing
trained on one object can know; this is chapter 8's question, and here only the mechanism
matters: what each model can do with the first two seconds after a new grasp. The three
numbers re-fit by least squares on twenty samples and land at 5% on both objects. The passive
model re-fits the same three numbers and keeps its network: 7% on the heavier object, 18% on
the lighter one, worse than the numbers alone, because what its network learned on the 1 kg
flight was partly that object. The free network fine-tuned on the same twenty samples goes to
34% on the heavier object and makes the lighter one worse (158%). The GP has no mechanism and
stays where it was.

**The energy balance.** The property the structure was built for is tested where an optimizer
would test it: at a thousand random states drawn around the mission's (velocities to twice
the mission's, every joint angle, every attitude), not on any trajectory. The power the
learned part delivers, $\boldsymbol\zeta^\top \mathbf{r}$, is positive at half of them for
the free network, up to 30 W, a tenth of the thrusters' total; the plant would be fed energy
from nothing there, which is what an optimizer looking for a cheap way to the reference will
find. The passive model's dissipative part is negative at every one of the thousand states
(the most it ever gives back is zero), as the construction says it must; on the mission's
flights it takes out 0.05 W on average. The GP's power is exactly zero at every random state:
its prior mean takes over a few length scales from the data and the model vanishes, which is
safe in the sense that nothing is invented, and useless in the sense that nothing is known,
and its variance says which. That is the three answers to chapter 9's question in one figure:
a free network invents energy where it has not been, a GP invents nothing and knows nothing,
and a passive model cannot invent energy anywhere, by construction and not by training.

## 7.4 Inside the optimizer

Chapter 9's NMPC failed after the grasp because its model did not know the object; here the
same question is put with each model inside. The controller is a vehicle NMPC on the CasADi
twin: thirteen states (position, attitude, twist), the arm's four joints as known inputs over
the horizon (the mission's targets, which the servos of chapter 5 follow), eight intervals of
0.1 s by multiple shooting, the tip's distance from its reference in the cost with the
attitude, the velocities and the thrust, the thrusters' limits as bounds, solved by ipopt
from the shifted previous plan. The identified model has no residual; each learned model's
vehicle rows sit inside the integrator (the passive one's added mass on the left-hand side).
The plant carries the object from the grasp state of chapter 5's flight through the lift and
the return, 30 s, with the mission's current; the controller sees the true state. Every
model is as trained on the 1 kg object; the 2 kg case is the object none of them knows.

A solve that does not converge holds the last wrench and leaves its iterate as the next warm
start. That policy is part of the result, because it was not the first one tried. With an
iteration limit of 25 and the unconverged iterate applied anyway, cold-restarting after it,
the free network survived on unconverged plans (110 failed solves of 301, $22^\circ$ of pitch) and
the passive model pitched the vehicle to $75^\circ$; with the limit of 25 and the wrench held, both
fell away from the first solve (every solve failed, the vehicle 1.5 to 4.5 m off), because
the first cold solve of a model with a network inside needs 45 iterations where the three
numbers need 14. The runs below use a limit of 100.

**What it shows.** Carrying the object it was trained on, the model that knows it is what
matters, and how it knows it does not: the three numbers and the passive model hold the tip
to 2.9 cm rms (14.4 cm at the grasp's instant, before any controller can react) and the
pitch to $2^\circ$, and finish on the reference to a millimetre; the identified model, with no
residual, lets the grasp pitch the vehicle $17.6^\circ$ and holds the tip to 3.9 cm with a standing
1.7 cm error, the object's weight that an NMPC without integral action never learns; the
GP, whose prediction vanishes a few length scales from its data, does exactly what the
identified model does, to a tenth of a millimetre. The free network, the most accurate model
on the training flight, is the worst controller: 6.0 cm rms, 40 cm at the grasp, $31^\circ$ of
pitch, eight solves that did not converge. It is chapter 9's result reproduced in three
dimensions, milder because the wrench is bounded and the attitude is in the cost: the
optimizer finds the states where the network is wrong, and they are near the plan.

The passive model's solves cost three times the three numbers' (1.8 s against 0.7 s per
solve on this laptop, under load; 3.4 s for the free network, 0.6 s for the identified
model), the price of a network and an implicit mass inside the integrator and its exact
Hessian. None of the five meets the 100 ms budget of a $10\ \text{Hz}$ loop; the three numbers are
seven times over it, the networks twenty to thirty. Real-time iteration or a compiled
solver would close most of that gap for the models without a network; the chapter reports
the ipopt numbers because they are the ones measured.

With the 2 kg object, which no model knows, every controller is wrong by the same 7 N of
weight, and none is good: 5.3 cm rms and $16^\circ$ for the three numbers (still the best, because
a model that is wrong by a scalar is wrong smoothly), 8.5 cm and $27^\circ$ for the identified
model, 8.6 cm and $43^\circ$ for the free network, and 8.8 cm and $50^\circ$ of pitch for the passive
model, with ten solves that did not converge. The certificate held, in the sense that the
model did not invent energy; it did not hold the vehicle, because passivity is a statement
about the model's energy, not about its accuracy, and the dissipation its network learned on
the 1 kg flight is as wrong at the 2 kg states as any other extrapolation. What the structure
offers here is the adaptation of section 7.3, three numbers from two seconds, which chapter 8
puts inside this controller.

What we learned:

- After a good identification the residual is physics with three unknown numbers, and the
  numbers beat the networks everywhere but on the flight they were trained on. Learning
  earned nothing here that least squares did not; what the structure earned is a certificate.
- Passivity by construction holds where it was never trained: a thousand random states, zero
  violations, at no measurable cost in accuracy on the data. A free network of the same size
  creates energy at half of them. The certificate is the model's, not the training's, and it
  is what an optimizer needs.
- Inside the optimizer the certificate is necessary and not sufficient. The passive model
  flies the retrieval as well as the physics it contains; the free network, more accurate
  on its data, is the worst controller of the five. But a passive model that is wrong is
  still wrong, and when the object changes the structure does not save the flight; what
  saves it is knowing the object, and the way to know it is three numbers.
- The failure policy of the NMPC decided the outcome as much as the model did, and an
  optimizer with a network inside needs three times the iterations and the time. Those are
  results of this chapter, not footnotes to it.
- Nothing trained on one object knows the next one. Adaptation is where structure pays: three
  numbers re-fit in two seconds, a network of thousands cannot, and a GP has no handle at all.
  That is chapter 8.

Chapter 7 ended on the object. Nothing trained on one object knows the next; the three
numbers that explain the residual can be re-fitted in two seconds; a network of thousands
cannot. That was shown offline, on two seconds of data, with the true accelerations. This
chapter does it the way a vehicle would have to: while carrying the object, ten times a
second, from the raw sensors, with the controller waiting for the answer. It is the
learning-versus-physics debate the PhD call sits in, with three kinds of adaptation on one
clock, and the question is what each gets right, how fast, with how much data, and how it
fails on an object outside anything it was prepared for.

The code is in `src/subsea_lab/adaptation.py`; the numbers
come from `scripts/evaluate_adaptation.py`.

## 8.1 The stream

The adapters never see the truth. After the grasp, at $100\ \text{Hz}$, they see chapter 2's sensors:
the velocities from the DVL at 1 cm/s of noise, the rates from the gyros, the attitude at
$0.3^\circ$, the joint angles and rates from the encoders; and they know the wrench the mixer
believed it delivered, lagged through the identified thruster dynamics as chapter 2 did.
Every tenth of a second one **sample** is made of the last ten measurements: the state at the
instant with its velocities averaged over the last five, the acceleration as the difference
of the two half-window means, and the residual of chapter 7, the force the designer's model
cannot explain, computed from them. The accelerations are the price: a centimetre per second
of velocity noise differenced over 50 ms is $0.2\ \text{m/s}^2$ of acceleration noise, and through the
vehicle's mass that is 3 N in surge and sway and 4.3 N in heave, sample to sample, on a
residual whose true heave is 6.9 N. Chapter 2 measured this cost offline (regression biased
by half on the added masses); here it is what every adapter has to live with.

The objects: a **training distribution** of 24, with mass uniform in 0.2 to 1.6 kg and volume
uniform in 0.05 to 0.5 L, so weights in water from 0.8 to 14.5 N, each flown through the
mission once by chapter 5's station controller with its own current and noise; and six
**test objects**, three inside that range (0.5, 1.0 and 1.4 kg) and three outside it: 2 kg
(13.7 N), 3 kg (20.6 N) and a buoyant 0.2 kg body of 0.4 L (2.0 N upward, lighter than the water
it displaces, a sign the training set never contains). Each adapter is scored one step ahead:
at every sample it predicts the residual at the true state from what it has learned from the
measured samples before, then sees the new one. The error is chapter 7's ratio, the
prediction's error over the residual's own size.

## 8.2 Three ways to learn an object, and a fourth

**Three numbers.** Chapter 7's weight, mass and drag at the jaws, by recursive least squares
on the stream: each sample gives ten equations in three unknowns, weighted by the residual's
scale per coordinate; the prior is no object; a forgetting factor of 0.98 gives the estimate
a memory of five seconds so that the grasp's transient fades, and the covariance is capped at
the prior so that it cannot wind up in a direction nothing excites (the drag, while the
vehicle hovers). The mass and the drag are kept non-negative; the weight may have either
sign. Chapter 9's winner, carried to three dimensions and to a stream of sensor noise. The
plan asked for four parameters; the fourth, an added mass distinct from the mass, is not
identifiable from the jaws' acceleration alone, since both multiply it, so it is three.

**Structure, updated online.** Chapter 7's passive model as it was trained on the mission's
object, with its three scalars replaced by the same least squares and its added-mass factor,
joint inertias and dissipation network moved by five Adam steps on every sample since the
grasp, each time one arrives. It stays passive at every step, by construction.

**Context.** In-context adaptation: no parameter moves at test time. An encoder reads a
summary of the last second of the stream (the mean of the features, the last features, the
mean and the spread of the residuals, and how full the window is: 55 numbers) and gives a
latent of four numbers; a head reads the state and the latent and gives the residual. Both
are trained end to end, offline, on the 24 training flights, every sample of each flight an
example with its own history, so the net sees the first second after a grasp as often as the
steady carry. The RMA recipe, without the teacher. A first version fed the encoder the ten
raw samples and learned the noise; the summary is what made it learn the object.

**Hybrid.** The three numbers as the physics prior, and a context head trained on what they
leave: the residual minus the numbers fitted on each training flight by least squares. At
test, the numbers come from the recursion and the head adds its correction from the latent.

**What it shows.** Inside the training range the free adapters do what they were trained
for, and no better: on the 1 kg object the context net is at 25% after five seconds and the
hybrid at 22%, where chapter 7's numbers fitted to that very object sit at 2% and the online
numbers, starting from nothing, reach 7% (they need 5.5 s to get under 20%, slower than on
the heavier objects because the 1 kg residual is small against the noise). On 1.4 kg all four
adapters are under 20% within 2.5 s. Outside the range the kinds part. The three numbers
reach 20% in 1.4 s on 2 kg, 2.3 s on 3 kg and 3.9 s on the buoyant object, and after five
seconds average 3%, 18% and 18%; the structured model, whose scalars are the same recursion,
follows them and then wanders as its network keeps moving on noisy samples (5%, 25%, 29%).
The context net, which has never seen a weight above 14.5 N or below zero, answers 13% on
2 kg (just past its edge), 67% on 3 kg and 113% on the buoyant object, worse than predicting
nothing; the hybrid, which has the numbers to stand on, does not stand on them: its learned
correction, trained on what the numbers left in-distribution, adds 12%, 60% and 96% out of
it. The most that can be said for the latent is that it degrades gracefully near the edge
of its distribution and not beyond it. That is not a property one can budget for.

**What it shows.** The weight is found: within 1 to 2 N of the truth by two seconds for
every object from the buoyant one to 2 kg, and on 3 kg within 1 N at five seconds before it
drifts to 27 N, a third too much. The mass is never found. Every estimate collapses to zero
within two seconds, for every object, though the true value runs from 0.2 to 3.5 kg: the
regressor for the mass is the jaws' acceleration, which from these sensors is noise, and
least squares with a noisy regressor is biased towards zero, chapter 2's errors-in-variables
by another name. What the mass should have explained, the inertia of a 3 kg body swinging
on the arm during the return, leaks into the weight, which is why the 3 kg curve drifts.
The drag is not found either, but it does not matter: the jaws move at a few centimetres
per second and the drag's force is below the noise. Three numbers, then, are really one
number and two placeholders, and the one number is the one the controller needs.

## 8.3 Inside the controller

The adapters go inside chapter 7's NMPC, which now takes the model's parameters at every
solve: the three numbers (the weight on the jaws' basis, the mass as an implicit added
inertia, the drag), and for the context and hybrid models the latent, with the head's
network inside the integrator. The retrieval is chapter 7's, 30 s from the grasp, carrying
the mission's object, the 2 kg one and the buoyant one, but the adapter sees only the
measured stream, made in the loop from the noisy sensors and the believed commands. The
fixed numbers of chapter 7 are the baseline, right on 1 kg and wrong on the other two.

**What it shows.** With the object it was fitted on, the fixed numbers are the ceiling: tip
2.9 cm rms, $1.8^\circ$ of pitch, 1.1 cm after five seconds; the online numbers, starting from
nothing, match them (2.9 cm, 1.0 cm after five seconds) at the price of $5.8^\circ$ of pitch in
the first second, while the estimate spikes to 13 N and back. With the 2 kg object the
fixed numbers are wrong by 7 N, and the controller feels it: $16^\circ$ of pitch at the grasp and a
standing error of 1.5 cm it never removes. The online numbers find the weight by three
seconds (23 N at one second, 15 at two, 13.5 at five against 13.7 true) and remove it: 0.2 cm
at the end against 1.5, the pitch $13.5^\circ$. With the buoyant object the fixed numbers are wrong
by 9 N the other way and the controller is wrong with them: a 41 cm excursion at the lift,
$16^\circ$ of pitch, a standing 2 cm. The online numbers find the sign in the first second
(-1.5 N at one second, -2.0 at two, true -2.0) and the tip never leaves 7 cm: 0.9 cm rms,
0.4 after five seconds, $3.7^\circ$ of pitch. Three numbers from the raw sensors, inside the
optimizer, cost nothing on the known object and give back everything on the unknown one.

The nets are a different story. The context net carries the 1 kg and 2 kg objects, with
18 to $22^\circ$ of pitch, sixteen and four solves that did not converge and a standing error of
2 to 4 cm, and on the buoyant object its latent leaves the region it was trained on, the
pitch climbs for four seconds, the thrust saturates and the plant diverges 4.5 s after the
grasp: chapter 9's failure in three dimensions, reproduced with a model that was at 13%
error on a 2 kg object and had never been told what a buoyant one looks like. The hybrid is
worse than both its parts on every object: 14 to 20 cm rms, 52 to $71^\circ$ of pitch, 22 to 32
failed solves, and a weight estimate thrown to 22, 38 and 10 N by the correction's forces,
which the recursion then has to unlearn over twenty seconds. The prior did not get to win,
and the optimizer found the states where the correction was wrong. The solve times say the
rest: 1.2 to 1.9 s per solve for the numbers, 6 to 21 s with a network inside, on a laptop
running three retrievals at once; none of them real time, the networks by two orders.

What we learned:

- Out of distribution is where physics wins, and the mission is always out of distribution:
  the object is the thing nobody measured. Three numbers by least squares adapt to a buoyant
  body or one twice the heaviest trained in two to four seconds; a latent trained on 24
  objects answers nonsense a few newtons past its edge, and gives no sign that it is.
- A hybrid is not automatically better than its physics. A learned correction trained on
  what the physics leaves in-distribution is a learned error out of it; the prior has to be
  allowed to win, which means the correction needs its own coverage test (chapter 4's bound
  on a learned model is the shape of it).
- Adaptation from raw sensors finds the weight and not the mass. The differenced
  acceleration is noise, and least squares on a noisy regressor shrinks what it multiplies;
  the controller lives with the weight, which is what matters at a few centimetres per
  second, and the inertia of a heavy object leaks into it.
- Inside the optimizer the ranking does not change, it sharpens. Three numbers from the
  sensors give the controller back everything the unknown object took, at no cost on the
  known one; a latent trained on 24 objects diverges on the 25th kind; a hybrid gives the
  optimizer a learned correction to exploit and it does. Chapter 7's warning was about
  energy; this one is about distribution, and the object is always out of it.

Every chapter so far has lived with a model that is wrong somewhere: the coefficients within a
few percent, the current a few centimetres per second behind, the pose within a bound that
holds nine times in ten. Each was a floor the next chapter accepted. This chapter is about the
cases that are not floors but faults: the water does something the model did not allow for,
and the question is not how wrong the model is but how soon the vehicle finds out, and what it
does then. The plan names three monitors and three faults. This first part builds the one
chapter 6 asked for: the gust the filter has not seen yet, caught by the filter's own
innovations, and fed to the shield's budget. The energy-balance monitor of chapter 7's learned
model and the object heavier than trained wait for those chapters; the report's closing
summary waits for them too.

The code is in `src/subsea_lab/monitors.py`; the numbers come
from `scripts/evaluate_monitors.py`.

## 10.1 A statistic the vehicle can compute

Chapter 6 measured the cost of not knowing the current: two centimetres of standoff per
0.1 m/s of uncertainty in still water, ten in a 0.4 m/s flow. Budgeting the mission's gusts
blind, at 0.3 m/s, kept the vehicle out of the keep-out zone in every flight and cost 20 cm of
reach and three grasps in eight. Not budgeting them cost a violation in five flights of eight.
What the mission needs is to pay the wide budget only while a gust blows, which needs to know
that one does.

The filter of chapter 3 already produces the signal. At every DVL update it computes the
normalised innovation squared, the mismatch between what the model predicted and what the
sensor measured, weighted by what the filter believed its uncertainty to be. Under the model
that number is chi-square with the measurement's dimension (four, with the pressure sensor);
when the water accelerates the vehicle in a way the model did not predict, it is not. The
**monitor** sums the last ten of them, a second of data, and compares the sum with the
chi-square quantile at 99.9% for forty degrees of freedom, 73.4: a model that is right exceeds
it one second in a thousand. When it is exceeded the monitor is **raised** for three seconds,
and while raised the shield of chapter 6 keeps its barrier against the wide ball of current,
0.3 m/s, instead of the narrow one, 0.1 m/s. That is the whole mechanism: a statistic, a
threshold, a hold, a switch.

Before any gust, a probe: in the mission's water the DVL statistic sums to between 14 and 21
(the filter is a little conservative, as chapter 3 found); under a 0.35 m/s gust it goes to
between 90 and 260 within a second.

Three budgets to compare it with: the narrow one throughout (chapter 6's flights), the wide one
throughout (chapter 6's safe-but-far case), and the **truth as monitor**, the same switch
driven by the gust itself, which is the ceiling: the best any detector could do. The flights
are chapter 6's, with the same eight gusts of 0.1 to 0.4 m/s at random moments of the work and
the same turbidities, the station on the boundary and the camera's bound as the margin. Each
monitor is scored by its **delay**, the time from the gust's onset to the raise, against the
**time to harm**, the time from the onset to the boundary crossing on the narrow budget.

What it shows:

- **The monitor sees the gust in under a second**, for every gust strong enough to matter: 0.8
  to 1.4 s after the onset for the five gusts above 0.24 m/s, never for the two below 0.17,
  which never threatened anything, and once after 4 s for the 0.21 m/s gust, harmless too.
  The statistic peaks twice, at the gust's rise and at its fall, which is what an innovation
  should do: the model is wrong while the water changes, not while it blows steadily. It is
  raised 3.6% of the time outside the gusts, on the approach's and the grasp's transients.
- **In under a second is not soon enough, by half a second.** On the narrow budget the three
  gusts that crossed the boundary did so 1.0 to 1.15 s after their onset; the monitor raised
  at 0.8 to 0.84 s on those flights, and the shield, now asking for 18 cm of standoff instead
  of 7, needs about a second more to move a vehicle that the water is pushing the other way.
  The monitored budget saved the 0.28 m/s flight (plus 0.5 cm where the narrow budget had
  minus 0.6) and lost the 0.31 and 0.40 ones by 1 and 7 cm, against 2 and 8 without it. Two
  flights of eight instead of three, 3% of the time inside instead of 4%.
- **The truth as monitor shows what the delay costs.** Switched at the onset, the shield backs
  the vehicle to 15 cm before the push arrives and no flight crosses, with 2 cm to spare at
  the worst. The difference between that and the monitored flights is the delay alone: the
  same shield, the same budget, 0.8 s later. A monitor that predicted the gust from its first
  tenth of a second, or a shield that backed off faster than its exponential pole allows,
  would close that gap; this one halves the violations and does not close it.
- **Every budget that is safe takes the grasp.** The wide budget stands 20 cm off and misses
  the handle by 6 cm on average; the truth as monitor stands 11 cm off and, in the one flight
  whose gust falls on the grasp, backs away by 25 cm at the moment the jaws close. The monitor
  pays the same price when it is raised during the grasp (7 cm at worst). Chapter 6's sentence
  stands: safety against the water is bought with reach, and the monitor only decides when to
  pay.

## 10.3 The energy the model cannot account for

Chapter 7 built a learned model that cannot put energy into the system, and asked for a
monitor that says when the plant does something the model's energy balance forbids. The
monitor lives on chapter 8's stream: ten times a second after the grasp, from the raw
sensors, the force the designer's model cannot explain, $\mathbf{r}$, and the generalized
velocity $\boldsymbol\zeta$. The power that force puts into the system is
$\boldsymbol\zeta^\top \mathbf{r}$; the power chapter 7's passive model predicts is
$\boldsymbol\zeta^\top \hat{\mathbf{r}}$; the **statistic** is the energy the model
cannot account for over the last second, the absolute value of the integral of the
difference. It is calibrated, not derived: three missions with the object the model was
trained on give the statistic's distribution under a model that is right, and its
quantile at 99.9% is the threshold, 0.76 J. A model that is wrong about the
object by its weight does work the model does not expect whenever the tip moves vertically,
and the statistic grows.

The second monitor watches chapter 8's adapter instead of chapter 7's model: the
innovation of every sample against the three numbers' prediction, normalised by the
recursion's own covariance and the sensors' noise, summed over the last second. A model
that is right is surprised by noise only; an object the numbers have not caught up with is
surprised every sample. Its threshold is calibrated the same way, a sum of 1618.

The **fault** is chapter 8's: an object heavier than the one the mission was planned for
(1 kg; 1.4, 2 and 3 kg are tried, and a buoyant one), carried by chapter 5's station LQR
and the arm's servos, whose integral needs five seconds to carry twice the weight it was
tuned for. The **harm** is chapter 9's: the tip more than 10 cm from its lifted target
once the lift is over, which is where every controller with integral action failed the 2 kg
object. The **response** is to wait: a raise of either monitor pushes the end of the lift,
and everything scheduled after it, six seconds past the raise, so that the integral has the
time the plan did not give it. It is the freeze of arm-lab's chapter 12 in water: not the
learning that is frozen but the plan.

**What it shows.** Both monitors see a heavier object within a second of the grasp. For 1.4, 2 and 3 kg
the energy statistic crosses its threshold at 1.0 s after the grasp and the adapter's at
1.0 s, two seconds before the harm (the lift's end at 3 s, where the 2 kg object is 10.7 cm
from its target and the 3 kg one 26 cm). The planned object raises neither during the lift;
both raise it once, at 8 s, when the return starts and the vehicle accelerates with the
object, a false alarm the calibration's three missions did not cover and the response, which
listens only during the scheduled lift, ignores. The buoyant object, 8 N wrong the other
way, raises the energy monitor at 1.6 s and the adapter's never: the adapter's innovations
scale with the object's forces, and a light one is quiet, which is also why the adapter's
statistic does not stay clear for a heavy one (around the threshold of 1618 for 2 kg, two to
five times above it for 3 kg, for the rest of the flight) and the response has to be a wait
bought once rather than a wait until the monitor is satisfied. The energy monitor does not clear either, for a better reason:
chapter 7's model is fixed and stays wrong about a 2 kg object for good, and the monitor says
so.

The response is what the monitors are for. Stretching the lift six seconds past the raise
turns the 1.4 kg object's 5.8 cm into 0.4, and the 2 kg object's 10.7 cm, chapter 9's
failure, into 6.0, a success by the same threshold: the servo's integral, given nine seconds
instead of three, carries twice the weight it was tuned for. The 3 kg object is beyond the
response: 20 cm after the stretched lift against 26 after the scheduled one, because the
elbow's torque limit, not its integral's time, is what the weight exceeds, and no waiting
helps a joint that cannot lift. That is the monitor's honest boundary: it can buy time, and
time is worth exactly what the controller can do with it.

## 10.4 What still waits

- **The turbidity rising past the calibration set** is chapter 4's coverage monitor; the
  camera's innovations against the bound are logged in every flight of chapter 6 and were
  never outside their band, because no flight left the calibrated water. The fault needs
  water the bound was not calibrated for, and the monitor needs a flight in it.
- **A monitor on the certificate itself.** The energy monitor above compares the model with
  the measurement; a monitor on chapter 7's passivity would compare the measurement with
  the certificate, and raise when the plant is seen to put in energy a passive residual
  could never explain. It is the same statistic without the absolute value and with the
  model's dissipative part as the only term, and it waits for a fault that creates energy,
  which a heavier object does not.

## 10.5 What scaled, and what the water changed

The report set out from three labs and one question: whether the discipline that worked on
a pendulum, a house and an arm would carry under water, where standing still is not an
option. Ten chapters later, this is the account.

**What scaled from the pendulum.** Chapter 9 of pendulum-lab ended with five hypotheses
and every one of them held in three dimensions: the model is the first thing to fix and the
last to trust (chapters 1 and 2: the textbook model is twice too fast and the identified one
is within a few percent, and chapter 9's optimizers diverge where it is wrong); the
estimate is the limit (chapter 3's tip floor, 4.2 cm on the estimate against 0.5 on the
truth, and chapter 4 buying it down to 1 cm with a camera); learning helps where it has
structure to stand on (chapters 7 and 8: three numbers beat two networks and a Gaussian
process off the training flight, and a latent trained on 24 objects answers nonsense on the
25th kind); a robust filter pays in reach (chapter 6: two centimetres of standoff per
0.1 m/s of uncertainty, ten in a 0.4 m/s flow); and a free residual network inside an
optimizer diverges (chapter 7, reproduced; chapter 9, reproduced again by a model-free
policy that never flew a mission).

**What scaled from the arm.** arm-lab's barriers at a kilohertz became chapter 6's shield
at twenty hertz with the current as the disturbance and station keeping as the fallback,
and kept the keep-out zone in every flight whose gust it was budgeted for; arm-lab's
learned-bound-with-trigger became chapter 4's conformal bound with a gate and chapter 10's
monitors with a hold; arm-lab's finding that a learned policy does not beat a classical
controller behind the layer became chapter 9's, at a smaller budget and with the same
answer. thermal-lab's physics-first identification became chapter 2's safe active
identification and chapter 8's three numbers, which is the same idea at a tenth of a second.

**What the water changed.** Three things, which are the report's three questions. Safety
has no stop: chapter 6's barrier is only as good as what is known about the water, and
chapter 10's first monitor finds a gust half a second too late to be free; the safe state is
a controller, and a controller has a time to act. Perception is a bound, not a fix: the
camera's pose is worth four times the USBL's only with a calibrated statement of when it
is not, and chapter 4's gate is what made the bound usable rather than the bound itself.
And the object is the thing nobody measured: after every model in the report, the residual
is the thing in the jaws, three numbers nobody knew, and finding them in two seconds from
the raw sensors is worth more than any network trained in advance. The last thing the water
taught is in chapter 5: the grasp is an impact, and a model of it is a model of the wrist.

**What the report would do next.** Real-time iteration for the whole-body optimizer, whose
solve time, not its model, keeps it out of the loop; the three numbers fed to the servo's
feedforward, the missing controller of chapter 9's gallery; the estimator in the loop of
every controller, which only chapters 3, 4 and 6 flew; a coverage monitor in water the
bound was not calibrated for; and hours, not minutes, for the policies of chapter 9, which
the comparison deserves and the laptop could not give.

What we learned:

- The gust that breaks chapter 6's barrier is visible in the filter's own innovations within a
  second of its onset, at no cost in sensors and with a false alarm 4% of the time.
- A second is half a second too long: the vehicle needs about a second to move to the wider
  standoff, and the water needs one to push it over the boundary. Detection time against time
  to harm is a race the monitor loses by a little, which the truth as monitor shows it could
  win.
- Every budget that keeps the vehicle out during a gust gives up the grasp that falls in it;
  the monitor's value is to give it up only then.
- A heavier object is visible a second after the grasp in two places the vehicle already
  computes, the energy its learned model cannot account for and the innovations of its
  adapter, two seconds before the lift fails. The response that works is to wait, once;
  what the wait is worth is what the controller can do with it, which is everything for
  twice the weight and nothing for three times, where the joint, not the plan, is the
  limit.
- The report's one finding, in a sentence: under water the model is wrong about the water
  and the object, both can be found out in about a second from what the vehicle already
  measures, and what that second is worth depends on whether the controller can wait.

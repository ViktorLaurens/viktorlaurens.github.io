A drone a few hundred metres away covers only a handful of pixels. This chapter tests how
few pixels are enough for two small cameras on a real sky: what the detector reports
when the sky is empty, how small a target it still finds, and from what distance it picks
up a drone that flies towards the camera.

The sky recordings and the false alarms are real. The drones are not. No drone could be
flown in front of the cameras that week, so they are drawn into the recordings with a
model. Section 2.12 lists what this leaves open.

## 2.1 The cameras and the recordings

The setup is two Arducam IMX519 cameras on a Jetson Orin Nano Developer Kit with JetPack
6.2 and Arducam's driver for that release. The driver offers four sensor modes:

| 3840 x 2160 | 17 frames per second |

## 2.2 Pixels on target

A target of width $s$ at range $d$ covers

$$n = \tfrac{f_x s}{d}, \qquad f_x = \tfrac{w}{2 \tan(\tfrac{1}{2} \mathrm{HFOV})}$$

pixels, where $w$ is the image width in pixels. The horizontal field of view is 67 degrees
according to the lens data. It has not been calibrated, and every range in this chapter
scales with it. It gives $f_x = 1450$ pixels at 1920 x 1080.

The 3840 x 2160 mode covers the same field of view as the 1920 x 1080 mode: a frame of
recording C shrunk to half size matches a frame of recording B to within two pixels. A
target is therefore twice as wide in pixels in the larger mode.

| Target | 2 px | 4 px | 8 px |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.30 m drone, 1920 x 1080 | 218 m | 109 m | 54 m |
| 0.30 m drone, 3840 x 2160 | 435 m | 218 m | 109 m |

## 2.3 The detector

The detector does not decide what an object is. It reports every small thing that is new
or moving against the sky and passes these candidates on to a later stage. For every frame
it does six things:

1. It aligns the frame with a slowly updated reference, to remove small shaking of the
   mount.
2. It band-pass filters the image, so that only structures of a few pixels remain. Clouds
   and brightness gradients disappear.
3. It subtracts two running averages of that filtered image, a fast one (about one second)
   and a slower one (about three seconds).
4. It divides the difference at each pixel by the noise level around that pixel, leaving
   out a small patch around the pixel itself. Busy regions then raise their own threshold.
5. It thresholds at five standard deviations, groups pixels into blobs, and keeps blobs of
   a plausible size that are not long thin streaks.
6. It confirms a candidate that shows up again nearby in three of the last five frames.

The six steps in formulas, with the values used.

**Alignment.** A shift between two images appears as a phase ramp between their Fourier
transforms $F_1$ and $F_2$. Dividing out the magnitudes leaves only that ramp, and its
inverse transform is a single peak at the shift [[55]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/):

$$
(\Delta x, \Delta y) = \arg\max \mathcal{F}^{-1} \left[ \frac{F_1 \overline{F_2}}{| F_1 \overline{F_2} |} \right]
$$

It is computed on an image reduced four times. A shift of more than 12 pixels means the
camera was moved, and the detector starts again.

**Band-pass.** The frame $I$ is smoothed with two Gaussians and the results are
subtracted:

$$
b = G_{1} * I - G_{5} * I
$$

The indices are the widths in pixels. A difference of Gaussians approximates the
Laplacian of a Gaussian [[56]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/). It responds most strongly to a spot of one to three
pixels and gives zero on anything that is smooth over more than about ten pixels, which
is why clouds and the brightness gradient of the sky disappear.

**Background.** Each pixel keeps a running average of the band-passed image:

$$
B_t = (1 - \alpha) B_{t-1} + \alpha b_t
$$

This is a first-order low-pass filter with a memory of $\frac{1}{\alpha}$ frames. With
$\alpha = 0.03$ that is 33 frames, or 1.1 seconds. The second average uses
$\alpha = 0.009$, which is 3.7 seconds, and catches targets that move too slowly to
differ from the fast one. The change is $d_t = b_t - B_{t-1}$.

**Score.** The change is divided by the noise level around the pixel:

$$
z(x) = \frac{| d(x) |}{\max ( \sigma_{loc}(x), \sigma_{glob}, \sigma_{min} )}, \qquad
\sigma_{loc}^2(x) = \frac{1}{576} \Big( \sum_{25 \times 25} d^2 - \sum_{7 \times 7} d^2 \Big)
$$

The local level is the mean square of $d$ in a window of 25 by 25 pixels without the 7 by
7 pixels in its centre, so that a target does not raise its own threshold. The global
level is 1.48 times the median of $| d |$ over the frame, an estimate of the standard
deviation that outliers do not disturb. This is the constant-false-alarm-rate test known
from radar [[18]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/): the threshold follows the noise, so the rate of false alarms stays
the same in quiet and in busy parts of the sky.

**Threshold.** A blob needs one pixel with $z$ above 5 and grows into neighbours above 3.
If the noise were Gaussian and independent, a pixel would pass 5 with a probability of

$$
P(z \geq 5) = 2 Q(5) = 5.7 \cdot 10^{-7}
$$

where $Q$ is the tail of the normal distribution. With 1.35 million sky pixels in a
frame that is 0.8 false pixels per frame. A target with a change of $A$ times the noise
is found with probability $Q(5 - A)$: half of the time at $A = 5$ and nine times out of
ten at $A = 6.3$.

**Persistence.** A candidate is confirmed when it is seen again within 23 pixels in 3 of
the last 5 frames. If a false candidate appears in that circle with probability $p$ per
frame, independently, the probability of at least three in five is

$$
P = \sum_{k=3}^{5} \binom{5}{k} p^k (1 - p)^{5-k} \approx 10 p^3
$$

With one false pixel per frame, $p$ is 0.0012 and $P$ is about $2 \cdot 10^{-8}$. Independent
noise can therefore practically never produce a confirmed candidate. The confirmed
candidates that do appear on the empty sky (section 2.4) must come from things that
last for several frames: real objects, and noise that is correlated in time. This
correlation returns in sections 2.11 and 5.6.

The background under a confirmed candidate is frozen for 45 frames, so that a slow target
does not become part of the background.

The trees have to be excluded, otherwise every moving leaf is a candidate. The mask
is built from the recording itself: sky is where the image has almost no fine texture in
three quarters of the sampled frames, and that area is shrunk by 30 pixels to stay away
from anything textured. A bird or an aircraft that passes once does not end up in the mask.

Without the mask the detector reports its maximum of 60 candidates in every frame, all of
them in the trees.

## 2.5 How detection is tested

Measuring detection takes targets whose position is known in every frame, in situations
that are hard to set up: a few pixels wide, hovering, or flying in from far away. The
targets are therefore drawn into the real recordings. The model does the following, in
this order:

- **Shape.** A quadcopter seen from below at an angle (body, arms, motors, and rotor discs
  that are partly transparent), a fixed-wing aircraft, or a bird with flapping wings.
- **Sampling.** The shape is drawn sixteen times finer than the image and averaged down at
  its exact sub-pixel position. A three-pixel target therefore covers some pixels only
  partly.
- **Optics.** A Gaussian blur of 0.8 pixels for the lens and focus.
- **Motion blur.** A streak along the direction of motion, as long as the distance the
  target moves during 30 % of the frame interval.
- **Contrast.** The target is blended with the sky behind it. Contrast 1 is a dark
  silhouette, 0.2 is a faint one as you would see through haze. A sunlit target is the
  same with a light grey.
- **Noise.** The pasted pixels get the same noise level as the background.
- **Compression.** The target is drawn into the brightness plane of the recording, in the
  camera's own pixel format. Then the frames are encoded again with the recorder's
  settings, and the detector gets the decoded result.

The compression step is essential, because compression removes exactly the faint small
detail the detector is looking for. A target that skips this step is too easy to detect: a two-pixel target at contrast 0.4 is found in 92 % of frames without the
encoder and in 52 % with it. All numbers below include the encoder unless stated otherwise.

<Figure src="/media/three-pixels-in-the-sky/pasted_targets.png" width={70} alt="A grid of magnified crops of blue sky with pasted targets. Rows: dark quadcopter, sunlit quadcopter, fixed-wing aircraft, bird. Columns: widths of 3, 4, 6, 8, 12 and 20 pixels. The smallest are faint smudges; at 12 pixels the shapes are recognisable.">

Pasted targets on my own sky at contrast 0.6, magnified five times, before compression. Rows: dark quadcopter, sunlit quadcopter, fixed wing, bird. Below about 6 pixels every target is the same soft spot. Shape is not available at the ranges that matter.

Two results stand out. A flapping bird needs more pixels than a drone with the same span,
because its wings are thin and folded half of the time. At 4 pixels the bird is found in
49 % of frames and the quadcopter in all of them. The second result is a weakness. A
hovering quadcopter is found much less often than a crossing one: 32 % of frames at
4 pixels against 100 %. The detector looks for change, and a hovering target stops
changing. After five seconds of hover a 4-pixel target at contrast 0.4 is only found in
9 % of frames. Section 2.11 returns to this.

## 2.8 The same flight at 4K

The 3840 x 2160 mode has the same field of view with twice as many pixels across, so the
same drone at the same range is twice as wide in pixels. If the number of pixels were the
only thing that counts, the ranges of section 2.7 would double. The flights were repeated on
the 4K recording (17 frames per second, 30 Mbit/s) with the same detector settings. The
lens blur is 1.2 pixels instead of 0.8, because a lens does not become sharper when the
sensor is read out at a higher resolution. Four clips of the one 90-second recording
with three drones each give 12 flights.

The blob detector responds only at short range, and only at contrast 0.8.">

The same flights at 3840 x 2160, 12 flights. Solid: the detector of this chapter. Dashed: the blob detector. Four times as many pixels give about one and a half times the range, and not twice.

</Figure>

| Contrast | First sustained detection, 4K | Median, 4K | Median, 1080p | Width there, 4K |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.8 | 356 to 367 m | 360 m | 274 m | 2.4 px |
| 0.4 | 254 to 322 m | 295 m | 186 m | 3.0 px |
| 0.2 | 178 to 215 m | 180 m | 127 m | 4.8 px |

The range increases by a factor of 1.6 at contrast 0.4 and 1.4 at contrast 0.2, so less
than the factor of 2 that the pixel count suggests. At 4K the detector needs a wider
target (3.0 pixels against 2.3 at contrast 0.4). The blur spreads the same drone over more
pixels, and each of those pixels collects less light and has more noise.

The top row says little, because the flights start near 350 m and the drone is detected
almost from the first frame. The four clips also overlap in time and come from one
recording, so they are less independent than the 18 flights at 1080p.

The cost is computing time: four times as many pixels per frame, for a detector that
already runs slower than real time at 1080p. The sensible design is to search at
low resolution and to read out a 4K window only around a candidate.

## 2.9 What the video encoder costs

The recordings are compressed to 10 Mbit/s before the detector sees them, and compression
removes faint small detail first. To find out how much detection is lost this way,
the size test and the approach were repeated with the target encoded at other bitrates,
up to lossless, where the encoder keeps every pixel value.

Right: range at which an approaching drone is first held; from 10 Mbit/s to lossless it rises from 186 to 234 m at contrast 0.4 and from 127 to 153 m at contrast 0.2.">

Detection against the bitrate of the video encoder. Left: small crossing targets, two clips. Right: first sustained detection of the approaching drone, four flights per contrast. Below 20 Mbit/s the encoder removes the smallest targets. Without it the range is a quarter longer.

</Figure>

| | 5 Mbit/s | 10 Mbit/s | 20 Mbit/s | 40 Mbit/s | Lossless |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 px target, contrast 0.4, frames detected | 26 % | 52 % | 77 % | 83 % | 92 % |
| 4 px target, contrast 0.2, frames detected | 45 % | 80 % | 96 % | 98 % | 99 % |

Over all six flights per contrast, the lossless ranges are 234 m and 153 m, against 186 m
and 127 m at 10 Mbit/s. That is a quarter more range at contrast 0.4 and a fifth more at
0.2, only from leaving the encoder out. Doubling the bitrate to 20 Mbit/s already brings
the number of stray candidates down to the level of the empty sky.

Two things limit this test. The sky under the target has already been through the
recorder's encoder once, so "lossless" here means "no second encoding" and not a raw sensor
frame. And the stray candidates at 10 Mbit/s come from encoding the sky a second time at
the same bitrate, which a live system would not do.

For the design this means two things. A live detector on the Jetson should work on the
camera frames before they are compressed. And recordings for later analysis should be made
at 20 Mbit/s or more.

## 2.10 Telling a drone from a bird

The detector only says that something small is moving. The next stage has to say what it
is. Separating drones from birds in video is a known problem with its own series of
challenges [[16]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/)[[17]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/). A small network was trained for this (four convolution layers, 61 000 weights). It looks
at a 32 x 32 crop around each candidate and gives one of five answers: drone, bird,
airplane, helicopter or clutter. On one desktop core it labels 60 candidates in 13 ms.

**The network.** It is a small convolutional network [[57]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/). Each of its four layers
applies a set of 3 by 3 filters $W$ to its input $x$, normalises the result, and keeps
the positive part:

$$
y = \max ( 0, \text{BN}( W * x + b ) )
$$

The layers have 16, 32, 64 and 64 filters. After each of the first three, the image is
halved by keeping the largest value in every 2 by 2 block, so a crop of 32 pixels becomes
4. The largest value of each of the 64 final maps goes into one linear layer that gives a
score $s_k$ for each of the five classes. In total there are 60 901 weights. The scores
become probabilities through

$$
p_k = \frac{e^{s_k}}{\sum_j e^{s_j}}
$$

**Training.** For $N$ labelled crops with true classes $y_i$, the weights are chosen to
minimise the cross-entropy

$$
L = - \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \ln p_{y_i}(x_i)
$$

which is the negative log-likelihood of the labels. It is minimised by gradient descent
with the AdamW rule [[58]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/), a step size that rises to 0.004 and falls again, and a
weight decay of 0.001. Each crop is scaled to zero mean and unit spread, and half of the
crops are mirrored, so that the network cannot use the brightness of the sky or the
direction of flight.

**Scoring.** The score used is the area under the ROC curve (AUC) for "drone against the rest".
It has a direct meaning [[59]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/): it is the probability that a randomly chosen drone crop
gets a higher drone score than a randomly chosen other crop. A value of 0.5 is guessing
and 1 is perfect.

It was trained in three ways, and each version was tested on data it had not seen:

- **On real flying objects from another camera.** The public Halmstad dataset [[15]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/) has 285 short
  clips of drones, birds, airplanes and helicopters with labelled boxes, at 640 x 512. The
  first three quarters of the clips of each class are for training (210 clips, 40 880
  crops) and the last quarter for testing (70 clips). Every object is used twice:
  at its own size, and shrunk to 3 to 12 pixels.
- **On pasted targets in my own sky.** Drones and birds of 2.5 to 14 pixels are pasted into
  recording A, encoded again and passed through the detector. Each candidate becomes a
  crop (18 074 crops). The test set is made the same way from recording B (9 707 crops).
- **On both together.**

Trained on real data from the other camera: 0.97 on real drones, 0.96 on the same shrunk, 0.66 on pasted drones in our sky. Trained on pasted data: 0.72, 0.71 and 0.83. Trained on both: 0.96, 0.96 and 0.78.">

Drone against everything else, for three training sets and three test sets. 0.5 is chance. A network is only good on the kind of images it was trained on: 0.97 on its own kind and 0.66 on the other.

</Figure>

On one camera the network works. Trained and tested on Halmstad clips, and set so that it
keeps 95 % of the drones, it lets through 5 % of the clutter and 8 to 13 % of the birds.
Airplanes and helicopters are harder: 43 % and 32 to 40 % of them pass as drones.

From one camera to another it does not work. The network trained on Halmstad calls 5 035
of the 5 069 pasted drones in my sky clutter. That could mean that my pasted targets are
unrealistic. But it says the same about the one real aircraft my camera recorded, in 99 %
of 1 970 frames. So at least part of the problem is the camera. The Halmstad targets are
sharp dots, and my lens and encoder deliver a soft blob, as the crops show.

The network trained only on pasted targets has never seen a real flying object. On my
unmodified recordings it still separates the things that fly from the noise:

| Candidates in recordings A and B, nothing pasted | Frames | Called drone or bird |
|---|---|---|
| Not confirmed by the tracker | 5 502 | 2 % |
| Confirmed, track shorter than one second | 1 858 | 9 % |
| The aircraft, tracked for 66 seconds | 1 970 | 97 % (as drone) |
| Eight other tracks of one to six seconds | 527 | 90 % |

What these tracks are was judged by eye. The aircraft is called a drone because this network
has no class for aircraft. Of the eight short tracks, three are called bird in at least
90 % of their frames, and the others are a mix of bird, drone and clutter.

What the network cannot do is tell a small drone from a small bird in a single frame. On
recording B, keeping 95 % of the pasted drones lets through 78 % of the pasted birds. Only
from 9 pixels upward does that drop to 7 %. Below that size both are the same blob. The
difference then has to come from how the blob moves over time, or from another sensor.

Training on both sets together keeps both abilities (0.96 on Halmstad, 0.78 on my sky) but
does not close the gap between the cameras.

## 2.11 Five attempts to improve it

The tests above show three weaknesses: the hovering target (2.6), the object that is
mistaken for a drone (2.10), and the range at low contrast (2.7). Five ideas were tried
against them, each with the same tests as before.

| Attempt | Aimed at | Result | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. A confirmed track holds on to its target | the hovering drone | hovering at 3 px: 24 % of frames becomes 99 % | works |
| 2. One second of video as input to the network | drone or bird | birds that pass as drones: 17 % becomes 18 % | no gain |
| 3. Letting the network vote along a track | drone or bird | birds that pass: 11 % becomes 3 % after one second | works |
| 4. Training data degraded to look like my camera | a classifier that is blind on my camera | reacts to the real aircraft in 99 % of frames instead of 1 %, but calls it a drone | half works |
| 5. Summing over time before deciding | faint targets | 2 px target: 52 % becomes 89 %; range gains 0 to 17 m | helps little |

Attempts 2 to 4 concern the classifier, and their lesson is the same as in section 2.10:
it needs labelled drones recorded with my own camera. The details are in
`runs/classifier/NOTES.md` in the repository. Attempts 1 and 5 say something about
the detector itself and are described here.

**Holding on needed a second rule.** The detector loses a hovering target because it only
reacts to change. A confirmed track now freezes the background under it and searches for
the spot itself in frames where the change has disappeared. That solved the hovering
case and created a new problem. On the empty sky of recording A the number of tracks of
two seconds or more went from zero to seven, all of them weak, motionless and in one
corner. The detector was holding on to something faint in the scene. A second rule
was needed: a track may only hold on if it has scored 9 once (the threshold is 5) or has moved 40
pixels. With it the empty sky is as before.

<Figure src="/media/three-pixels-in-the-sky/hover_hold.png" width={70} alt="Fraction of frames detected against target width for a hovering quadcopter at three contrasts, before and after. At contrast 0.4 the old detector rises from 0.03 at 2 pixels to 0.88 at 12 pixels; the new one is at 0.99 from 3 pixels. At contrast 0.2 the old one stays near or below 0.5; the new one is at 0.26 at 4 pixels and 1.0 from 6 pixels. At contrast 0.8 the new one is at 1.0 for all sizes.">

A hovering quadcopter that appears in place, before (dashed) and after (solid). Two clips, recordings A and B. A track that holds on to its target repairs the hovering case from 3 to 4 pixels upward.

</Figure>

A real drone does not appear out of nothing. It flies in and then stops. In a test where
the target crosses for five seconds and then hovers, the share of frames detected during
the hover goes from 24 % to 99 % at 3 pixels and contrast 0.4, and from 16 % to 97 % at 4
pixels and contrast 0.2. The rule has a price: the detection range does not improve,
because a drone of one or two pixels far away neither scores 9 nor moves 40 pixels.

**Summing over time works in theory and little on my sky.** A faint target that keeps its
course should become visible if the evidence is added up along its path before a
threshold is applied. This is known as track-before-detect [[19]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/)[[60]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/). For an assumed
motion $v$ in pixels per frame the detector keeps a sum that fades with a factor $\gamma$:

$$
A_t^{v}(x) = d_t(x) + \gamma A_{t-1}^{v}(x - v)
$$

A target that moves with $v$ adds up in the same place of the sum. Its amplitude grows by
$\frac{1}{1 - \gamma}$, while independent noise only grows by
$\frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - \gamma^2}}$. For $\gamma = 0.8$ that is a factor 5 against 1.67, a gain
of 3 in signal-to-noise ratio. If the noise has a correlation $\rho$ from frame to frame,
its variance grows by an extra factor $\frac{1 + \gamma \rho}{1 - \gamma \rho}$. On artificial frames
with independent noise it does what the theory predicts: a spot of 1.5 times the noise
level is found in 99 % of frames, where the plain detector finds it in 2 %. On my sky the
first version gained almost nothing, for a reason that can be measured. The noise of the sky is not
independent from frame to frame. Half of it is still there in the next frame, so the
summed noise grows by a factor of 2.5 where independent noise would give 1.67. A finer
version with 25 assumed motions finds a 2 pixel target in 89 % of frames instead of 52 %,
at six times the computing. In metres it gives 0 to 17 m, because a drone that flies
straight at the camera hardly moves in the image while it is far away. The same
correlation in time returns in section 5.6, where it misleads the tracker.

## 2.12 Limits

- **The targets are a model.** There is no real lighting on the airframe, no propeller
  flicker and no rolling shutter, and the approach is one straight path with a weave. The
  compression of the target is modelled, but not the camera's own noise reduction and
  sharpening.
- **The only check against real drones is on another camera.** On the public Halmstad
  dataset [[15]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/) (640 x 512, heavily compressed, drones of 16 pixels and more) an earlier version
  of the same detector put a candidate on the labelled drone in 96 % of frames over 61
  clips from a static camera.
- **One sky.** Cloudless, around three in the afternoon, with almost no pixel noise (0.16
  grey levels in open sky). Cloud edges, haze, dusk and rain are not covered, and
  all of them will reduce the range.
- **The field of view is assumed** (67 degrees) and the camera is not calibrated. The
  ranges scale with it.
- **The sky under every pasted target has been encoded twice**, once by the recorder and
  once by the test. At 10 Mbit/s the second pass adds about four stray candidates per
  frame that a real recording does not have.
- **The classifier has not seen a real drone on my camera.** It is trained on pasted
  targets. The test clips of the public dataset may come from the same sessions as the
  training clips. Its only check on real objects is a handful of tracks named by eye.
- **Speed.** About 7 frames per second at 1920 x 1080 on one desktop core, and not measured
  on the Jetson. It is not a live detector yet.

## 2.13 How this compares with the literature

**Detecting by motion.** Finding what changes against a learned background is one of the
oldest methods in video analysis [[53]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/). For flying objects it remains the method of
choice when the target is only a few pixels wide. A well-known study on detecting
aircraft and drones from a moving camera concluded that appearance in a single frame is
not enough at that size and that the motion over several frames has to be used
[[43]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/). The detector here is the version of that idea for a camera that stands still.

**How many pixels are needed.** A criterion from 1958 still describes this well
[[46]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/). An observer detects an object when about one pair of light and dark lines fits
across it, which is two pixels. Recognising what kind of object it is takes about four
pairs, eight pixels, and identifying it six to eight pairs. The detector here becomes reliable
between two and four pixels, and the classifier starts to separate drones from birds at
nine pixels. Both agree with the criterion. It also means that no amount of training
will make a classifier name a target of three pixels. That takes more pixels, from a
longer lens or from a shorter distance, which is the argument of chapter 6.

**Drones and birds.** The public dataset used here reports its results by the size of the
drone in the image, down to a class of one to five pixels [[15]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/), which is where my
tests start. A series of international challenges on telling drones from birds in video
shows the same two difficulties: the smallest targets are missed, and birds cause
most of the false alarms [[16]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/)[[17]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/). A larger benchmark for following drones in
thermal and daylight video exists as well [[54]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/).

**Small targets in infrared.** The closest relatives of this detector come from infrared
search systems, where a target of a few pixels against sky and cloud has been the
standard problem for decades. A recent review sorts the methods into those that look at
the local contrast in one frame and those that use motion over several frames
[[61]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/). The detector here combines one of each.

**What this test adds and lacks.** The published results come from the cameras of their
authors. A range curve cannot be carried over to another camera, because it depends on
the lens, the sensor noise and the video encoder. This chapter measures that curve for
my camera, including the loss in the encoder, which does not seem to be reported elsewhere.
What it lacks is what the published work has: real drones.

A camera needs light and a free line of sight. A microphone needs neither, and a small
drone makes quite a lot of noise. This chapter analyses a recording of a Crazyflie made
with two phones, describes what the drone sounds like, and estimates from how far a
microphone could still hear it.

The sound of the drone is measured. How loud it is could not be measured with phones, so
the range comes from a model of how sound gets weaker with distance, for a range of
assumptions. Section 3.9 lists what is still open.

## 3.1 The recording

Two phones on the floor of a living room, about 2 m apart, recorded for 100 seconds. The recording contains a clap, some silence, the Crazyflie hovering about 1 m
away for 25 seconds, then the same drone at about 3 m and 5 m, a crash, and a second clap.

Only the hover at 1 m is steady. Further away the drone flew over the edge of a low table
and then over furniture. The height controller had to work hard there, and in the end the
drone landed on the floor.

## 3.2 What a Crazyflie sounds like

The hover sounds like a set of narrow tones on top of a broad hiss. The strongest tone is
at $533~\text{Hz}$ (between 516 and $545~\text{Hz}$ during most of the hover), and tones
near its multiples can be followed up to 6 kHz. This is what rotors produce. Each of the four
motors turns two blades, the blades pass about 530 times per second, and the four motors
never run at exactly the same speed. Every multiple is therefore a small group of lines.

A rotor with $n_b$ blades that turns $f_{rot}$ times per second produces a tone at

$$
f_{bp} = n_b f_{rot}
$$

With two blades, $533~\text{Hz}$ means 267 turns per second, or 16 000 per minute. The
propellers of this Crazyflie are 55 mm across, so the blade tips move at
$2 \pi \cdot 267 \cdot 0.0275 = 46$ m/s. This is the quantity a radar uses to recognise a
drone (chapter 4). At $9.4~\text{GHz}$ these tips would give Doppler shifts up to
$2.9~\text{kHz}$. The microphone and the radar measure the same rotation in two ways.

The tones do not stay in one place. While the drone was struggling to keep its height, the
strongest tone moved between 439 and $574~\text{Hz}$ and the level changed by 7 dB
(against 2 dB during the steady hover). A detector that waits for one fixed frequency
would miss a drone that is manoeuvring. It has to look for the whole pattern of tones,
wherever that pattern sits.

## 3.3 What the phones did to the level

In open air a sound source is 9.5 dB weaker at 3 m than at 1 m, and 14 dB weaker at 5 m.
In the recording the part at 3 to 5 m is within 1 dB of the hover at 1 m, on both phones.
The room adds echoes, but not that much.

The explanation is that the phones adjust their own gain. After each clap the level of
the silence drops and then slowly comes back. The two phones also disagree by 18 dB about
how quiet the same room is: the hover is 57 dB above the room on one phone and 39 dB on
the other.

So these recordings show what the sound looks like, but not how strong it is. They
cannot be used to check how the level drops with distance, or to read off how far the
drone is above the background. That takes a recording app that switches off the phone's
processing, or a measurement microphone.

## 3.4 How sound changes with distance

Three things change a sound on its way to a microphone that is far away. With a model
of these, the hover at 1 m can be turned into the same drone at any distance.

The level drops because the wave spreads out, and because the air absorbs part of it:

$$
L(r) = L(1~\text{m}) - 20 \log_{10} r - \alpha(f) (r - 1)
$$

with $r$ in metres. The absorption $\alpha$ increases strongly with frequency [[20]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/).
At 10 degrees and 70 % humidity it is 3.7 dB per kilometre at 1 kHz and 33 dB per kilometre
at 4 kHz. A distant drone is therefore not only quieter, it also sounds duller.

The sound that arrives now left the drone a moment earlier, because it needs time to
travel. When the distance changes, this delay changes, and that causes the Doppler shift.
A tone of frequency $f$ from a drone that approaches at speed $v$ is heard at

$$
f' = \frac{f}{1 - \frac{v}{c}}
$$

where $c$ is the speed of sound, about 343 m/s. The model applies the changing delay
directly, and the shift follows from it.

A pure tone that passes at 10 m/s checks the model against these formulas. The
received frequency agrees to within $0.6~\text{Hz}$ and the level to within 0.1 dB at four
ranges between 26 and 129 m. With the recorded hover as the source, the band from 4 to
8 kHz arrives 8 dB weaker than the band from 400 to $1000~\text{Hz}$ at 131 m. That is the
effect of the absorption.

## 3.5 How far the rotor tones carry

The detector follows from section 3.2. Once per second it computes the spectrum and
measures how far each frequency stands above its smoothed surroundings. It then adds these
values along a comb: a fundamental between 400 and $700~\text{Hz}$ and its multiples up to
6 kHz. It does this for every possible fundamental and keeps the largest sum as the score.
The threshold is set on noise alone, at one false alarm per 1000 seconds.

In formulas: let $X(f)$ be the spectrum of one second of sound and $e(f)$ the number of
decibels by which it stands above its own median over the neighbouring $100~\text{Hz}$. The score
for a fundamental $f_0$ is the mean of that excess at its multiples, and the detector
takes the best fundamental:

$$
C = \max_{f_0} \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^{K} e(k f_0), \qquad K = \left\lfloor \frac{6000}{f_0} \right\rfloor
$$

Dividing by the local median makes the score independent of how loud the recording is
and of the slope of the background spectrum. It is the same idea as the local noise level
in the camera detector of section 2.3. Averaging over about ten multiples is what lets
the detector find tones that are each too weak to stand out on their own.

The drone in this test is the recorded hover, weakened by the model. The background is
pink noise, which has a falling spectrum similar to wind and distant traffic. One number
is missing: how far the drone at 1 m is above the background, in the band from 300 to
$8000~\text{Hz}$. This is the margin. The phones cannot give it (section 3.3), so the
range is given for several margins.

|---|---|
| 20 dB | 9 m |

The table gives the range at which the detector still fires in half of the seconds. Every
10 dB of margin roughly triples it, which is what the spreading law predicts.
In the quiet room the right phone put the Crazyflie 39 dB above the background. That
number cannot be trusted (section 3.3), and outdoors the background is higher anyway.
On the other hand a Crazyflie weighs a few tens of grams, and a drone that carries a camera is
much louder.

For comparison, outdoor studies with larger drones report detection ranges of about 100 to
200 m, and up to 300 to 500 m when the detector is trained for long distances [[22]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/).
The microphone hardware matters as much as the processing [[23]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/). A Crazyflie is much
smaller than the drones in those studies.

So a microphone hears a drone of this size at tens of metres, and
larger drones at a few hundred metres at most. But it does so in fog and at night, when the
camera of chapter 2 sees nothing.

## 3.6 Two phones as a pair


A sound reaches the nearer phone first. With the phones 2 m apart the difference is at
most 5.8 milliseconds, and it gives the direction of the source. This time
difference is estimated with the generalised cross-correlation method [[21]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/). With $X_1$ and $X_2$ the spectra of
the two microphones, it finds the delay $\tau$ at which they agree best, after dividing
out the magnitude at every frequency so that all frequencies count equally:

$$
\hat{\tau} = \arg\max_{\tau} \int \frac{X_1(f) \overline{X_2(f)}}{| X_1(f) \overline{X_2(f)} |} e^{2 \pi i f \tau} df
$$

For microphones a distance $a$ apart, the delay gives the bearing $\theta$ from
$\sin \theta = \frac{c \tau}{a}$, and an error $\sigma_\tau$ in the delay becomes an error
in bearing of

$$
\sigma_\theta = \frac{c \sigma_\tau}{a \cos \theta}
$$

This has the same form as the phase method of a radio receiver in section 4.7. Sound is
a million times slower than radio, so two phones 2 m apart can do with a timing of a
tenth of a millisecond what radio antennas have to do with a few picoseconds. In the model, with a pair
3 m apart, the bearing is correct to within a few degrees when the noise is 20 dB below
the drone. It fails when the noise is as loud as the drone (see the figure in section
3.4).

In the real recordings the time difference during the hover is steady at 18.4
milliseconds. That is three times more than the geometry allows, so the two files are not
lined up well enough. The clap lines them up only roughly: the two phones have
separate clocks, and the clap itself reached them at different times.

The change can still be read off. When the drone left its hover the time difference
moved by 2.9 milliseconds. That is one metre of extra path to one of the phones, and for
phones 2 m apart it means the direction changed by about 30 degrees. For an absolute
bearing both microphones need to be on the same clock, so one recorder with two inputs.

## 3.7 Ears with a shape

Owls and bats do not hear with bare eardrums. The outer ear collects sound from one
direction and shades it from others. Before radar, aircraft were found in the same way,
with large horns and concrete sound mirrors. Two questions decide whether this helps
against a drone: how much a shaped ear gains, and at which frequencies.

**A dish.** A parabolic dish of diameter $D$ concentrates sound of wavelength $\lambda$
on a microphone in its focus. Its gain and the width of its beam are approximately

$$
G = \eta \left( \frac{\pi D}{\lambda} \right)^2, \qquad \theta \approx 70 \frac{\lambda}{D} \text{ degrees}
$$

with an efficiency $\eta$ of about one half. These are the same formulas as for a radar
antenna in chapter 4. Sound waves are long, and that limits what a dish can do:

| Tone | Wavelength | Gain of a 0.5 m dish | Beam width |
|---|---|---|---|
| $533~\text{Hz}$, the fundamental | 64 cm | 5 dB | 90 degrees |
| $2~\text{kHz}$ | 17 cm | 16 dB | 24 degrees |
| $4~\text{kHz}$ | 8.6 cm | 22 dB | 12 degrees |

At the fundamental the dish is smaller than the wavelength and does almost nothing. At
the higher rotor tones it gains 16 to 22 dB. By the result of section 3.5, where every
10 dB of margin triples the range, that would multiply the range several times. But these
are the tones that the air absorbs first (section 3.4): at 500 m the tone at 4 kHz has
lost 16 dB to absorption alone. A dish therefore helps most at a few hundred metres, and
it gives a direction to within its beam width.

**An array.** A dish has to be pointed, so it has the same problem as a zoom camera: it
needs to be told where to listen. An array of $N$ microphones does not. Adding their
signals with the right delays forms the same kind of beam in software, in every direction
at once, and gains $10 \log_{10} N$ decibels against noise that differs between the
microphones: 12 dB for 16 microphones and 18 dB for 64. The beam width follows from the
size of the array as it does for a dish, and the microphones have to be closer together
than half a wavelength, which is 4.3 cm at 4 kHz. The outdoor studies cited in section
3.5 use such arrays.

**What the animals add.** The ears of a barn owl sit at different heights and point in
different directions. A sound from above is louder in one ear and a sound from below in
the other, which gives the owl the elevation of a source with only two ears
[[64]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/). Two microphones in shaped, asymmetric shells could do the same.
This is cheaper than an array, and it works best at the high frequencies where the shells
are large compared with the wavelength.

For a system that has to find a drone at any bearing, an array is the better first
choice. A dish, or a pair of shaped ears on a pan-tilt mount, makes sense as a second
stage that listens along a bearing another sensor has given.

## 3.9 Limits

- **No level.** The phones adjust their gain, so neither the loudness of the drone nor
  the background was measured. The ranges in section 3.5 are for assumed margins.
- **One drone, one steady hover of 18 seconds, indoors.** The echoes of the room are part
  of the recording. A drone in forward flight sounds different from a hovering one.
- **The background is pink noise.** Real backgrounds contain tones of their own (engines,
  birds, wind in cables) that a comb detector can confuse with rotors. A real
  outdoor background was not recorded.
- **The model leaves out** the reflection from the ground, wind, temperature layers, and
  the fact that a drone sounds different in different directions. Over a few hundred
  metres these change the level by several dB in either direction.
- **No bearing from the real recordings**, only a change of bearing, because the phones
  do not share a clock.

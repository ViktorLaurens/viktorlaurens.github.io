The camera of chapter 2 reaches a few hundred metres and the microphone of chapter 3 tens
of metres. The sensors that
reach kilometres are a radar and a radio receiver, and the sensor that keeps working at
night is a thermal camera. None of them was available for this project. This chapter explains how each one
works, starting from the physics, and calculates what it could do against a small drone.

Nothing in this chapter is measured. The formulas are standard and the numbers put into
them are stated each time, so every result can be recalculated.

## 4.1 Radar: how it measures

A radar sends a radio wave and listens for the part that comes back. It gets three things
from the echo.

**Range from the delay.** The wave travels to the target and back at the speed of light
$c$, so an echo that arrives after a time $\tau$ comes from a range

$$
R = \frac{c \tau}{2}
$$

Two targets can be told apart in range when their echoes do not overlap. That depends
only on the bandwidth $B$ of the transmitted signal:

$$
\Delta R = \frac{c}{2B}
$$

A bandwidth of $50~\text{MHz}$ gives 3 m. A modern drone radar does not send short pulses.
It sends a continuous tone whose frequency rises steadily (FMCW), and it measures the
delay as the frequency difference between what it sends and what it receives. The
resolution is the same, but the peak power can be a thousand times lower.

**Radial speed from the Doppler shift.** A target that approaches with speed $v$
shortens the path by $2v$ every second, and the echo comes back at a frequency that is
higher by

$$
f_d = \frac{2 v}{\lambda}
$$

where $\lambda$ is the wavelength. At $9.4~\text{GHz}$ the wavelength is 3.2 cm, and a
drone at 10 m/s gives a shift of $627~\text{Hz}$. To measure the shift with a resolution
$\Delta f$ the radar has to look for a time $T = \frac{1}{\Delta f}$. Looking for 20 ms
separates speeds that differ by 0.8 m/s.

**Direction from the antenna.** An antenna of width $D$ forms a beam of about
$\frac{\lambda}{D}$ radians. An antenna of 1 m at this wavelength has a beam of 1.8
degrees, which is 160 m wide at 5 km. Comparing the echo in two overlapping beams
(monopulse) improves this by about a factor of ten. Many drone radars scan only in
azimuth, so the height of the target is the weakest of their measurements.

## 4.2 Radar: how far it reaches

The radar equation follows the power step by step [[29]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/). The transmitter
sends a power $P_t$ through an antenna with gain $G$. At range $R$ this gives a power
density of $\frac{P_t G}{4 \pi R^2}$. The target takes power out of the wave as if it had
an area $\sigma$, the radar cross-section, and scatters it in all directions. Back at the
radar, the density is reduced by another $4 \pi R^2$, and the antenna collects it with an
effective area $\frac{G \lambda^2}{4 \pi}$. The received power is the product:

$$
P_r = \frac{P_t G^2 \lambda^2 \sigma}{(4 \pi)^3 R^4}
$$

The echo competes with the noise of the receiver. If the radar adds up the echo
coherently over a time $T$, the signal-to-noise ratio is

$$
\text{SNR} = \frac{P_t G^2 \lambda^2 \sigma T}{(4 \pi)^3 R^4 k T_0 F L}
$$

where $k T_0$ is $4 \cdot 10^{-21}$ joule, $F$ is the noise figure of the receiver and $L$
collects the losses. A reliable detection with few false alarms needs a ratio of about 13
dB. Solving for the range:

$$
R_{\max} = \left( \frac{P_t G^2 \lambda^2 \sigma T}{(4 \pi)^3 k T_0 F L \cdot \text{SNR}_{\min}} \right)^{\frac{1}{4}}
$$

Take a small radar: 10 W, an antenna gain of 30 dB, $9.4~\text{GHz}$, 20 ms on the
target, a noise figure of 5 dB and 6 dB of losses. For a drone with a cross-section of
0.01 square metre, which is the value measured for small quadcopters
[[14]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/), the formula gives 5.7 km.

Right: signal-to-noise ratio against range for a drone and a small bird, falling by 12 dB for each doubling of range and crossing the 13 dB detection line at 5.7 km and 3.2 km.">

The radar equation for a small radar. Left: detection range against the size of the target. Right: the signal-to-noise ratio of a drone and of a bird against range. Range falls only slowly with the size of the target, so a radar that sees drones sees birds as well.

</Figure>

The fourth root in this formula explains most of what radar can and cannot do.

- **A small target costs less range than expected.** A drone has a cross-section a
  thousand times smaller than a light aircraft. The range is only 5.6 times shorter.
- **More power buys little.** Doubling the range takes 16 times the power.
- **Sensitivity is not the problem.** The same radar detects a small bird at 3.2 km. A
  radar that can see drones sees every bird within kilometres, and there are far more
  birds than drones. In flight, the cross-sections of small drones and of birds overlap,
  so the strength of the echo cannot separate them [[14]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/).

The real limits are elsewhere. The echo of the ground, of buildings and of the sea is
millions of times stronger than that of a drone. A radar removes it with the Doppler
shift, because the ground does not move. But a drone that hovers, or that flies across the
beam, has no radial speed either, and it disappears together with the ground. The second
limit is the line of sight. A drone that flies low behind a hill, a building or a ship is
not illuminated at all.

## 4.3 Radar: telling a drone from a bird

The body of a drone gives one Doppler line. The rotors add something a bird does not
have [[30]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/)[[13]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/). A blade of length $L$ that turns
$f_{rot}$ times per second has a tip speed

$$
v_{tip} = 2 \pi f_{rot} L
$$

For a blade of 12 cm at 100 turns per second this is 75 m/s. Points along the blade move
at every speed between zero and $v_{tip}$, towards the radar on one side of the hub and
away from it on the other. Twice per turn the blade is broadside to the radar, and at that
moment the whole blade reflects at once. The radar sees a short flash that covers all
Doppler shifts up to

$$
f_{d,\max} = \frac{2 v_{tip}}{\lambda} = \frac{4 \pi f_{rot} L}{\lambda}
$$

which is $4.7~\text{kHz}$ here. A rotor with two blades flashes 200 times per second.

<Figure video="/media/three-pixels-in-the-sky/micro_doppler_rotor.mp4" poster="/media/three-pixels-in-the-sky/micro_doppler_rotor.webp" ratio="1600 / 640" width={100} alt="Left: a two-bladed rotor seen from above with a radar wave arriving from below; the blade half that moves towards the radar is red, the other half blue. Right: Doppler shift against time over 30 ms, with a bright vertical stripe from minus 4.7 to plus 4.7 kHz every 5 ms, each time the blade is broadside to the radar.">

One rotor and the signal it returns, simulated at $9.4~\text{GHz}$. Each blade gives a flash twice per turn. The height of the flash gives the tip speed and the rhythm gives the rotor speed.

</Figure>

A bird has a body line too, and wings that beat a few times per second. Their tips move
at a few metres per second, which gives a slow wave of a few hundred hertz around the body
line. The two patterns differ by a factor of ten in width and of thirty in rhythm.

<Figure src="/media/three-pixels-in-the-sky/micro_doppler.png" width={100} alt="Two spectrograms over 120 ms on the same axes. Left, a quadcopter: a bright body line near 0.5 kHz and a band of repeating vertical flashes from minus 4.7 to plus 4.7 kHz around it. Right, a bird: only the body line with a slight slow widening.">

Simulated echo of a quadcopter with four rotors and of a bird, both flying towards the radar at 8 m/s. Rotors spread the echo over kilohertz and wings over a few hundred hertz. This is the feature that separates a drone from a bird.

</Figure>

Three consequences follow from the formulas, and they are not obvious from a datasheet.

- **The radar must be built for it.** To see shifts of $4.7~\text{kHz}$ on both sides
  without ambiguity it has to sample the echo more than 9 400 times per second. To see
  the flashes repeat it has to stay on the target for several turns of the rotor, so tens
  of milliseconds. A radar that scans the horizon quickly cannot do both.
- **Recognition reaches less far than detection.** The blades are thin and mostly
  plastic, so their echo is much weaker than that of the body with its battery and
  motors. If it is 20 dB weaker, the fourth root turns that into a range that is
  3.2 times shorter: 1.8 km for the radar above, where it detects at 5.7 km. Between those
  two ranges the radar has a target and cannot say what it is.
- **A hovering drone is still visible.** Its body line sits at zero and is removed with
  the ground, but the blade flashes are not at zero. A radar that keeps them can find a
  drone that stands still.

Two further ideas come from the literature. The polarisation of the echo differs between
a bird and a drone, which gives a second feature next to the rotor pattern
[[52]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/). Both features were measured on real birds and a small drone by a British and
Norwegian team [[62]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/). And a radar does not have to transmit. A passive radar uses the
signal of an existing television or mobile-network transmitter and listens for its echo
[[50]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/). It needs no licence to transmit and cannot be found by its own
signal.

## 4.4 Radio: what a drone transmits

A remotely controlled drone takes part in up to three radio links.

| Link | Sent by | Typical form | What a listener gets |
|---|---|---|---|
| Control | the pilot's controller | narrow, hops between frequencies, about $1~\text{MHz}$ wide | that a controller is active, and where the pilot is |
| Video and telemetry | the drone | wide, 10 to $20~\text{MHz}$, at 2.4 or $5.8~\text{GHz}$ | that a drone is in the air, and its direction |
| Remote identification | the drone | a short broadcast over Wi-Fi or Bluetooth | the drone's own position, its serial number, often the pilot's position |

The third link is required by law in Europe [[10]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/) and is standardised
[[35]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/). The largest manufacturer also sends its own version, which has been
decoded and shown to carry the positions of both the drone and the pilot
[[31]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/). A receiver that decodes these broadcasts gets a position for free.
The same study shows the limit: the broadcast can be switched off or falsified by someone
who wants to. So it sorts the careless from the rest, and nothing more.

## 4.5 Radio: how far a receiver hears

A receiver does not need an echo. The wave travels one way, so the power falls with the
square of the distance and not with the fourth power. This is the Friis formula
[[34]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/):

$$
P_r = P_t G_t G_r \left( \frac{\lambda}{4 \pi d} \right)^2
$$

Engineers write it in decibels. The loss between two antennas $d$ apart is

$$
L_{fs} = 20 \log_{10} \frac{4 \pi d}{\lambda}
$$

which is 100 dB at 1 km for $2.44~\text{GHz}$. A video link that radiates 100 mW (20 dBm)
then arrives at a receiving antenna with a gain of 8 dB at a level of

$$
20 - 100 + 8 = {-72}~\text{dBm}
$$

The noise in the receiver is $kT_0$ per hertz, which is $-174$ dBm, times the bandwidth,
times its noise figure. For $10~\text{MHz}$ and 6 dB:

$$
N = -174 + 10 \log_{10} 10^7 + 6 = {-98}~\text{dBm}
$$

The signal is 26 dB above the noise at 1 km. If the detector needs 10 dB, the link is
heard out to 6.2 km in free space.

Right: a map of the position error of two receivers 1 km apart that each measure a bearing with 5 degrees error; the error is under 200 m within about 1 km and grows beyond 1 km at 3 km distance.">

Left: how far a receiver hears a drone's video link, for three kinds of terrain. Right: the position error when two receivers each give a bearing of 5 degrees. The terrain decides the range, and two bearings locate well only within about the spacing of the receivers.

</Figure>

Free space is the best case. Near the ground the wave also arrives by reflection, and the
two paths partly cancel. Measured over longer distances, the power then falls with a
higher power of the distance than two. With an exponent of 2.7 the range is 2.1 km, and
with 3.5, which is typical between buildings, 1.1 km. That is the range the commercial
receivers are specified for [[11]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/)[[12]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/). The formula also
shows why the narrow control link carries much further than the video link: a tenth of
the bandwidth lets in a tenth of the noise.

In practice the limit is often not the noise of the receiver. The drone uses the same
band as every Wi-Fi network, and the detector has to pick out one transmitter among
hundreds. Two approaches are described in the literature. One recognises the signal by
its structure, such as its hopping pattern or its frame timing. The other learns the
small imperfections of each type of transmitter [[33]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/)[[51]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/).

## 4.6 Radio: finding the signal

The link budget says how strong the signal is. Finding it is a second problem, because
the receiver does not know the frequency, the timing or the format in advance, and the
band is full of other transmitters.

**What the receiver sees.** A software-defined radio turns a slice of the spectrum into
a stream of samples. A sampling rate $f_s$ covers a bandwidth of $f_s$. The band at
$2.4~\text{GHz}$ is $83.5~\text{MHz}$ wide and the one at $5.8~\text{GHz}$ is wider
still, so a small receiver sees only part of a band at a time and has to step through
it. Cutting the stream into short pieces and taking the spectrum of each gives a picture
of power against frequency and time, in which each kind of transmitter has its own
shape.

<Figure src="/media/three-pixels-in-the-sky/rf_waterfall.png" width={100} alt="Left: a waterfall picture of the 2.4 GHz band over 120 ms. A Wi-Fi network appears as irregular horizontal bars 20 MHz wide on the left, a drone video link as a regular ladder of bars 10 MHz wide near 2457 MHz, a control link as small bright squares at a different frequency every 11 ms, and Bluetooth as faint short dashes all over the band. Right: probability of detection against signal-to-noise ratio for an energy detector with 100, 1000, 10 000 and 100 000 samples, reaching 90 percent at minus 3, minus 8, minus 14 and minus 19 dB.">

Left: how a drone's two links look next to a Wi-Fi network and Bluetooth, drawn schematically. Right: how weak a signal an energy detector can find, for different numbers of samples. A drone is recognised by the pattern of its signal and not by its strength, and averaging more samples reaches signals far below the noise.

</Figure>

**Is there energy?** The simplest detector measures the power in a piece of the picture
and compares it with the noise. For $N$ samples $x_n$ the measured power is

$$
T = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=1}^{N} | x_n |^2
$$

Without a signal, $T$ scatters around the noise power $\sigma^2$ with a spread of
$\frac{\sigma^2}{\sqrt{N}}$. A signal raises the mean by its own power, so it is found
when its power exceeds a few times that spread. The signal-to-noise ratio that is needed
therefore falls with the square root of the number of samples. For one false alarm in a
thousand and nine detections out of ten, it is $-3$ dB for 100 samples and $-14$ dB for
10 000. This has a limit. The detector has to know the noise power, and when that is
uncertain by even a fraction of a decibel, no amount of averaging finds a signal below
that uncertainty [[65]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/).

**Is it a drone?** Energy alone does not say what transmits. The shape does.

- **A control link hops.** It sends a short burst, about $1~\text{MHz}$ wide, and moves
  to another frequency for the next one, in a pattern that looks random. The time
  between hops and the length of a burst are fixed for a given make of controller.
- **A video link is wide and regular.** It fills 10 to $20~\text{MHz}$ and repeats with
  the rhythm of its frames. A Wi-Fi network has the same width, but it sends when there
  is data and stays quiet in between.
- **Bluetooth hops too**, but 1 600 times per second and with very short bursts.

A classifier can work on these measured properties, or directly on the picture
[[33]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/)[[51]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/).

**Using what is known about the signal.** Most video links use the same modulation as
Wi-Fi and mobile phones (OFDM). Every symbol of such a signal starts with a copy of its
own last part. If the received signal $x$ is multiplied with a copy of itself that is
delayed by one symbol length $T_u$, the copies line up and add, while noise does not:

$$
R(T_u) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n} x_n \overline{x_{n + T_u}}
$$

This finds the signal below the noise, and the delay at which it peaks gives the symbol
length, which tells one family of links from another. Many links also begin every frame
with a fixed sequence. A receiver that knows the sequence correlates against it and gains
a factor equal to its length. This is how the broadcast of the largest manufacturer was
found and decoded [[31]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/).

**Reading the identification.** The remote identification of section 4.4 is sent as
ordinary Wi-Fi or Bluetooth messages [[35]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/). A receiver chip that costs a few euros
reads them, so this layer of a detection system can be placed in large numbers.

**Drone or pilot.** The video link comes from the drone and the control link from the
pilot on the ground. A receiver that separates the two gets two bearings, one in the air
and one on the ground, and the second is often the more useful one.

**What makes it hard.** A hopping link is only caught when the receiver happens to look
at the right part of the band, so a narrow receiver misses most bursts. A drone that
flies over the mobile network looks like a telephone. And everything in this section
needs the drone to transmit.

## 4.7 Radio: from a signal to a position

**A bearing from the phase.** Two antennas a distance $a$ apart receive the same wave
with a phase difference that depends on the direction $\theta$ it comes from:

$$
\Delta \varphi = \frac{2 \pi a \sin \theta}{\lambda}
$$

Noise disturbs the measured phase by about $\frac{1}{\sqrt{\text{SNR}}}$ radians, so the
bearing has an error of

$$
\sigma_\theta = \frac{\lambda}{2 \pi a \cos \theta \sqrt{\text{SNR}}}
$$

With the antennas half a wavelength apart (6 cm) and a ratio of 10 dB this is 5.8
degrees, which is what the datasheets state. Moving the antennas to four wavelengths
apart gives 0.7 degrees, but then several directions give the same phase. Practical
direction finders therefore combine several spacings, or use an array with an algorithm
such as MUSIC [[32]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/). The error grows towards the ends of the array,
where $\cos \theta$ goes to zero, and reflections from buildings add errors that no
amount of signal removes.

**A position from two bearings.** Two receivers each give a line, and the drone is where
the lines cross. The right panel of the figure above shows how good that crossing is for
receivers 1 km apart. At 1 km in front of them the error is 170 m. At 2 km it is 540 m
and at 3 km 1.2 km, because the lines become almost parallel. A pair of receivers locates
well only within about its own spacing.

**A position from arrival times.** Three or more receivers with a common clock compare
when the same signal arrives. Each time difference $\Delta t$ places the drone on a
hyperbola, and an error in time becomes an error in distance of

$$
\sigma_d = c \sigma_t
$$

Ten nanoseconds is 3 m. This is far better than bearings at long range, and it improves
with the bandwidth of the signal, which favours the wide video link. The cost is that the
receivers need clocks that agree to nanoseconds, usually from satellite navigation, and a
data link between them.

**What a radio receiver cannot do.** It hears nothing from a drone that does not
transmit. A drone on a programmed route is silent, and so is one that is steered through
an optical fibre [[9]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/).

## 4.8 Heat: what a thermal camera sees

Every object radiates because it is warm. Planck's law gives the radiance at each
wavelength for a temperature $T$:

$$
B_\lambda(T) = \frac{2 h c^2}{\lambda^5} \cdot \frac{1}{e^{\frac{h c}{\lambda k T}} - 1}
$$

The peak lies at a wavelength of $\frac{2898}{T}$ micrometre. For the sun at 5 800 K that
is 0.5 micrometre, which is why eyes and ordinary cameras work there. For an object at
300 K it is 9.7 micrometre. A thermal camera for the band from 8 to 14 micrometre (long
wave infrared, LWIR) therefore sees objects by their own radiation and needs no light.

<Figure src="/media/three-pixels-in-the-sky/thermal.png" width={100} alt="Two panels. Left: Planck curves against wavelength from 0 to 16 micrometre for a warm drone part at 310 K, air at 283 K and a clear sky at 233 K, with the visible, mid-wave and long-wave bands shaded; the warm curves peak near 10 micrometre inside the long-wave band. Right: apparent temperature difference in one pixel against range on a logarithmic axis for four cases, falling below the detection line of 0.25 K at 1.8 km for a patch 10 K warmer in clear air, 3.5 km for 50 K against a clear sky, 1.4 km in haze and 0.4 km in fog.">

Left: thermal radiation of a warm part, of the air and of a clear sky. Right: a model of how the signal of a small warm patch falls with range. Once the patch is smaller than a pixel its signal falls with the square of the range. Fog limits a thermal camera, and darkness does not.

</Figure>

**The signal is a small difference.** At 300 K, one kelvin changes the radiance in the
LWIR band by 1.5 %. An uncooled thermal camera resolves about 0.05 K, so it is a sensitive
instrument, but what it measures is contrast with the background and not temperature.

**What the contrast of a drone is.** The motors, the speed controllers and the battery of
a flying drone are warmer than the air. The plastic shell around them is close to air
temperature. The background matters as much as the drone. A clear sky overhead radiates
very little in this band and looks tens of degrees colder than the air. Towards the
horizon the camera looks through much more air, and the sky there looks almost as warm
as the air itself. Low cloud does too. So a drone high in a clear sky stands out even
when it is cold, and a drone low over the horizon or under cloud stands out only through
its warm parts. A drone that approaches a site does so at a low angle. Birds are warm as
well, so heat alone does not separate the two [[40]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/).

**A thermal camera has few pixels.** A typical uncooled sensor has 640 by 512 pixels of
12 micrometre. With a lens of 35 mm one pixel covers 0.34 milliradian and the whole image
12.6 degrees. A 0.30 m drone at 300 m is 2.9 pixels wide. The daylight camera of chapter 2 covers 67
degrees with pixels of 0.69 milliradian. Per pixel the thermal camera is twice as sharp,
but it would take five of them to watch the same part of the sky.

The pixels cannot simply be made smaller. Diffraction blurs a point to an angle of

$$
\theta = 1.22 \frac{\lambda}{D}
$$

for a lens opening $D$. The wavelength is twenty times longer than for visible light. For
the 35 mm lens above the blur is 0.35 milliradian, the size of one pixel. A sharper
thermal image needs a larger lens, and these lenses are made of germanium because glass
does not let this radiation through.

**How far it reaches.** Beyond about 350 m the warm parts of the drone are smaller than
one pixel. The pixel then averages the patch, of area $A$, with the background around it,
and the air absorbs part of the radiation on the way. The temperature difference the
camera sees is

$$
\Delta T_{app} = \Delta T \cdot \frac{A}{(\theta_{px} R)^2} \cdot e^{-\beta R}
$$

where $\theta_{px}$ is the angle of one pixel and $\beta$ the extinction of the air. The
table below requires five times the noise of the camera, 0.25 K, for a warm patch of 12 cm.

| Case | Assumed | Range |
|---|---|---|
| Warm parts against a background at air temperature, clear air | 10 K difference | 1.8 km |
| Drone against a clear sky overhead | 50 K difference | 3.5 km |
| Haze, visibility 2 km | 10 K, three times the extinction | 1.4 km |
| Fog, visibility 200 m | 10 K, forty times the extinction | 0.4 km |

These ranges are upper limits. The model only contains the noise of the camera. In a real
image the edges of clouds and the horizon produce far more false candidates than that
noise, as chapter 2 showed for the daylight camera. A study with real drones found that a
thermal camera detected them as well as a daylight camera of higher resolution, at ranges
of a few hundred metres [[15]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/).

**Fog.** The visibility $V$ that a weather report gives is tied to the extinction for
visible light by $V = \frac{3.9}{\beta}$ [[45]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/). In haze the particles are
much smaller than 10 micrometre, and the long wave passes them almost undisturbed. In
dense fog the droplets are about as large as the wavelength, and most of the advantage is
lost. The table assumes that the thermal camera suffers 40 % of the extinction that
visible light does in fog. A thermal camera sees through darkness completely and through
haze well, and through thick fog only a little better than the eye.

## 4.9 Other sensors

| Sensor | What it adds | Why it is not a first choice |
|---|---|---|
| Laser rangefinder or lidar | range to centimetres, which is the dimension a camera lacks | the beam is narrow, so it has to be pointed by another sensor first |
| Cooled mid-wave infrared camera | twice the contrast per kelvin and sharper optics than LWIR | cost, and a cooler that wears out |
| Short-wave infrared camera | sees through haze better than a daylight camera | needs light, like a daylight camera |
| Receiver for aircraft transponders (ADS-B) | removes the aircraft from the list of unknown objects | drones do not carry these transponders |

## 4.10 What each sensor contributes

| | Radar | Radio receiver | Thermal camera | Daylight camera (chapter 2) | Microphone (chapter 3) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Works against a silent drone | yes | no | yes | yes | yes |
| Range for a small drone | kilometres | 1 to 2 km near the ground | hundreds of metres to 1 to 2 km | 130 to 300 m with the camera of chapter 2 | tens of metres for a Crazyflie |
| Gives direction | 1 to 2 degrees | about 5 degrees | a fraction of a pixel | a fraction of a pixel | tens of degrees |
| Gives range | yes, to metres | only with several sites | no | no | no |
| Tells drone from bird | by the rotor pattern, at shorter range | yes, a bird does not transmit | hardly | with enough pixels | by the rotor tones |
| Fails when | no line of sight, hovering target, many birds | the drone does not transmit | thick fog, low over a warm background | night, fog, haze | wind, rain, background noise |
| Reveals itself | yes | no | no | no | no |

No column has a yes in every row. The two long-range sensors fail in opposite cases: the
radio receiver against a silent drone, the radar against a drone behind terrain. The

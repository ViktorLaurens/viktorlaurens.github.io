Chapters 2 to 4 looked at one sensor at a time. This chapter combines them. A small model
shows how the alerts of different sensors should be added up when each sensor fails in
some kind of weather, a tracker is tested on the real tracks of chapter 2, and one flight
is followed from 3 km to the site.

The camera and microphone numbers in the model are the measurements of chapters 2 and 3.
The two long-range sensors are assumptions based on the literature.

## 5.1 The long-range sensors

The camera of chapter 2 first holds a 0.30 m drone at 127 to 186 m, and the microphone of
chapter 3 hears a Crazyflie at tens of metres. Radar and radio receivers reach
kilometres [[5]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/)[[8]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/). According to the calculations of chapter 4, a radio receiver hears a 100 mW video link
at 1 to 2 km near the ground and gives a bearing with an error of about 5 degrees, which
is also what commercial sensors are specified for [[11]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/)[[12]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/). It hears nothing from
a drone that does not transmit [[9]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/). A small radar detects a drone of 0.01 square metre
at several kilometres and gives range and bearing at once, but it needs a line of sight
and it sees every bird as well [[14]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/)[[13]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/).

In the model below, the radio receiver reports a transmitting drone in 80 % of the seconds
within 2 km, decreasing to zero at 3 km. The radar reports any drone in 70 % of the seconds
within 1.5 km, decreasing to zero at 2.5 km. These two curves are assumptions within the range of the published figures.

## 5.2 Why a zoom camera needs to be pointed

A camera can see a small drone at 2 km, but only through a narrow window. To get four
pixels on a 0.30 m drone at 2 km, the formula of section 2.2 gives a field of view of 8
degrees for a sensor that is 3840 pixels wide. The full horizon then takes 45 windows, and
every elevation band doubles that number. If the camera scans blindly at one window per
second, the drone has flown a kilometre before the camera returns to the same window.

With a radio bearing that is accurate to 5 degrees, the same camera needs one or two
windows per elevation band. So the long-range sensors and the camera do not compete. The
long-range sensors tell the camera where to look.

## 5.3 Adding up weak evidence

Every sensor is treated in the same way. Once per second it either reports something or it
does not. It reports a drone that is really there with probability $p_d$, and it reports
something that is not a drone with probability $p_f$. A report then counts as

$$
\ell = \ln \tfrac{p_d}{p_f}
$$

and a second without a report counts as $\ln \tfrac{1 - p_d}{1 - p_f}$, which is negative.
This is the log-likelihood ratio of sequential testing [[24]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/). The fusion adds these
values over all sensors and over time, and never lets the sum drop below zero, as in a
cumulative-sum test [[25]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/):

$$
S_t = \max(0, S_{t - 1} + \sum_i \ell_{i,t})
$$

This sum has a useful property. As long as there is no drone, every term is negative on
average and the sum stays near zero. Once a drone is present, the terms are positive on
average, and the sum climbs at a rate equal to the information each second carries. The
time to an alarm is roughly the threshold divided by that rate, and among all tests with
the same rate of false alarms this one has the shortest delay in the worst case
[[63]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/).

It declares a drone when $S_t$ exceeds a threshold. The threshold comes from running the
model without a drone and allowing one false declaration in 24 hours. A sensor with few
false reports gets a large $\ell$ and can almost declare a drone on its own. A sensor with
many false reports gets a small one and needs support from the others.

The inputs are:

| Sensor | Reports a drone that is there | False reports | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Camera | the detection curve against range from chapter 2 at contrast 0.4; contrast 0.2 in haze; nothing at night | 0.02 per second | measured: 11 tracks of one second or more in 10 minutes of empty sky |
| Microphone | the rotor-tone detector from chapter 3 with a margin of 40 dB; 20 dB in wind | 0.001 per second; 0.01 in wind | detector as designed; the wind values are assumed |
| Radio receiver | 80 % within 2 km, if the drone transmits | 0.001 per second | assumed |
| Radar | 70 % within 1.5 km | 0.01 per second | assumed |

A drone flies straight in from 4 km at 12 m/s, 400 times for each case. Two ways are compared
of weighting the reports. With **fixed weights** every report counts as much as it would
on a clear and calm day. With **weights that follow the conditions** a report counts as
much as it is worth at that moment. A report from the camera at night then counts for
nothing, because at night the camera only produces false reports, and a report from the
microphone in wind counts for less.

## 5.4 What the model shows

<Figure src="/media/three-pixels-in-the-sky/fusion_ranges.png" width={85} alt="Bar chart of the range at which a drone is declared, for four kinds of weather and four ways of using the camera and the microphone. Clear and calm: camera alone 148 m, microphone alone 76 m, both with fixed weights 124 m, both with weights that follow the conditions 148 m. Haze: 88, 76, 88 and 100 m. Night: camera never, microphone 76 m, fixed weights 64 m, following weights 76 m. Wind: camera 148 m, microphone never, fixed 124 m, following 148 m.">

Median range at which a drone is declared, with camera and microphone only, alone and together. The model works in steps of 12 m. Together they give no more range by day, but there is no weather left in which the system is blind.

</Figure>

The figure shows three things.

- **Each sensor has a kind of weather in which it never declares a drone.** For the camera
  that is night, for the microphone wind.
- **With weights that follow the conditions, the pair is as good as its better sensor in
  every kind of weather.** In haze it is slightly better (100 m against 88 m), because
  both sensors are weak there and their evidence adds up. The system does not need to know
  beforehand which sensor will be the good one that day.
- **With fixed weights, the pair is worse than its better sensor.** It reaches 124 m
  against 148 m by day and 64 m against 76 m at night, and 3 % of the night flights are
  not declared at all before 30 m. The false reports of the camera at night are counted
  as if they meant something, so the threshold has to go up for everything.

So combining the sensors well does not give more range. It makes sure that there is no
kind of weather in which the system is blind, without losing anything in the weather where
one sensor would have been enough.

With the two assumed long-range sensors added, a drone that transmits is declared at about
2.6 km and a drone that does not at about 1.9 km, in every kind of weather. The radio
receiver handles the first case and the radar the second. The camera and the microphone
then no longer decide when a drone is declared. They decide what it is and where exactly
it is, which is the subject of the next section.

## 5.5 What a report is worth in metres

A bearing with an error $\sigma_\theta$ (in radians) at range $r$ places the drone within

$$
e = r \sigma_\theta
$$

across the line of sight. It says nothing about the distance along the line of sight.

| Report | Bearing error | Across the line of sight | Along it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Radio receiver at 2.6 km | 5 degrees (specified) | 230 m | unknown from one site |
| Radar at 1.9 km | 1 degree (assumed) | 33 m | metres to tens of metres |
| The camera of chapter 2 at 150 m | one pixel, 0.035 degrees | 0.1 m | unknown from one camera |
| Two phones at 50 m | a few degrees at best (chapter 3) | several metres | unknown |

The bearing of the camera is more than a hundred times finer than that of the radio
receiver, but the camera gives no range at all. Two cameras give range by triangulation.
The error grows with the square of the distance and decreases with the baseline $B$
between the cameras:

$$
\sigma_r = \tfrac{\sqrt{2} r^2 \sigma_\theta}{B}
$$

For a drone at 150 m and an error of one pixel in each camera, this gives about 100 m for
a baseline of 0.2 m, 10 m for 2 m, and 1 m for 20 m. The two cameras of chapter 2 are mounted a
hand's width apart on one case, so as a pair they cannot measure the range of a drone at
any useful distance. Twenty metres apart, they could.

The chain for the rough location is then as follows. A radio bearing or a radar detection
gives a box of hundreds of metres at a distance of kilometres. A camera that is pointed
into that box reduces it to decimetres across the line of sight. A second site, a radar
range or a laser rangefinder gives the missing dimension.

## 5.6 A track with an uncertainty

A detection is a point in one frame. What the next sensor needs is a track: where the
object is now, how it moves, and how sure the system is. This section runs a Kalman
filter [[44]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/)[[28]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/) on the tracks of chapter 2 and tests whether its uncertainty can
be trusted. One camera measures a direction and no range, so the filter works in the
image.

**The model.** The state holds the position $p$ in the image and its velocity $v$,
$x = (p, v)$. Between two frames, a time $\Delta t$ apart, the filter assumes a constant
velocity that is disturbed by a random acceleration of strength $q$:

$$
p_{k+1} = p_k + \Delta t \cdot v_k, \qquad v_{k+1} = v_k + w_k, \qquad \text{var}(w_k) = q \Delta t
$$

In matrix form this is $x_{k+1} = F x_k + w_k$ with noise covariance $Q$. A detection
measures the position with an error of $r$ pixels in each direction: $z_k = H x_k + n_k$,
where $H$ picks the position out of the state and $n_k$ has covariance $R = r^2 I$.

**Predict.** The filter carries an estimate $\hat{x}$ and its covariance $P$, the
uncertainty of that estimate. Without a new measurement both are moved forward with the
model, and the uncertainty grows:

$$
\hat{x}^- = F \hat{x}, \qquad P^- = F P F^T + Q
$$

**Update.** A detection $z$ differs from what the filter expected by the innovation
$\nu$, and the filter knows how large that difference should be:

$$
\nu = z - H \hat{x}^-, \qquad S = H P^- H^T + R
$$

The estimate is corrected by a fraction $K$ of the innovation:

$$
K = P^- H^T S^{-1}, \qquad \hat{x} = \hat{x}^- + K \nu, \qquad P = (I - K H) P^-
$$

The gain $K$ is the choice that makes the remaining uncertainty $P$ as small as
possible. In one dimension it reads $K = \frac{P^-}{P^- + R}$, which shows what it does.
When the prediction is uncertain compared with the measurement, $K$ is close to 1 and the
filter follows the detection. When the measurement is noisy, $K$ is close to 0 and the
filter keeps its prediction. For linear models with Gaussian noise no other estimator
has a smaller error. For numerical safety the code uses the equivalent form
$P = (I - KH) P^- (I - KH)^T + K R K^T$, which stays symmetric.

**Is the uncertainty right?** The innovation gives one test value per frame:

$$
\epsilon_k = \nu_k^T S_k^{-1} \nu_k
$$

If the filter describes the target and the detector correctly, $\epsilon_k$ follows a
chi-square distribution with two degrees of freedom: its mean is 2, and 95 % of the
values lie below 6. When the true position is known, the same value can be computed with
the true error and $P$ in place of $\nu$ and $S$. These are the standard consistency
tests [[28]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/).

**Choosing $q$ and $r$.** The innovations also give the likelihood of the detections
under the model,

$$
\ln L(q, r) = - \frac{1}{2} \sum_k \left( \nu_k^T S_k^{-1} \nu_k + \ln \det ( 2 \pi S_k ) \right)
$$

and $q$ and $r$ are chosen to maximise it for each track.

**Predicting without detections.** After a time $h$ without a detection, the variance of
the predicted position is

$$
\sigma_p^2(h) = P_{pp} + 2 h P_{pv} + h^2 P_{vv} + \frac{q h^3}{3}
$$

The first three terms carry the uncertainty the filter already had, and the last is what
the target may have done in the meantime. For long gaps the spread grows with
$h^{\frac{3}{2}}$, so doubling the gap almost triples the area to search.

There are six tracks. Five are real: the aircraft of recording B (1288 detections in
43 seconds) and four birds (between 37 and 170 detections). The sixth is a pasted
drone that flies in from 272 m to 168 m, for which the true position is known in every
frame. Deciding which detection belongs to which track is a subject of
its own [[48]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/). Here the tracks are taken as the detector formed them.

| Bird 1 | 0.65 px | 8.5 | 2.11 | 94 % |

From one frame to the next, the filter is consistent on every track. Three further tests
show where that stops being enough.

**1. Consistent on the next frame is not consistent seconds ahead.** A cue for a zoom
camera or for a friendly drone is a prediction over seconds. With the detections of the
aircraft hidden for a few seconds, the filter predicts, and the prediction can be
compared with where the detector saw the aircraft afterwards. After 5 seconds the filter claimed an
error of 31 pixels and the real miss was 6 pixels. It was five times too cautious.
The reason is in the detector. Its position errors are not independent from frame to
frame. On the pasted drone, where the truth is known, the error in one frame has a
correlation of 0.54 with the error in the next. The filter can only explain such a slow
wobble as a manoeuvre of the target, and a manoeuvre grows over time where a wobble does
not. When $q$ is chosen so that the prediction 2 seconds ahead is consistent, it becomes
215 times smaller. After 10 seconds without detections the filter then claims 0.25 degrees,
the median miss is 0.25 degrees, and 97 % of the predictions fall inside the 95 % region.
But the test on the next frame now fails, with a mean of 3.1. A filter with white noise
cannot be right on both time scales, so it has to be tuned for the time scale on which
its output is used. The proper solution is to give the slow error of the detector its
own state in the filter: an error $e_k = \rho e_{k-1} + u_k$ with $\rho$ near 0.5, estimated
together with the position.

**2. Against the truth the filter is too confident.** For the pasted drone the filtered
position is off by 0.48 pixels, which is 0.019 degrees, and better than a single
detection at 0.65 pixels. But the filter claims less than that. The same test value, now
computed against the true position, has a mean of 7.2 where 2 is expected. With the
detection error measured against the truth instead of fitted, it is 3.1. The cause is
again the correlated error, together with a constant offset of 0.2 pixels in the
vertical direction.

**3. How an object moves says what it is.** The fitted change of velocity differs by a
factor of 46 between the aircraft and the most agile bird. The classifier of chapter 2
looks at the pixels of one crop and fails on small targets. This number needs no pixels
on the target at all. A real drone cannot be placed on this scale yet, because the path
of the pasted drone comes from a model.

For a bird the model itself is wrong. After 2 seconds without detections only 50 % of
the predictions for bird 3 are inside the 95 % region. A bird does not fly at a constant
velocity with small disturbances. It turns.

The right panel of the figure above gives the practical answer. The zoom camera of
section 5.2 has a window of 8 degrees, so a cue is useful while its 95 % region is
smaller than 4 degrees. For the aircraft that is the case for more than 10 seconds, for
the pasted drone for about 4 seconds, and for a bird for 1 second. A system that takes
longer than that to turn a camera has to keep detecting while it turns.

## 5.7 One flight, start to finish

To tie the numbers of the chapters together, this section follows one drone of 0.30 m that flies
straight at the site at 12 m/s, by day and in clear weather. It starts 3 km out, which is
250 seconds before it arrives.

Dashed lines mark where the drone is declared: 2.7 km if it transmits, 1.9 km if it is silent, 148 m with camera and microphone alone.">

The first two rows use the assumed curves of section 5.1 and the third the calculations
of chapter 4. The rows below come from the recordings.

Three things stand out. The hardware built for this report gives 12 to 25 seconds of warning, and the
long-range sensors give about three minutes. With these sensors the identity comes last, 4
seconds before the drone arrives, which is too late to be of use. And the range is never
known from these sensors alone.

This is the case for the last step of the chain. Suppose a friendly drone that flies at
20 m/s is sent out when the radar declares the intruder at 1.9 km. The two approach each
other at 32 m/s and meet after 59 seconds, 1.2 km from the site. There its camera has
the 9 pixels that the camera on the ground only gets at 48 m, with a minute and a half
left. The speed of 20 m/s is an assumption.

## 5.8 Limits

- **It is a simple model.** One report per sensor per second, independent from second to
  second, and a drone that flies straight at the site. Chapter 2 showed that the noise of
  the sky is not independent from frame to frame, and real false alerts come in bursts (a
  flock of birds, a gust of wind).
- **The long-range sensors are assumed**, based on a datasheet and a survey. Their curves
  determine the 2.6 km and the 1.9 km, and nothing else supports those two numbers.
- **The weather is assumed as well.** "Haze" is the curve for contrast 0.2, "night" is a
  camera that sees nothing, and "wind" takes 20 dB off the microphone's margin and gives
  it ten times more false reports. The camera curves come from one cloudless afternoon.
- **The system is told what the weather is.** Weights that follow the conditions require
  that the conditions are known. Estimating them from the behaviour of the sensors, for
  example from the rate of false reports in the last minutes, is the real problem. It is not attempted here.
- **The microphone curve is for a Crazyflie against pink noise with an assumed margin**
  (chapter 3).

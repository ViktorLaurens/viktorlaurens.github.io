Chapter 5 ends with a drone that has been declared and a rough position: a box of hundreds
of metres from a long-range sensor, or a precise bearing without range from a camera. Two
questions remain, what the object is and exactly where it is. The best way to answer both
is to bring a sensor closer.

Nothing was built for this chapter. It is calculated from the numbers of the earlier
chapters and described from the literature, and it is the direction in which this work
would continue.

## 6.1 What getting closer gives

All the limits found in this report are limits of distance.

| Limit | At | Chapter |
|---|---|---|
| The camera first holds a 0.30 m drone at contrast 0.4 | 186 m | 2 |
| The classifier separates a drone from a bird in one frame | from 9 pixels, which is 48 m at 1920 x 1080 | 2 |
| The microphone hears a Crazyflie with a 40 dB margin | 88 m | 3 |
| Two cameras 0.2 m apart measure range to within 1 m | 15 m | 5 |

A sensor on a tower has to wait until the drone comes within these distances. A sensor on
a friendly drone can fly there. At 50 m from the target, the same small camera that has
trouble at 190 m has 9 pixels on the target, which is enough for the classifier of chapter
2. Within 15 m, a pair of cameras a hand's width apart, which is useless on the tower,
measures the range to within a metre.

## 6.2 Two cameras that can be pointed

One step closer can be taken without leaving the ground. Two cameras with long lenses,
each on a pan-tilt mount and some distance apart, work like a pair of eyes. Once both
look at the same object, the directions of the two give its distance by triangulation,
and each camera has enough pixels on it to say what it is.

Section 5.2 gave the lens: to put four pixels on a 0.30 m drone at 2 km, the camera sees
a window of 8 degrees, and one pixel is then 0.002 degrees. With the formula of section
5.5, the range error of a pair with baseline $B$ is

$$
\sigma_r = \frac{\sqrt{2} r^2 \sigma_\theta}{B}
$$

| Drone at | Baseline | Range error, one pixel in each camera |
|---|---|---|
| 1 km | 20 m | 2.6 m |
| 2 km | 20 m | 10 m |
| 2 km | 100 m | 2.1 m |

This is the first sensor in this report that gives a range without transmitting anything.
Three conditions come with it.

- **The pair has to be told where to look.** Scanning the sky through a window of 8
  degrees takes too long (section 5.2). A radio bearing, a radar detection or a wide
  camera gives the direction, and both cameras turn to it. They can point almost
  parallel: for a baseline of 20 m and any range beyond 300 m, the directions of the two
  cameras differ by less than 4 degrees, which is inside one window.
- **The pointing has to be known to a pixel.** If the angle of a mount is only known to
  0.01 degrees, the range error at 2 km grows from 10 m to 50 m. The angles therefore
  have to be calibrated on distant landmarks, and checked while the system runs.
- **Both cameras have to expose at the same moment.** A drone that crosses at 15 m/s at
  2 km moves two pixels in 10 milliseconds, so the two exposures have to agree to about
  a millisecond.

Such a pair answers both remaining questions for a target that is in sight and in
daylight. It does not help against a drone behind terrain or in fog.

## 6.3 A sensor that flies to the target

The other way to get closer is to send a sensor. The idea is a small friendly drone that
stands ready in a box at the site, always charged. When the ground sensors declare a
possible drone, it takes off without waiting for the alert to be certain, flies to the
rough position, and looks. It is a mobile mount for a camera, and its first task is to
reduce the uncertainty: is this a drone, which kind, and exactly where is it.

**How soon it is there.** For the flight of section 5.7, an intruder at 12 m/s, and a
friendly drone that needs 20 seconds to leave its box and then flies at 20 m/s:

| Declared at | By | The two meet after | At a distance from the site of |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2.7 km | the radio receiver | 97 s | 1.5 km |
| 1.9 km | the radar | 72 s | 1.0 km |

The launch delay and the speed are assumptions. In both cases the drone meets the
intruder at least a kilometre out, where the camera on the ground would still see a
single pixel.

**What it has to search.** How large an area the drone has to search on arrival depends
on who gave the alert. A radar gives a box of tens of metres, which fits in one image
from 100 m away. A radio bearing gives a strip 230 m wide and no range, so the drone has
to fly along the bearing and search as it goes. This is why the uncertainty of the
ground track matters as much as its position.

**What it gains by being close.** Everything the camera delivers improves in proportion
to the distance.

| Distance to the target | Pixels on a 0.30 m drone | What that allows (section 2.13) |
|---|---|---|
| 190 m | 2 | detection |
| 48 m | 9 | drone or bird |
| 20 m | 22 | which type of drone, and what it carries |

At 50 m, one pixel of the drone's camera is 3 cm on the target. Its bearing crosses the
bearing from the ground at a wide angle, and together they give the position to well
under a metre, in all three directions. From that point on, the accuracy is no longer
set by the cameras but by how well the friendly drone knows its own position.

**What it sends back.** The drone reports a track with its uncertainty and the images
themselves, so that a person can see what the object is before anything is decided. The
drone can then keep the target in view from a distance that holds enough pixels on it,
about 20 m for the last row of the table.

**What the ground has to hand over.** The ground track has to provide three things:
a position, a velocity and an uncertainty. The uncertainty is essential, because it tells
the friendly drone how large an area its own camera has to cover when it arrives.

Two things make the handover harder than it sounds. First, the position of the friendly drone
itself has to be known as accurately as the target position is wanted, and its own rotors
disturb its microphone and shake its camera. Second, the target moves. A quadcopter can
change its velocity within a second, so a track that is handed over is a prediction that
gets older. The uncertainty has to grow with the age of the last measurement. If it does
not, the friendly drone searches in the wrong place and is confident about it. Section
5.6 measured how fast this happens: a cue for an 8 degree window stays
useful for about 4 seconds for a drone and for 1 second for a bird.

Each of these parts has been shown to work. A group at NTNU flew a thermal camera on an
unmanned aircraft, placed each detection on the map with the navigation data of the
aircraft, and followed boats with a Kalman filter and a constant-velocity model, the
same model as in section 5.6. With at least 100 measurements they report a position
accuracy of 5 to 15 m from an altitude of 400 m [[36]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/), and they extended it to a camera
that can be pointed [[37]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/). The error of such a position is the error of the pointing
angle times the distance, plus the error of the aircraft's own navigation, which the
same group has studied in its own right [[38]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/). For a target in the air, the distance is
no longer given by the sea surface, which is what makes this problem harder.

A drone with only a camera measures directions and no distances. From one moving camera
the range does become observable over time, but only if the camera's own path bends
[[49]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/).

Being this close is also the starting point for stopping the intruder. That step is not
part of this report, which ends at knowing what the object is and where it is. The
literature on it starts from classical guidance [[47]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/) and continues in
recent work on small drones [[27]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/).

## 6.4 Where to look next

With one tower and no moving parts there is nothing to decide, because every sensor looks
where it looks. As soon as there is a camera that can be pointed or a drone that can be
sent, there is a decision to make many times per second: which box gets the narrow sensor
now? The track with the largest uncertainty? The track that is most likely a drone? The
one closest to the site that is protected? Every choice occupies the sensor for a moment,
and during that moment the other tracks are not observed and their uncertainty grows.

In the literature this is called sensor management [[26]](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/references/). The next look is chosen so that the
expected uncertainty over all tracks, weighted by importance, decreases the most. This is
the same calculation as in section 5.3, with a different question: section 5.3 asks how
much a report is worth, and sensor management asks how much a report from a certain place
would be worth. The model of chapter 5 already has two of the ingredients, a sum of
evidence per track and a weight per sensor that follows the conditions. What it does not
have yet is the geometry and the timing.

This is also where learning methods are most useful. In the detector, chapter 2
showed that classical signal processing did most of the work. The choices are a better
fit: which look, which route for the friendly drone, and when to stop looking and report.

## 6.5 What comes next

These are the next steps, ordered by how much doubt each one removes:

1. **A real drone in front of the cameras at measured distances.** Every range in chapter 2
   depends on pasted targets, and the classifier has never seen a real drone on this
   camera.
2. **A calibrated microphone outdoors**, so that the range in chapter 3 no longer depends
   on an assumed margin, and two microphones on one clock for a bearing.
3. **The detector before the encoder, running live on the Jetson.** Chapter 2 estimates
   the gain at a quarter of the range.
4. **More kinds of sky**: clouds, dusk, rain. False alerts come from there, and that is
   where the weights of chapter 5 would prove their value.
5. **A second camera site** twenty metres away, to measure range by triangulation.
6. **Conditions that are estimated, not given**: the fusion of chapter 5 should determine
   the weather itself from the recent behaviour of the sensors.
7. **The friendly drone**: first as a moving camera that confirms a ground alert, later as
   part of the track.
8. **Darkness and cold**: a thermal camera next to the daylight camera, and a test in
   winter conditions (section 1.7).
9. **A filter that models the slow error of the detector**, so that its uncertainty is
   right on every time scale (section 5.6).

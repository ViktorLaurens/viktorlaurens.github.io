## Why arms

Control answers one question: given a trajectory, make the system follow it.
[Pendulum Lab](/projects/pendulum-lab/) ended there. For a robot arm the question becomes
*which trajectory*, under constraints that control never had to think about: the arm must not
hit the table, the bin, the person or the other arm. That is motion planning. It sits above
control, it is global and combinatorial where control is local and continuous, and the field
has answered it in a dozen ways over forty years, each with its own way of failing.

This project starts where the pendulum stopped, with the arm as a dynamical system and the
controllers that make it follow a path, and then asks what manipulators still cannot do in
practice:

1. **Replan in milliseconds in a changing scene, with guarantees.** Safety comes from staying
   inside convex regions of free space, enforced at 1 kHz; the planner searches over those
   regions to find a good route; learning chooses the behaviour, free to be imperfect because
   the layer beneath it is not.
2. **Know when a plan is wrong.** Certificates on the regions and on every returned plan.
3. **Close the loop.** Plans become corridors, followed by controllers with a stated
   disturbance bound.
4. **Coordinate several arms without a central planner.** Each arm plans on its own clock and
   treats the other's committed plan as an obstacle whose motion is known.

The goal for the later chapters is a robot that becomes bolder as it learns, without becoming
less safe.

## How it is made

The same way as Pendulum Lab: one scene, one storyline, every claim with a figure. Everything
is Python, with MuJoCo for the physics and Drake for the convex planning; every figure and
animation is regenerated from the same code, and the results come from simulation. It builds
on my master's thesis on multi-robot motion planning.

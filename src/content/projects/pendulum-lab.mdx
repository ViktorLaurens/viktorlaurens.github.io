## Why a pendulum

A pendulum is the smallest system that has everything: a nonlinear equation with no
closed-form solution, two kinds of equilibria, a real sensor with real outliers, and, in
its Furuta form, fewer motors than degrees of freedom. Every tool of control engineering can
be tried on it and judged against ground truth. This project does exactly that, one chapter
at a time, with every figure and animation generated from the same code.

## The storyline: one problem, four questions

Every chapter works with the same model of the pendulum and its sensor, written per sample $k$:

$$
\mathbf x_{k+1} = \mathbf f(\mathbf x_k, p) + \mathbf w_k,
\qquad
y_k = \theta_k + b + v_k,
\qquad
\mathbf x_k = \begin{bmatrix} \theta_k \cr \dot\theta_k \end{bmatrix}.
$$

It contains four kinds of unknowns:

| unknown | what it is | changes over time |
|---|---|---|
| $\mathbf f$ | the structure of the dynamics: which equation | no |
| $p = (\omega_0^2, \mu, b)$ | the parameters | no |
| $\mathbf x_k$ | the state: angle and angular velocity | every sample |
| $\mathbf w_k$, $v_k$ | what the model and the sensor get wrong | random |

Chapter 1 is the **forward problem**: given $\mathbf f$, $p$ and the initial state, compute
the motion. Every later chapter is an **inverse problem**: given the measurements $y_k$,
infer what is unknown. They all answer the same Bayesian question, *what do the data say
about the unknowns?*, and they differ along three axes:

1. **What is asked.** Constant unknowns (structure, parameters) are *identification*. Every
   new sample adds information about them, so their uncertainty keeps shrinking. The state
   is new at every sample: that is *state estimation*, and it is never finished.
2. **Which data may be used.** *Offline*, after the experiment, the whole recording
   $y_1, \dots, y_N$ is available, future samples included. *Online*, for example inside a
   control loop, only $y_1, \dots, y_k$: the past and the present.
3. **How much structure $\mathbf f$ has.** Known from physics, discovered from a library of
   candidate terms, or a neural network learned from scratch.

| | the state $\mathbf x_k$ | the parameters $p$ | both |
|---|---|---|---|
| **offline** | smoothing: the Savitzky–Golay derivative (2.2), the Rauch–Tung–Striebel smoother (5.8) | regression (2.2), output error (2.4), Bayesian inference (2.5), learning-based methods (3) | output error also estimates the initial state (2.4) |
| **online** | filtering: Kalman filter, EKF, UKF (5.1 to 5.5, 5.7) | | the augmented EKF (5.6) |

### How identification and state estimation connect

Output error (2.4) treats the model as exact, $\mathbf w_k = 0$. Then the parameters and the
initial state fix every later state, and estimating $\mathbf x_0$ *is* estimating the whole
trajectory: identification and state estimation are one problem, and the states stay hidden
inside it. State estimation (chapter 5) lets the model be imperfect, $\mathbf w_k \ne 0$.
The model can then no longer carry the state forward on its own, and the state has to be
re-estimated from the measurements at every sample.

The two meet again inside the Kalman filter. Its innovations give the likelihood of the
measurements for given parameters and noise levels (5.5); maximizing it is the
*prediction-error method* of identification, and output error is its special case without
process noise. Made part of the state, the parameters are identified online (5.6), and at the
end of the recording the filter lands on the offline result of 2.4. Identification and state
estimation are the same posterior, asked about different unknowns with different data.

### Why this order

1. **The pendulum**: the forward problem, the model and its phase space.
2. **Classical identification**: the fewest unknowns. The structure comes from physics, only
   the parameters are unknown, and the whole recording is available.
3. **Learning-based identification**: the same question with a flexible model class.
   A Neural ODE is output error with a neural network in place of the pendulum equation.
4. **Identification benchmark**: all models of chapters 2 and 3, on the same frozen data.
5. **State estimation**: online. It needs a model, any of the identified ones, and estimates
   the state in real time, the input a controller needs.
6. **The Furuta pendulum**: the same questions for a harder, underactuated system.
7. **Control with guarantees**: a model and an estimated state, used to act, with stability
   and safety that can be argued for.
8. **Hybrid learning and control**: control learned from interaction, alone and combined with
   the models and guarantees of chapter 7.
9. **An underwater manipulator**: the whole programme repeated on a thruster-driven arm, to
   see what scales and what does not.

## Every controller, side by side

The [controller gallery](/projects/pendulum-lab/controller-gallery/) runs every controller of
chapters 7 and 8 on the same pendulum at the same time: swinging up from rest, balancing
through a push, and carrying a payload it was never designed for. It is the fastest way to see
what each method buys and what it costs.

## How it was made

Everything is Python. Physics, sensors, estimators and data loading live in one reusable
package; scripts regenerate every static figure from frozen benchmark datasets that are
committed and never edited by hand; the animations are Manim scenes that only draw, with all
physics imported from the same package. Tests check the physics, the data and the report.

Code available on request.

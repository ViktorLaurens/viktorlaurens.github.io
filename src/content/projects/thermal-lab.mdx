## Why heating

Most houses heat every room from one thermostat in the living room, and radiators elsewhere
are switched by hand when someone remembers. This project asks how much energy a house saves
when it knows three things, and how much of the saving comes from each:

1. **Where is the heat?** What a thermal network of the house looks like, which of its
   parameters ordinary data can identify, and how many sensors it takes to know every room.
2. **Where will the people be?** How well occupancy can be forecast an hour ahead, and what a
   forecast is worth in kWh.
3. **How does heat move?** Through walls, doors and stairs; how that coupling limits what
   zoning can save, and what a predictive controller can do about it.

## One house, one score

Every chapter is scored the same way: energy used against comfort lost in the rooms where
people are, against a one-thermostat baseline and zoning on a timetable. The model is a
grey-box thermal network identified from data, with a small learned part for the free heat
from sun and people; the controller is an economic MPC with a forecast of who will be where.
The results are checked on [BOPTEST](https://github.com/ibpsa/project1-boptest), a standard
benchmark for building control, as well as on this project's own simulated houses, and on
measurements from real houses.

## How it is made

The same way as Pendulum Lab: one house, one fixed score, every claim with a figure.
Everything is Python, from the thermal networks to the CFD runs of the air in a room, and
every figure is regenerated from the same code.

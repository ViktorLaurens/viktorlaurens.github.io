## Why a small drone

A drone that costs a few hundred euros can close an airport or damage infrastructure worth
millions, so it has to be detected and located before it does any harm. At the distance
where that still leaves time, it covers a few pixels of a camera image, and no single
sensor finds it reliably: a camera fails in fog and at night, a microphone in wind, a radar
behind terrain, and a radio receiver when the drone does not transmit. This project works
out how the sensors can work together, tests the two that could be built, and calculates
from the physics what the others would add.

## Selected results

Every result is labelled as measured on the two cameras and the phones, modelled on those
recordings, or calculated from the physics. No real drone was flown in front of the
cameras: where a drone appears in a camera test, it is drawn into a real sky recording by a
model.

The video at the top of this page is such a recording with nothing added. In ten minutes
of cloudless sky the detector gives no noise track of two seconds or more, and what it
does follow for seconds is real: a high aircraft and birds a few pixels wide
([2.4](/projects/three-pixels-in-the-sky/02-seeing-a-small-drone/#24-what-the-empty-sky-gives)).

## From a first alert to a position

Detecting a drone is a chain of four questions, and each of them is answered by different
sensors at a different distance.

<Figure src="/media/three-pixels-in-the-sky/chain.png" width={100} alt="Four boxes from left to right connected by arrows. Is something there: radio receiver 1 to 2 km, radar several km, daylight camera 127 to 274 m, microphone 26 to 88 m. Roughly where: radio bearing 5 degrees, radar range and 1 to 2 degrees, camera bearing 0.035 degrees without range. What is it: radar blade flashes to about 1.8 km, rotor tones at 533 Hz, how it moves, classifier needs 9 pixels at 48 m. Exactly where: tracker 0.02 degrees with a cue valid for 4 seconds, two cameras 20 m apart, friendly drone. Colours mark whether a number is measured, modelled on the recordings, calculated, or described.">

The four questions, the sensors that answer each one and the number found for it, coloured by where the number comes from. The sensors that were built answer the questions at short range; the first alert at kilometres has to come from a radar or a radio receiver.

</Figure>

1. **Is something there?** Sensors that watch everything at once raise an alert: a radio
   receiver, a radar, wide cameras, microphones. A weak signal is enough, as long as false
   alerts stay rare.
2. **Roughly where?** The alert comes with a direction, and from a radar with a distance.
   That gives a sector or a box that can be hundreds of metres across.
3. **What is it?** A sensor with a narrow view is pointed at the box and has enough pixels
   to tell a drone from a bird. The rotors help: they leave flashes in a radar echo and
   tones in a microphone.
4. **Exactly where?** All reports go into one tracker that keeps a position, a velocity
   and an uncertainty. A precise position needs a radar range, two sites that see the
   target from different sides, or a sensor that flies closer.

The questions feed each other. A zoom camera that can see a small drone at 2 km only sees
a window of about 8 degrees, so the rough position of the second question is what makes
the third one possible, and what the camera then sees improves the position of the fourth.

### What the numbers are

Only part of the chain could be built, so the chapters keep three kinds of numbers apart.

| Part | Number | Where it comes from |
|---|---|---|
| False alarms, the aircraft and bird tracks | no noise track of 2 s or more in 10 minutes | measured, two cameras on a Jetson Orin Nano |
| Detection against size and range | 127 to 274 m at 1080p, 295 m in 4K | real sky; the drones are drawn into the recordings by a model |
| Classifier | needs about 9 pixels, which the camera has at 48 m | trained on a public dataset and on drawn-in drones; it has not seen a real drone on this camera |
| Tracker | 0.02 degrees; a cue stays valid for about 4 s | five real tracks and one drawn-in drone |
| Rotor tones of the Crazyflie | 533 Hz, with multiples up to 6 kHz | measured with two phones |
| Hearing range | 26 to 88 m | modelled; the phones could not measure loudness |
| Radar, radio receiver, thermal camera | kilometres; 1 to 2 km for a video link; 0.4 km in fog to a few kilometres | calculated from the physics or simulated; no hardware |
| Combining the sensors, the flight from 3 km | declared at 148 m by camera and microphone alone | a model on the measurements; the two long-range sensors are assumed curves |
| Pointed cameras, the friendly drone | range to 1 m at 150 m with two cameras 20 m apart | calculated and described; not built |

### Why this order

1. **How to detect and locate a drone**: what a drone gives away, how far each sensor
   reaches, and the four questions.
2. **Seeing a small drone**: the camera, the sensor that could be built and tested best. A
   detector for targets of a few pixels, its false alarms on a real sky, and its range.
3. **Hearing a small drone**: the second sensor that could be built. The rotor tones of a
   Crazyflie on two phones, and a model of how far they carry.
4. **Radar, radio and heat**: the sensors that were not available, from their physics, so
   that the chain is complete from kilometres down to metres.
5. **From an alert to a position**: all sensors together. How weak reports add up when
   every sensor fails in some weather, and a tracker whose uncertainty is tested on real
   tracks.
6. **Closing in**: what the ground track has to hand over to a sensor that goes closer,
   and what comes next.

## What was built

Two Arducam IMX519 cameras on a Jetson Orin Nano record the sky, and two phones record a
Crazyflie 2.1 Brushless as microphones. The detector, a small classifier and a Kalman
filter run on the recordings afterwards, at about 7 frames per second on one desktop core;
it is not a live detector yet.

## What is open

The largest doubt is the target. Every range of the camera depends on drones that were
drawn into the recordings, and the classifier has never seen a real drone on this camera,
so the first step is a flight with a real drone at measured distances. The second is a
calibrated microphone outdoors, so that the hearing range no longer rests on an assumed
margin. The sky in the recordings is one cloudless afternoon; clouds, dusk and rain will
reduce the range.

The last link of the chain is described and calculated, not built: a friendly drone that
flies to the rough position, confirms what the object is and measures where it is from
close by. The project ends there, at knowing what the object is and where it is.
Interception is the step that follows, and it is deliberately left out.

## How it was made

Everything is Python. The sensor models and the tracker live in one reusable package, the
recordings are frozen and never edited by hand, and the figures are regenerated from the
same code. The framing is defensive throughout: sensing and estimation, with nothing that
captures, damages or interferes with an aircraft.
